Resumen

La inferencia causal, el proceso de estimar el efecto de una variable sobre otra bajo diferentes
intervenciones, ha sido un desafı́o fundamental en las últimas décadas. En biomedicina, com-
prender qué intervenciones causan realmente mejoras en la salud es una cuestión crucial que
afecta a millones de pacientes. Sin embargo, identificar relaciones causales fiables a partir de
datos complejos y mayoritariamente observacionales sigue siendo un reto central. Es necesario
integrar el conocimiento experto, el modelado probabilı́stico y los métodos computacionales.
Un paso crucial que a menudo falta para lograr la inferencia causal efectiva es el descubrimiento
causal, que consiste en la construcción de grafos causales: grafos acı́clicos dirigidos que repre-
sentan relaciones de causa y efecto. Sin grafos causales bien definidos, los investigadores suelen
depender de diagramas predefinidos o carecen de estructuras causales explı́citas.

Los enfoques existentes para el descubrimiento causal varı́an desde métodos puramente es-
tadı́sticos hasta construcciones manuales dirigidas por expertos, que a menudo son inviables. Los
Modelos de Lenguaje de Gran Tamaño (LLM, por sus sigles en inglés) ofrecen una oportunidad
prometedora para automatizar y escalar el descubrimiento causal al aprovechar su conocimiento
de dominio nativo e incorporado. A pesar de sus demostradas capacidades de razonamiento y
generalización, su potencial para producir grafos causales que permitan la inferencia causal sigue
estando poco explorado. El objetivo de esta tesis es construir grafos causales probabilı́sticos
con LLMs y evaluar su potencial para la inferencia causal con aplicación biomédica.

Este trabajo propone metodologı́as para la construcción de grafos causales basados en LLMs,
comenzando con un enfoque básico que se apoya en el conocimiento general de un LLM funda-
cional ya entrenado. A continuación, avanzaremos hacia un marco de Generación Aumentada
por Recuperación (RAG, por sus siglas en inglés) que incorpora contexto especı́fico del dominio
procedente de bases de conocimiento externas para mitigar limitaciones conocidas, como las
alucinaciones y la información desactualizada. Con el fin de modelar con mayor precisión los
escenarios del mundo real, el marco introduce incertidumbre mediante la generación de aristas
probabilı́sticas y la cuantificación numérica de los niveles de confianza y alucinación.

Para realizar la inferencia causal, integraremos los grafos estimados por el LLM con modelos gen-
erativos causales. Estos modelos van más allá del modelado de distribuciones observacionales y
permiten modelar intervenciones (estimar distribuciones poblacionales bajo cambios hipotéticos
en una variable) y realizar estimaciones contrafactuales (predecir resultados individuales bajo
intervenciones alternativas).

Tras la implementación, se valida todo el flujo de trabajo. La construcción del grafo causal
se evalúa frente a verdades de referencia establecidas, incluyendo un benchmark general de
causa y efecto, mientras que la utilidad práctica de los grafos generados por LLM es validada
mediante su aplicación a la inferencia causal en datos biomédicos del mundo real con evaluaciones
cuantitativas.

Al combinar el descubrimiento de grafos impulsado por LLMs con el modelado generativo causal,
este trabajo desarrolla un flujo de trabajo completo de extremo a extremo para la inferencia
causal automatizada en dominios biomédicos.
Summary

Causal inference, the process of estimating the effect of one variable on another under different
interventions, has been a fundamental challenge in recent decades. In biomedicine, determining
which interventions actually improve health is crucial for millions of patients. However, iden-
tifying reliable causal relationships from complex, mostly observational data remains challeng-
ing. It requires the integration of expert knowledge, probabilistic modeling, and computational
methods. A key step often missing from effective causal inference is causal discovery, which
involves constructing causal graphs — directed acyclic graphs representing cause-effect rela-
tionships. Without well-defined causal graphs, researchers often rely on predefined diagrams or
lack explicit causal structures.

Existing approaches for causal discovery range from purely statistical methods to expert-driven
manual constructions, which are frequently infeasible. Large Language Models (LLMs) offer a
promising opportunity to automate and scale causal discovery by leveraging their embedded and
native domain knowledge. Despite their demonstrated reasoning and generalization capabilities,
their potential for producing causal graphs that enable causal inference remains under-explored.
The aim of this thesis is to build probabilistic causal graphs using LLMs and to evaluate their
potential for causal inference in biomedical applications.

This work proposes methodologies for LLM-based causal graph construction, beginning with
a baseline approach that relies on the general, pre-trained knowledge of a foundational LLM.
Next, we will advance to a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) framework that incorporates
domain-specific context from external knowledge bases to mitigate known limitations, such
as hallucinations and outdated information. To more closely model real-world scenarios, the
framework introduces uncertainty by generating probabilistic edges and quantifying confidence
and hallucination levels numerically.

To perform causal inference, we will integrate the LLM-estimated graphs with causal generative
models. These models go beyond modeling observational distributions, enabling intervention
modeling (estimating population distributions under hypothetical changes to a variable) and
counterfactual estimation (predicting individual outcomes under alternative interventions).

After implementation, the entire pipeline is validated. Causal graph construction is validated
against established ground truths, including a general cause-effect benchmark, while the practi-
cal utility of the LLM-generated graphs is assessed through their application to causal inference
on real-world biomedical data with quantitative evaluations

By combining LLM-driven graph discovery with causal generative modeling, this work develops
a complete end-to-end framework for automated causal inference in biomedical domains.

                         1. Introduction and Objectives


1.1     Motivation

The concept of causality historically remained a philosophical enigma, difficult to formalize
and considered beyond the reach of mathematical treatment [1]. In the scientific tradition, we
usually relied on association; yet our human nature kept looking for the “why” behind any
phenomenon. In the last decades, however, this view has changed. Causality has evolved from
a purely philosophical construct to a well-defined mathematical object with logical foundations.
Paradoxes and controversies have been resolved, ideas have been explained, and practical prob-
lems relying on causal information can now be solved using elementary mathematics; causality
has been mathematized [1].

The mathematization of causality is the starting point. Determining whether an association is
causal is a fundamental challenge with profound implications across many domains [2]. Causal
inference addresses this challenge by uncovering the causal relationships behind observations,
thereby enabling a deep understanding of the underlying mechanisms and allowing estimation
of how a treatment affects outcomes of interest relative to an alternative treatment [3].

This is particularly relevant in medicine, where the goal of physicians is, in many cases, to
intervene, i.e., to administer a treatment that produces an effect on a patient’s disease. Decisions
regarding the harms or benefits of therapeutic agents rely, in part, on rules for assessing the
strength of evidence of causal links between interventions and health outcomes [2]. Hence,
causal inference is embedded in many aspects of medical practice.

The adoption of large language models (LLMs) has opened promising opportunities for tradi-
tional causal inference tasks [3], such as causal discovery, although these applications remain
underexplored [4]. This research proposes the integration of LLMs to facilitate causal inference
in medicine.

Two questions that drive the dual motivation of this work must be addressed: first, the need
for causal inference in clinical contexts; and second, the use of LLMs to automate the discovery
of causal graphs.


1.1.1    Why Causal Inference in the Medical Domain?

Causal inference determines how changes in one variable affect others. This capability is es-
sential for evaluating the impact of interventions (manipulating a specific variable to observe
the changes in outcome variables) and guiding critical decisions. This impacts disciplines such
as social sciences, public health, as well as in the medical domain [5, 6]. Its integration in the
latter enhances the precision of preventive protocols and the reliability of diagnostic outcomes.
By identifying how specific treatments drive disease progression, healthcare institutions can
develop more effective clinical strategies [5].

A primary driver for adopting causal reasoning in healthcare is the limitation of correlation-
based analysis. While traditional machine learning methods effectively identify general trends
and correlations, where two variables increase or decrease, they fail to establish if one variable
is actually responsible for the changes observed in another [7]. Correlation means that there is
a relationship between two variables, whereas causal reasoning is about determining whether
                                                                                                 3


one variable is partially responsible for the effect of another variable, and whether the effect
is partially dependent on the cause [8]. Highlighting these edges is vital in clinical settings
to prevent erroneous medical decisions that might otherwise arise from treating a statistical
association as a causal connection [5].

The principle that correlation is not causation [7] becomes particularly important when con-
sidering the nature of medical data. In an ideal scenario, causal effects could be identified
through randomized controlled experiments, where the investigator has full control over the
treatment assignment mechanism (how treatment is assigned). Randomized experiments en-
sure that treatment is not a function of covariates (unrelated to all other patient factors). As a
result, in randomized experiments, association is causation [8]. This relationship can be illus-
trated using causal diagrams, as in Figure 1, where T represents the treatment, Y represents the
outcome, and X represents covariates (all other factors). The absence of an arrow between X
and T indicates that X does not affect T . This complete control over how treatment is chosen
is what distinguishes randomized experiments from observational studies [8].

                                         T             Y

However, such experimental conditions are rarely feasible in real-world medical practice. Ethical
constraints, financial costs, limited sample sizes, and practical considerations often restrict the
use of randomized trials [9]. From an ethical perspective, how can a physician uphold their duty
of care when allocating treatments at random [10]? Physicians are expected to act in the best
interests of each individual patient, therefore they cannot intentionally administer a treatment
that they believe to be inferior or less effective, even for the purpose of experimentation [10],
making true randomization difficult. This does not imply that RCTs are no longer performed; on
the contrary, they remain a crucial and indispensable phase in the development and evaluation of
new medical treatments. Nevertheless, causal effects often need to be inferred from observational
data [9], where the experimenter does not have control over the treatment assignment and may
depend on characteristics of the patient [8].

1.1.2   Why LLMs for Causal Discovery?

Causal discovery (CD) involves uncovering cause-and-effect relationships [15], specifically iden-
tifying the presence and direction of these links rather than quantifying the strength or shape
of the effects. Such understanding is fundamental to scientific discovery and informed decision-
making in a variety of fields [15]. CD is often considered as the “Rung 0” of causal inference,
as it serves as a fundamental component [3]. Understanding causal mechanisms is crucial for
effective causal inference tasks such as interventions, from discovering the causes of diseases and
developing effective treatments to optimizing complex systems, like city traffic flow or global
supply chains [15].

On the other hand, LLMs have achieved remarkable success in in natural language process-
ing (NLP) including translation, question-answering (QA) and cloze tasks [16], as well as in
other areas such as vision-language models, personalized learning, biomedicine, and code gen-
eration [17], emerging as a significant area of Artificial Intelligence (AI) research due to their
performance in understanding and generating human-like content [18].

The causal capabilities of LLMs are a matter of significant debate, with implications for their
use in impactful domains such as medicine, science, law, and policy [18]. While LLMs are not
explicitly designed to replace formal causal inference methods, their ability to reason about
variable dependencies, suggest plausible causal hypotheses, and incorporate large-scale prior
knowledge makes them promising tools for assisting causal discovery from observational data
[15].

Although CD and LLMs have evolved largely independently, with CD specializing in uncovering
causal relationships from data and LLMs excelling at natural language processing and gener-
ation, their integration presents unique opportunities for advancing causal understanding [15].
The use case for LLMs has evolved from initially predictive models focused on next-token infer-
ence toward the development of agentic systems capable of solving complex cognitive problems.
This includes the capacity for causal reasoning, facilitating the implementation of specialized
systems such as Causal Agents [19] among others. This apparent capacity for both implicit
and explicit consideration of causal factors has created excitement around understanding their
reasoning capabilities [18].

Furthermore, our motivation is also fostered by the scarcity and expense of real-world data for
causal discovery. CD algorithms are usually evaluated primarily on simulated data, which may
not accurately reflect real-world performance. This is because causal ground truth graphs are
incredibly rare, as they often require real-world experiments and experts [20].

Despite promising performance on simulated data and in spite of several results on the identi-
fiability of causal directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) or parts of them from passive observations,
there is still skepticism about the applicability of these algorithms to real data. This is because
traditional causal discovery algorithms are based on assumptions such as faithfulness, additive
noise, post-nonlinear models, or independence principles, all of which can be violated in practice
[20]. Indeed, recent studies on causal discovery methods [21] reveal a disconcerting reality about
their applicability to real-world datasets: there is always a gap between theory and practice.

1.2   Objectives

The primary objectives of this thesis are as follows:


  1. Development of a generalizable LLM-based causal discovery framework: To
     develop and integrate LLMs into a causal discovery pipeline to leverage their in-context
     learning and domain-specific knowledge. A core design priority is its generalization and
     its extensibility to corporate and industrial environments, ensuring the pipeline can be
     extensive to other contexts and sectors by transforming raw datasets and metadata into
     structured DAGs.

  2. Implementation of an end-to-end causal inference pipeline: To design and imple-
     ment a pipeline that leverages the discovered structures to perform causal effect estima-

     logic to be applied to diverse research environments beyond the initial test cases.

  3. Real-world biomedical application: Validate the clinical utility of the end-to-end
     pipeline by applying it to a real-world biomedical dataset. This application establish a
     foundation for its adoption in industrial environments .

  4. Hybrid evaluation and benchmarking: Firstly, the causal discovery framework is
     assessed by measuring the inferred causal relationships using standard metrics, across
     several models and techniques. Secondly, the utility of the inferred causal graphs in the
     causal inference stage is evaluated by analyzing the estimated causal effects. By comparing
     these two stages with ground-truth data, this hybrid strategy allows us to benchmark both
     the structure of the discovered causal graph and the reliability of the resulting causal effect
     estimations.


It is important to emphasize that the system developed in this work is not intended to be
trusted blindly as a definitive source of causal conclusions. When considering its real-world
application, such systems should be viewed as tools that could accelerate medical research and
experimental phases, support the opinions of medical experts, or provide additional evidence
within a highly complex validation process. Ultimately, the framework developed is not intended
to be a tool that can be relied upon with absolute certainty, but rather a component part of a
larger verification process.

             2. Theoretical and Mathematical Foundations

2.1     Causal Inference Theoretical Foundations

A practical approach for accurate causal inference has been proposed. The aim of this section
is to provide an overview of the essential theoretical and mathematical foundations.


2.1.1    Core Concepts for Causality

This section provides the mathematical definitions of the graph-theoretic and causal concepts
introduced in the previous subsections that we will revisit throughout this work:

Acyclic Property:: A graph G is defined as acyclic if it contains no directed cycles [8]; that
is, there is no sequence of directed edges that starts and ends at the same node.

Partially Directed Acyclic Graph (PDAG) and Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG): A
graph G is called a partially directed acyclic graph (PDAG) if there is no directed cycle; that
is, there is no pair of nodes (j, k) with directed paths from j to k and from k to j. G is called
a directed acyclic graph (DAG) if it is a PDAG and all edges are directed [8].

Causal Path: A path through the DAG is any unbroken route along or against arrows from
variable to variable. A causal or directed path is a sequence of arrows all in the same direction
[22].

Variables: The nodes in the causal graph. In our biomedical context, these include demo-
graphic factors (e.g. age, sex), clinical measurements (e.g. BMI, blood pressure) and outcomes
(e.g. diabetes, cardiovascular event) [22].

Mediators: A mediator is a variable that transmits the effect of a treatment to an outcome
(e.g. BM I → Hypertension → CHD). Understanding mediation is vital in healthcare to
elucidate the “mechanism of action” of a risk factor. If we intervene on BMI, we affect CHD
partly through reducing Hypertension (indirect effect) [22].

Confounders: Variables affecting both treatment and outcome. A confounder has three spe-
cific criteria: (1) must be an extraneous risk factor for the disease outcome that is independent
of the main treatment under study; (2) must be associated with the treatment in the source
population of the study participants either as a direct effect on the treatment or through the
relation of a common cause variable that precedes both the treatment and the confounder; and
(3) must not be affected by the treatment or the disease outcome [22].

Collider: A variable that is a common effect of two other variables (e.g., when treatment and
an outcome independently cause a third variable and their effects “collide”) [22].

Queries: Different types of causal questions that can be answered using causal models [23]:


   • Interventional Queries: Queries of the form Q(M ) = P (Y | do(t)). Represented by the
     do-operator, do(T = t), this operator signifies a physical change to the system, replacing
     the natural structural equation fT with a constant T = t. In the graph, this corresponds
     to “graph surgery”: deleting all incoming edges to T while keeping outgoing edges intact.
                                                                                                        9


        The primary causal estimands derived from these queries include the Average Treat-
        ment Effect (ATE), which quantifies the impact of such interventions on the entire
        population by taking an average over the ITEs (see next bullet point) [8]:

                             AT Eα,β = E[Y | do(T = α)] − E[Y | do(T = β)]

        Other common measures include the Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE), fo-
        cusing on a specific subgroup, and the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT)
        [3].

   • Counterfactual Queries: Queries of the form Q(M ) = P (Ycf | do(tcf ), xf ), where xf is
     the observed factual evidence. Intuitively, this query represents the distribution the out-
     come would have had, had we intervened on the treatment variable with a counterfactual
     value tcf instead of the factual value observed in the data. These are potential outcomes
     that you we do not (and cannot) observe because they are counter to fact (reality) [8].
        A classic estimand is the Individual Treatment Effect (ITE), which aims to quantify
        the effect of a treatment on a specific individual by comparing their factual outcome with
        their counterfactual potential outcome [3].

2.1.3     Causal Identifiability Assumptions

Ideal randomized experiments are unrealistic but useful to introduce some key concepts for
causal inference. Three core assumptions allow causal effects to be estimated from observational
data.

For example, for the causal query P (y | do(t), x):


   • Exchangeability (No unmeasured confounders): This assumption implies that, once
     we account for the observed covariates X, the treatment T is effectively as if randomized.
     Formally, it states that the potential outcomes are independent of the treatment given
     the covariates:
                                           Y (t) ⊥ T | X                                    (9)
        It means that the groups of individuals receiving different levels of treatment are compa-
        rable, and any difference in their outcomes can be attributed to the treatment itself rather
        than to inherent differences in the groups’ background characteristics. In the presence of
        unobserved confounders, this assumption is violated, as the treatment and the outcome
        would share a common cause not accounted for in the model, making the groups non-
        exchangeable. When the treated and the untreated are exchangeable, it is sometimes said
        [27] that the treatment is exogenous.

   • Positivity: This condition guarantees that every individual in the population has a non-
     zero probability of receiving any level of the treatment T , regardless of their observed
     covariates X. Mathematically, for all t and x:

                                          P (T = t | X = x) > 0                                (10)

        This is essential for causal inference because if a certain subgroup of the population never
        receives a specific treatment, the data contains no information to estimate what their
        outcome would have been under that intervention, making causal comparisons impossible.

   • Consistency: This assumption ensures that the values of treatment T under comparison
     correspond to well-defined interventions. It states that the observed outcome Y is identical
     to the potential outcome under the treatment actually received:

                                            Y = Y (t)   if T = t                               (11)
                                                                                                  13


        For instance, if the treatment T is defined as receiving a medication, consistency would
        be violated if different patients received different dosages (e.g., 5 mg vs. 10 mg) that have
        distinct effects on the outcome Y , yet these versions are not explicitly captured by the
        variable T [27]. For this to be the case, the treatment T must be well-defined, and there
        must be no hidden versions of the treatment that could lead to different results.


These three assumptions provide the foundation for using observational data to emulate ran-
domized experiments and make valid causal inferences [27].


2.1.4     Interventions and Causal Effects

Under the assumption that these identifiability conditions (exchangeability, positivity, and con-
sistency) hold, it is possible to move beyond associations to formalize causal queries and, sub-
sequently, estimate their resulting effects.

In the regular notation for probability, we have conditioning, but that isn’t the same as inter-
vening. Conditioning on T = t just means that we are restricting our focus to the subset of the
population to those who received treatment t. In contrast, an intervention would be to take the
whole population and give everyone treatment t [8].

Therefore, we can define an intervention as the action of modifying a system variable, usually
the variable for which the causal effect is to be estimated. Some authors refer to interventions
as “manipulations” [8]. Crucially, intervening implies that a variable acts independently of its
natural causes; that is, it involves overriding the structural equation of a variable to force it to
a specific value. Theoretically, if we were able to physically intervene in a system, we sever the
links from the variable’s parents as illustrated in Figure 7:




Figure 7: Graph manipulation representation. In the left figure, the effect of T is confounded
by variable X. By intervention, the edge from X to T is severed, then X no longer affects T
[8].

The concept of intervention is mathematically formalized by the do-operator [28], denoted as
do(t). Unlike standard conditional probability, which merely observes data, the do-operator
simulates the physical action of forcing the treatment variable T to a specific value t, indepen-
dently of its natural causes. do(T = α) implies an intervention, whereas observing T = α is
strictly correlational.

In the context of the generative model, once trained, the causal flow allows us to simulate and
quantify the causal queries previously defined. Specifically, it allows for the numerical compu-
tation of the ATE by simulating the interventional distributions, under distinct interventional
values (α and β). A key theoretical advantage of this approach is that the causal flow can re-
                                                                                               14


cover the underlying structural mechanisms solely by maximizing the likelihood of the observed
data, provided the causal structure is correctly specified [24, 23].


2.1.5   Causal Inference in Healthcare: Perspectives and Limitations




Figure 8: Causal Inference in medical problems [5]. (top-left) the impact of confounding fac-
tors; (top-right) Pearl’s Ladder of Causation; and (bottom) the fundamental structural frame-

Figure 8 illustrates key challenges in causal inference in the medical domain. It demonstrates the
impact of confounding factors through Simpson’s Paradox [14], presents the three-level hierarchy
of causal reasoning known as Pearl’s Ladder of Causation [7] and depicts the fundamental
structural framework used for causal effect estimation in clinical settings.

Having previously established the motivation; not only why a physician can employ causal in-
ference for certain tasks, but also why such a need exists, we can now categorize causal inference
queries into a three-layered structure, often referred to as Pearl’s “Ladder of Causation” [7].

In the medical domain, the causal queries are seen as follows [5]:

1. Association: This level represents the lowest rung, focused on seeing and identifying
associations or correlations between variables by observing data. It answers questions such as
“What if I see...?”, with a typical medical example being: “Do patients with high blood pressure
experience heart attacks more frequently?”.
                                                                                                15


2. Intervention: This level moves beyond observation by manipulating a variable to see how
it alters an outcome. The most common clinical application is the Randomized Controlled
Trial (RCT), where patients are assigned specific treatments to measure their direct impact on
recovery. Answering questions like, “Will increasing the patient’s medication dosage speed up
their recovery?”

3. Counterfactuals: Considered the foundation of causal reasoning, counterfactuals address
“what-if” scenarios. They allow clinicians to estimate how an individual patient’s outcome
would have differed if a different course of action had been taken (e.g., “What would the patient’s
health have been like if they received Drug X instead of Drug Y? Or without treatment?”).

Regarding the causal inference framework in the medical domain illustrated in Figure 8, within
a standard medical causal model [5], the patient is defined as the Unit, the specific drug or
treatment is the Intervention (denoted as T ), and the clinical result corresponds to the outcome
(denoted as Y ). Causal inference involves estimating how the outcome changes when a certain
intervention is implemented. That is, when given T and Y , a parameter θ characterizing the
causality between T and Y must be found, along with a method to estimate θ, (θ typically
representing the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) or the Conditional Average Treatment Effect
(CATE)).

A fundamental challenge in real world studies is the impossibility to observe both potential
outcomes (under treatment and under no treatment) for the same individual simultaneously
[27]. Instead, only the observed outcome is available. Hernán and Robins (2020)[27] defines an
ideal randomized control trial (RCT) as an experiment where the investigator assigns treatment
to participants according to a random mechanism, ensuring these values are missing purely by
chance. As a result, effect measures can be consistently estimated in randomized experiments
despite the missing data [27].

However, in many real-world medical settings, randomization is not feasible, and causal inference
must rely on observational data [8]. In observational studies, the estimation of causal effects is
complicated by the presence of confounders (defined in Section 2.1.1), such as a patient’s age,
pre-existing conditions, or genetic markers, potentially creating misleading relationships [5].
These variables are critical because they simultaneously influence both the treatment choice
(T ) and the likelihood of recovery (Y ). In such cases, the assumption of exchangeability (iden-
tifiability assumption 1 in Section 2.1.3) may be violated.

While randomization is effective in RCTs, it is often impractical in real-world studies. To
address confounding in observational settings, causal inference methods constructs comparable
groups ensuring that any observed change in the outcome can be confidently attributed to the
intervention itself [5]. Alternative statistical and structural strategies are employed, such as
stratification [29], where the study population is divided into homogeneous subgroups based on
specific confounders. Other strategies include matching, in which participants in the treatment
and control groups are paired based on shared confounding characteristics to neutralize the
influence of those specific variables [29] and outcome regression, an innovative way which builds
a statistical model that predicts the outcome variable while accounting for the influence of
confounding variables.

To illustrate the concept of a confounding variable more intuitively, consider the classical ex-
ample depicted in Figure 9. Suppose a researcher examines the relationship between coffee
consumption and the incidence of heart disease. An initial observation may suggest that individ-
uals who consume coffee exhibit a higher prevalence of heart disease compared to non-drinkers.
However, this apparent association may be misleading due to the presence of a confounding
                                                                                                16


factor, such as smoking. Coffee consumers are also more likely to smoke than non-consumers.
As smoking is an established risk factor for heart disease, it confounds the observed relationship,
making it unclear whether the increased risk is attributable to coffee consumption itself or to
smoking behavior [29].




                Figure 9: Example of confounding variable in healthcare [29].


In this scenario, the presence of smoking can trigger a cascade of variables that where not
initially associated. Smokers are more likely to engage in unhealthy lifestyle behaviors, such
as poor diet, alcohol consumption, and sedentary lifestyle. These factors may confound the
observed association with heart diseases [29]. Extending this idea, if alcohol consumption is
also considered, it introduces a new set of factors. For instance, alcohol is linked with higher
depression links, demonstrating how confounding relationships can branch out like a tree. One
important aspect to note is the frequent exclusion of psycological and social factors in causal
inference within medicine, but generally in RCT. While these factors are generally difficult to
quantify, we believe they may act as strong confounders in real-world randomized controlled
trials, and their influence is often substantial.

In such settings, identifying and appropriately adjusting for confounding variables is essential
to ensure valid causal interpretation. Careful consideration of potential confounders strength-
ens the reliability of clinical findings and supports the development of effective public health
interventions and preventive strategies [29].


2.2     Large Language Models Foundations

2.2.1    Large Language Models

The field began with the well-known Transformer architecture. This architecture was introduced
in the landmark paper “Attention Is All You Need” [30], which literally transformed the world
of machine learning. Transformers have especially revolutionized NLP through self-supervised
learning; these models are trained on vast amounts of raw text data using a self-supervised
approach. This means they learn language patterns directly from the text without the need for
explicit labels.

We are able to understand language through statistics. Transformers develop a deep statistical
understanding of the language they’ve been trained on, enabling them to generate text, answer
questions, and perform a wide range of other NLP tasks. The impact of the Transformer extends
far beyond NLP. It serves as the backbone of Generative AI (GenAI), artificial intelligence
that can create original content in response to human prompts [31], across multiple domains,
including computer vision (Vision Transformers [32]) and audio processing (wav2vec 2.0 [33]).
                                                                                                17


Earlier architectures, such as recurrent neural networks (RNN), are effective at processing se-
quences, but struggle to learn long-term dependencies due to their inherent sequential nature
[34]. In contrast, Transformers are solely based on the attention mechanism [30], which relates
any two positions of the input sequence and therefore models arbitrarily long dependencies.

Transformer models can be grouped into three categories based on their architecture and the
training objectives used to train them, which ultimately determines which tasks they are most
suitable for:

Encoder-only: Encoder-only models are also known as autoencoding models. Tokens in the
input sentence are randomly masked and the training objective is to predict the masked token in
order to reconstruct the original sentence. They are trained using Masked Language Modeling
(MLM). The idea is to randomly select certain tokens from the input sequence and mask them.
The model, during training, tries to predict the masked token based on the surrounding tokens.
Suitable for sentence classification tasks (e.g., sentiment analysis) and token-level tasks such as
named entity recognition or word classification. Examples: BERT [35], RoBERTa [36].

Encoder-decoder: Encoder-decoder models are also known as sequence-to-sequence models,
and their training method actually varies; an example would be span corruption. Suitable for
translation, text summarization, and question answering. Examples: T5 [37], BART [38].

Decoder-only: Decoder-only models are also known as autoregressive models and are trained
using Causal Language Modeling. CLM is the task of predicting the token following a sequence
of tokens. The model only attends to the left context; such training is particularly interesting
for generation tasks. That’s why they are also called autoregressive, because the current value
(token to predict) is a function of its past values. Suitable for text generation, large decoder-
only models show strong zero-shot inference abilities and can often perform a range of tasks
well. Here we see the prominent example: GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer)[16] series,
which serves as the foundation for ChatGPT[39], which is the primary example of a LLM.

LLMs have been available for a couple of years already, even though the significant hype began
at the end of 2022 [40, 39]. Their impact is built upon several characteristics that distinguish
them:


   • Deep learning algorithms to process, understand and generate natural language.

   • Training on massive amounts of text data to learn patterns and entity relationships in the
     language [17].

   • A general-purpose, task-agnostic nature: Unlike traditional NLP models that required
     task-specific architectures and fine-tuning on large supervised datasets [16], LLMs can
     perform a wide range of tasks, such as translation, sentiment analysis, QA and summa-
     rization [17], without specialized training for each.

   • In-context learning capabilities: These models can adapt to new tasks through zero-shot
     learning (where it performs the task without any prior examples) or few-shot learning
     (where the model executes a task by observing only a few examples within the instruc-
     tion), using natural language prompts to generate coherent, contextually accurate, and
     grammatically correct text across diverse domains [16].


Moreover, LLMs continue to evolve: a key direction is the integration of multiple modalities.
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) [41] extend LLM capabilities by combining text
                                                                                                 18


and visual information, supporting dialogue and instruction-following. These models have been
applied to tasks such as visual grounding, image generation and editing, and domain-specific
visual understanding, highlighting the broader potential of foundation models beyond purely
textual data [41].

LLMs are ready to be used for inference in order to obtain completions and have achieved
remarkable success, though they still face significant limitations, especially in domain-specific or
knowledge-intensive tasks [42] or when handling queries beyond their training data or requiring
current information [43]. LLMs occasionally produce outputs that deviate from the user’s input,
previously generated context or factual knowledge. This phenomenon is commonly referred to
as hallucination [44].

The notion of hallucination has expanded with the increasing versatility of LLMs. Hallucinations
are often categorized into the following types [44], as illustrated in Figure 10. Input-conflicting
hallucinations occur when an LLM generates content that diverges from the source input pro-
vided by the user. Context-conflicting hallucinations arise when the model produces outputs
that contradict information it has previously generated. Finally, fact-conflicting hallucinations
refer to cases in which the generated content is inconsistent with established world knowledge
or cannot be verified against it.




          Figure 10: Three types of hallucinations occurred in LLM responses [44].


There are also current efforts to mitigate hallucinations at different stages [44]. At the data
level, careful curation of pre-training and fine-tuning datasets, such as filtering out unreliable
sources, can help reduce the amount of incorrect knowledge the model internalizes. During
model training and alignment, techniques like supervised fine-tuning are used to align model
outputs with human judgments of correctness. This process consists of training a pre-trained
language model on curated input–output pairs so that it learns to produce responses that better
                                                                                               19


match desired and task-specific behaviors. At inference, methods such as retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG) [43] or incorporating external knowledge sources are used to ground model
responses in factual information rather than relying solely on the model’s internal parameters.
An alternative strategy is model editing [44], which modifies the model’s parameters or behavior
to correct known factual errors.

In addition to hallucinations, LLMs exhibit non-deterministic behavior; the same input can
produce different results across multiple executions because of their probabilistic nature, which
poses significant reliability issues in production environments [45].

To overcome these limitations, in this work we explore one of the techniques mentioned above:
RAG [46]. Primarily, it addresses the issue of knowledge cut-offs by allowing the model to access
up-to-date information from external databases, ensuring that the output is not restricted by
the date the model was last trained. Furthermore, RAG provides a cost-effective alternative
to the high computational and financial expenses associated with frequent model re-training or
fine-tuning. This architecture is particularly advantageous for the secure handling of proprietary
data, as it enables the model to process sensitive knowledge during inference without the need
to incorporate that data into the underlying model weights. Consequently, RAG serves as a
critical framework for enhancing factual accuracy and LLM performance in domain-specific and
knowledge-intensive contexts [43].


2.2.2   Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

The key concept of RAG can be explained simply: RAG systems enhance LLMs by integrating
external knowledge sources, enabling grounded and contextually relevant responses tailored to
user needs [43]. RAG is the combination of information retrieval with text generation.

RAG has been widely applied in various practical scenarios in several domains like biomedical
or legal, recommendation systems, customer service, and personal assistants. RAG can be as
personalized as desired, drawing from all the personal data shared with it (including previous
interactions and user preferences). This capability enables an assistant to deliver responses that
feel both relevant and individualized, creating a more engaging and user-focused experience.

RAG Architecture

As illustrated in Figure 11, the RAG pipeline architecture entails two temporal phases: pre-
query time (the indexing stage) and query time (the retrieval and generation stages) [43].
                                                                                              20




Figure 11: RAG pipeline architecture. The process at pre-query time (on the left) focuses on
the data ingestion, chunking, and embedding. At query time (on the right right), a user query
triggers search, retrieval and generation by the LLM.


The process can be broken down into more granular steps [43]:


  1. Data Ingestion: Retrieving text from different data sources that you want to search on.

  2. Chunking and Representation: If using semantic search, the data ingested in the
     previous step is split into chunks and converted into vector embeddings to allow for
     similarity-based comparison. However, if a simpler keyword-based approach is used, ex-
     plicit chunking and embedding may be bypassed in favor of direct indexing of documents.

  3. Storage: Store the chunks and embeddings in an appropiate retrieval system, such as
     a vector database or a keyword-based index. This step involves indexing; organizing the
     stored data into data structures that enable efficient search.

  4. Querying: The retrieval system processes the user query and compares it against the
     indexed data, either through lexical matching or similarity-based comparison.

  5. Retrieval: Retrieve the Top-k chunks most relevant to the question using an information
     retrieval mechanism. This mechanism may rely on different approaches, such as keyword-
     based search or semantic similarity methods, depending on the system design. In semantic
     retrieval settings, vector representations and similarity measures (e.g., cosine similarity)
     are commonly used to compare queries and documents.

  6. Reranking (Advanced Optional Step): Retrieve an initial set of chunks and reorder
     (rerank) them so that the most relevant results appear first. It acts as both an enhancer
     and a filter, delivering refined inputs for more precise language model processing [47], as
     the performance of LLMs degrades with increasing the amount of provided context, often
     refer as context stuffing [48].

  7. Generation: The user query and selected documents are synthesized into a coherent
     prompt to which an LLM is tasked with formulating a response.

From Naive to Advanced RAG

The evolving demands of various applications have fostered the development of RAG, resulting
in the integration of advanced retrieval techniques, LLMs, and complementary technologies.
This evolution has led to increasingly complex RAG systems. In the realm of Gen AI, there
are countless opportunities for enhancement and exploration. While many focus on improving
the LLMs themselves, significant improvements can also be made in the retrieval process and
system prompts. Different techniques, such as hybrid retrieval, context compression, and query
rewriting, can provide valuable enhancements. The choice of LLM is just one aspect among a
broader system. This leads to Advanced RAG [46].

Naive RAG Drawbacks

What we have referred to as RAG is essentially the foundational level, often called Naive RAG.
However, this basic version has several limitations [46]:

   • Retrieval Challenges: The retrieval process may struggle to identify precise and rele-
     vant information, leading to the inclusion of irrelevant content or missing crucial details.
   • Generation Difficulties: The generation process can produce irrelevant, toxic, or biased
     content, compromising the quality of the output. This is what we know as hallucinating,
     one of the biggest challenges of Generative AI that can affect trust.
   • Augmentation Hurdles: Integrating retrieved information effectively into the genera-
     tion process can be challenging, resulting in disjointed or incoherent responses.

Advanced RAG

To mitigate these limitations, Advanced RAG introduces several key improvements that can be
added to the original Naive RAG pipeline in various stages [45]:

Pre-retrieval Process: Advanced techniques before the Retriever acts.

   • Indexing: Enhances the quality of indexed content for more effective retrieval. Several
     options exist (Vector Index, Full-text index/keyword index).
   • Query Rewriting: Improves query clarity and specificity. It involves reformulating or
     refining the original query to better match the intent of the user and the content of the
     knowledge base, as users are normally not subject matter experts or Prompt Engineers.
     Common query rewriting techniques include keyword expansion, query reformulation,
     query decomposition (sub-queries), and query contextualization [43].

Post-Retrieval Process: Once relevant context is retrieved, it is crucial to integrate it effec-
tively with the query before passing it to the LLM:

   • Reranking: Prioritizes the most relevant information for optimal integration. LLMs,
     like humans, tend to focus on the beginning or end of long contexts and miss information
     in the middle; we want to avoid the “Lost in the Middle” phenomenon [49].
   • Context Compression: Condenses the retrieved context to prevent information over-
     load. This concept has been implemented in frameworks such as LlamaIndex and LangChain.
     More information is not always better; feeding all relevant source context directly into
     LLMs can lead to information overload.

In the context of this work, RAG allows the causal discovery process to be evidence-based,
connecting the semantic reasoning of the LLM with real-world biomedical literature.

This chapter examines the state of the art in causal discovery and inference, tracing the evolution

3.1     LLMs for Causal Discovery

3.1.1    Traditional Causal Discovery

If SCM is employed as explained in the theoretical foundations in the previous chapter, the goal
of causal discovery is to recover these structural components from data, particularly identifying
the parent sets P a(Xi ) for each variable Xi , which defines the underlying causal graph structure.

This is an ill-posed problem because multiple causal structures can produce the exact same con-
ditional independence relations (Markov Equivalence). Common traditional statistical methods
include [15]:

Constraint-Based Algorithms: The most extended example is the PC Algorithm [50]. It
starts with a fully connected graph and iteratively removes edges between variables if they
are found to be conditionally independent (X ⊥⊥ Y |Z). A variation, the FCI (Fast Causal
Inference) algorithm [50], that is feasible in large variable sets provided the true graph is sparse
and there are not many bidirected edges chained together.The algorithm gives asymptotically
correct information about causal structure when latent variables may be acting and outputs a
Partial Ancestral Graph (PAG). These methods are computationally efficient for sparse graphs
but there are several weaknesses: they are highly sensitive to data noise and sample size and
the output is categorical: no indication of the strength of the conclusion. Another limitation is
that their output depends on the threshold used in independence tests. For causal conclusions
to be correct asymptotically, the threshold must be adjusted as a function of sample size (N).
In practice, however, it is difficult to find this function or impossible to test if the function is
correctly specified [50].

Score-Based Algorithms: Methods like GES (Greedy Equivalence Search) [50] treat discov-
ery as an optimization problem. They define a scoring function (e.g., BIC or Bayesian Dirichlet
score [15]) that measures how well a graph fits the data, penalized by complexity. They search
the space of possible graphs to maximize this score. While theoretically robust, the search space
                                                                2
grows super-exponentially with the number of variables (O(2N )), making them computation-
ally prohibitive for large networks. Moreover, most of them need strong assumptions on the
functional forms of causal mechanisms, as well as on data distributions, which limit their appli-
cability. In practice the precise information of the underlying model class is usually unknown.
If the above assumptions are violated, both spurious and missing edges may result [51].

One common limitation of these approaches is their inability to fully identify the true underlying
graph G beyond its Markov equivalence class [52, 53].

Domain Experts: Given the fragility of statistical methods, manual graph construction re-
mains the gold standard in healthcare. Experts use clinical knowledge to draw DAGs. However,
this approach is non-scalable, inconsistent as well because different experts may draw different
graphs, and can fail to capture complex or counter-intuitive pathways [15].
                                                                                                 24


3.1.2   LLMs for CD: Benefits and Challenges

The integration of LLMs into causal discovery attempts to solve the problems of traditional
causal discovery methods by leveraging these LLM’s native benefits, despite yet presenting
some challenges, described all in this section.

Benefits

Domain Knowledge: Traditional causal methods focus on numerical data, but domain knowl-
edge is crucial in fields like medicine for identifying causal relationships [3]. LLMs act as “Meta
Experts,” effectively extracting the consensus of millions of scientific papers, instead of individ-
ual subjective opinions. They can identify causal links that are statistically weak in a specific
dataset but biologically established [15](e.g., a genetic link to a disease in a small sample).

Semantic Understanding: Natural language, with its native ambiguity and complexity,
presents challenges for identifying causal relationships. Advances in NLP and LLMs, par-
ticularly in semantic modeling, have progressively evolved from basic NLP techniques (keyword
matching, parsing, lemmatization) to a deep natural language understanding which involves
understanding the semantics of our language (semantic modeling), introducing common sense,
“world knowledge” (common-sense reasoning) and resolving synonyms, and distinguishing be-
tween concepts in long context texts (context discerning) [16]. By leveraging these capabilities,
LLMs can uncover causal connections that are often hidden in medical text.

Explainability: While LLMs are inherently opaque due to their massive neural network ar-
chitectures, in this context, the explainability of these models for causal discovery comes not
merely from the final “A causes B” output but from its intermediate reasoning, when correctly
instructed to do it. LLMs can articulate the mechanism behind a causal claim (“BMI causes
Diabetes because excess adipose tissue increases insulin resistance”) [15]. By examining the
logical steps the model takes to arrive at a conclusion, we transform a “black-box” prediction
into a communication that provides a layer of explainability, building trust with the human
end-user.

In fact, in this global landscape, complicated by AI-generated fake images and synthetic content,
establishing transparency is essential. This is particularly true in the case of the current issue
with the Grok AI tool [54]. This necessity is also vital in the healthcare domain, where analysis
is derived from patient data and predictions directly inform clinical outcomes.

To address this, the methodology in this study preserves the LLM’s intermediate reasoning
within the final system output. This approach ensures that every causal discovery is not merely
a statistical output but a traceable assertion.

Challenges

Reversal Curse: A fundamental challenge in using LLMs for causality is the Reversal Curse
[55]. Research shows that LLMs trained on data like “A is B” often fail to deduce “B is A”
in reverse. In the context of causal discovery, this manifests as an inconsistency in identifying
causal directionality. An LLM might correctly answer “Does Smoking cause Cancer?” (Yes) but
fail to correctly identify the cause when asked “What causes Cancer?” or “Is Cancer a cause of
Smoking?” [55]. This limitation stems from the autoregressive (next-token prediction) training
objective, which encodes asymmetric weights between tokens, failing to capture the inherent
symmetry of logical equivalence or the bidirectional nature of structural understanding.

Absence of Ground Truth: Validating LLM-based discovery is difficult because, for many
                                                                                                 25


real-world systems (especially novel biological pathways), the “true” causal graph is unknown
[20]. Dependence on benchmarks like Tuebingen or Framingham is necessary, but there is a risk
of data contamination, the model may have memorized the specific dataset’s structure during
pre-training rather than reasoning causally.

Hallucinations: LLMs are stochastic engines that can generate plausible but false causal
claims (hallucinations) [43]. A model might confidently assert a causal link based on a common
misconception present in its training data (e.g., “Vaccines cause autism”) rather than scientific
consensus. This necessitates the use of robust uncertainty quantification methods to filter
outputs.

Unlike regular data, natural language is unstructured, high-dimensional, and large-scale, making
traditional causal methods ineffective. Besides, causal relationships in the text are often obscure,
and the complex semantics require advanced language models to uncover them. These challenges
create significant hurdles for causal inference in natural language processing [3].


3.1.3   LLMs for Causal Discovery Tasks

Understanding the causal relationships is a fundamental prerequisite to accurate decision-
making [53]. Causal discovery focuses on identifying the presence and direction of cause-and-
effect relationships that underlie a system. Typical tasks include pairwise causal discovery
(PCD), which evaluates causality between two variables, and full-graph discovery, which seeks
to identify the complete causal structure between all the variables in a system [3]. Recent ad-
vances leverage LLMs to assist in these tasks, either directly or in combination with statistical
causal discovery methods [56].

As shown in Figure 12, the interaction between humans and LLMs in causal discovery follows
a “Fast vs. Slow” dynamic. LLMs provide rapid, knowledge-driven inferences that may oc-
casionally be inaccurate, while human experts contribute slower, deliberate analytical rigor to
ensure reliability. In this collaborative workflow, humans can validate LLM outputs or provide
essential prior knowledge that the model might otherwise miss [15].

A. LLMs Directly Inferring Relationships. The LLM is queried directly to generate the

this framework, we can better define the strategic boundaries of our hybrid approach. It shows
how researchers are starting to move away from using LLMs as “all-in-one” solvers and instead
using them as specialized components of a larger ecosystem. LLMs are applied across the causal
pipeline [3]:

Causal Discovery: As established in the previous section, Causal Discovery serves as the
initial phase of the pipeline, focusing on inferring graph structures and identifying cause-effect
relationships among variables. Whether through Pairwise Causal Discovery (PCD) or Full
Graph Discovery, the goal is to map the systemic dependencies of the data. Many research
aims to acomplish this task [4, 60]. Our research focuses specifically on this node. This phase is

causal calculation.

Causal Effect Estimation: Some research [61] investigates how LLMs can estimate causal

strategies on predictions. It treats the LLM’s own internal architecture as a system to be

Other Tasks: The final category encompasses advanced reasoning capabilities that extend
beyond structure into logic and interpretation. This category includes causal attribution, the
process of identifying the root cause of an event, addressed in some studies [4]. It also includes
counterfactual reasoning, which requires the model to imagine “what if” scenarios and predict
the outcomes of events that did not occur; some studies follow this approach [18]; and causal
explanation, where research works [63] explore how the LLM generates human-readable narra-
tives justifying the existence of a causal relationship. These tasks depend heavily on the model’s
linguistic ability to bridge the gap between technical causal data and human understanding.

Therefore, in our work, we use a hybrid approach, recognizing the limitations in direct numerical
estimation. Rather than relying on an LLM to compute causal effect estimation, we leverage
the LLM’s semantic strengths for Causal Discovery to establish the graph structure. This
structure then serves as a formal constraint for a Causal Generative Model, which performs the
quantitative effect inference.

While the LLM ecosystem is expansive in causal inference, these sections provide a survey of
the state of the art to precisely locate our study at its intersection with Causal Discovery for
direct inference, encompassing both pairwise and full-graph discovery.


3.3   Causal Inference Methods in the Medical Domain

In the field of medicine, some literature [5] categorizes causal inference methods into five main
approaches based on their underlying principles: probability-based, model-based, scale-based,
regression-based, and balancing-based methods. This taxonomy offers a structured approach
to analyse different methodological assumptions, strengths and limitations in clinical contexts.
Recent literature emphasises the growing integration of causal inference with machine learning
and deep learning, for advancing precision by estimating personalized treatment effects [64].

Probability-Based and Matching Methods
                                                                                                29


Probability-based methods often begin with a conceptual representation of causal assumptions
[5]. Causal graphs, typically expressed as DAGs [1], provide a visual representation of variable
dependencies and causal relationships (as described in Section 2.1.2). While highly useful for
clarifying assumptions, causal graphs themselves do not estimate causal effects and must be
combined with analytical methods [5].

Some probability-based techniques rely on tree-based representations, which are widely used
[64]. Probability trees [65] are one of the simplest models of causal generative processes. They
possess clean semantics and can represent context-specific causal dependencies, which are nec-
essary for causal induction. A probability tree consists of an interconnected network of branches
and nodes, where each node represents a potential outcome and each branch denotes a specific
event with its associated probability. They facilitate the identification of critical points in the
decision-making process, highlighting the benefits and risks associated with different courses of
action [65].

Building on this concept, more advanced tree-based methods have been developed, such as
Causal Trees [66], Causal Forests [67], Model-Based Recursive Partitioning [68], and Bayesian
Causal Forests [69], which are designed to partition the covariate space in such a way that the
resulting subgroups exhibit heterogeneous treatment effects [64]. Causal forest extends the ran-
dom forest algorithm and integrates an honesty mechanism in the training process to overcome
potential overfitting issues. Model-based recursive partitioning automatically identifies patient
subgroups with differential treatment effects by segmenting a model of the overall treatment
effect, and linking subgroups to predictive factors using a decision tree approach. Bayesian
causal forest incorporates an estimate of the propensity function into the response model and
separately regularizes treatment effect heterogeneity [64]. Some limitations of tree-based meth-
ods include the fact that certain simplifications and assumptions need to be made regarding the
problem [5].

On the other hand, widely used techniques used for causal inference in the medical domain are
matching techniques [70, 71]. Matching aims to equalize the distribution of covariates in the
treated and control groups, thereby, isolating the effect of the exposure or treatment of interest
[71]. However as“perfect” matching is rarely feasible [70], statistical methods like Propensity
Score Matching (PSM) have been developed to enhance the matching process. PSM [72] esti-
mates the probability of treatment assignment (referred to as the propensity score) based on
observed confounders, enabling researchers to match or stratify cases and controls with similar
scores. PSM enables matching based on the propensity score rather than all covariates individ-
ually [72]. Formally, the propensity score is defined as the conditional probability of receiving a
specific treatment given a vector of observed covariates [70], reducing the impact of confound-
ing factors. As limitations, matching can induce a loss in sample size and statistical efficiency.
Therefore, careful variable selection, sensitivity analyses, and complementary methods (e.g.,
inverse probability weighting explained in balancing methods) are often recommended [70].

Model-Based Methods

Rubin’s Causal Model (RCM) [73] and Pearl’s Causal Model (SCM) [1] are the foundational
frameworks in causal inference [70, 64]. SCM was already explained in theoretical foundations
(see Chapter 2), while RCM, also known as the potential outcome framework, defines causality
based on comparing outcomes under different treatments, emphasizing randomized experiments
and counterfactual reasoning. These two frameworks offer complementary tools. While RCM
focuses on comparisons between two conditions and specializes in the initial identification of
relationships, using Pearl’s model requires starting with an initial causal DAG and allows for
the analysis of more complex causal networks [70].
                                                                                                30


Recent advancements incorporate deep neural networks in causal inference. Counterfactual
Matching with Adversarial Networks (CTAM) [74] combines Generative Adversarial Networks
(GANs) to create virtual control groups, allowing for the estimation of treatment effects while
handling confounding and endogeneity. There is also research [64, 5] that uses variational au-
toencoder (VAEs) [75] methods and Conditional Variational Autoencoders (CVAE) [76], such as
Disentangled Mediation Analysis Variational AutoEncoder (DMAVAE) [77], which learns a rep-
resentation of latent variables of treatment and covariates to estimate the effects of personalized
treatment. In addition, Fenerative Adversarial Nets for Inference of Individualized Treatment
Effects (GANITE) [75], uses adversarial learning to capture the uncertainty in counterfactual
outcomes.

GANs and VAEs have demonstrated impressive performance results on challenging tasks [78].
However, several issues limit their application in practice: they do not allow for exact evaluation
of the probability density of new points and training can be unstable due to a variety of phenom-
ena including mode collapse, posterior collapse, vanishing gradients and reduced interpretability
[78].

Other work in deep neural networks includes Normalizing Flows[78], generative models which
produce tractable distributions where both sampling and density evaluation can be efficient
and exact. A recent advancement in this direction is the causal normalizing flow [24], which
builds on normalizing flows to enable causal inference. It leverages results from nonlinear
independent component analysis and autoregressive flow architectures to identify causal models
from observational data and answer interventional and counterfactual queries.

Scale-Based Methods

Scale-based methods are primarily utilized in clinical practice to grade the probability of causal
relationships between specific treatments and adverse events, such as Adverse Drug Reactions
(ADR). The Naranjo Algorithm [79] employs a ten-question scoring system covering factors
like symptom onset timing and drug re-exposure to classify adverse reactions as “possible,”
“probable,” or “unlikely”. While valuable, the algorithm relies on responses to a series of
questions that may involve subjective judgments [80].

Similar to this algorithm, the WHO-UMC method [81] assesses the causal relationship between
a drug and an adverse event by carefully considering and weighing all relevant data pertaining
to the suspected reaction. It considers time-series relationships between clinical events and
suspected drug use. However, it relies on voluntary reporting by healthcare professionals and
patients [5].

Other more specific methods include the Roussel-Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM)
[82], which is used to evaluate the causal relationship between drugs and liver injury, with the
aim of determining whether drugs are the direct cause of liver damage. Some limitations include
diagnostic inaccuracy and lower reproducibility. To overcome these limitations, some modified
versions have been proposed, such as eRUCAM [82].

Regression-Based Methods

For individual treatment effect estimation, Counterfactual Regression (CFR) [83] simultaneously
fits a balanced representation of the data and outcome hypotheses. CFR learns a representation
of the data such that the processed distribution is more similar to the control distribution [83],
and fits a linear ridge regression model on it. Some limitations include the need for more
resources and expertise [5].
                                                                                                31


Balancing-Based Methods

As already mentioned, a primary challenge in causal inference is to mitigate the confounding
bias. Balancing-based methods address this challenge by reweighting or restructuring obser-
vational data. It is important to note that these methods have their own assumptions and
limitations and should be used carefully in conjunction with other methods to ensure the valid-
ity of causal inference [5].

Inverse Propensity Weighting (IPW) [83] is a re-weighting approach commonly used [64, 5, 70]
that initially calculates the probability of an observation receiving treatment and then assigns
weights to each data point based on the reciprocal of this probability. However, a large amount
of observational data is needed to estimate the reweighted weights. In addition, the Regression-
With-Residuals (RWR) [84] method is widley used as well.

While methods such as propensity score matching and inverse probability weighting can be
effective in adjusting for confounding, they may not fully account for time-dependent confound-
ing variables [70]. The G-formula [85] is a technique developed that handle time-dependent
confounding variables changing over time, which standard methods may fail to capture. By
modeling the joint distribution of exposure and confounders at each time point, the G-formula
simulates hypothetical intervention scenarios to provide unbiased estimates in longitudinal stud-
ies. Additionally, the Instrumental Variable (IV) [86] method addresses endogeneity and un-
measured confounding by introducing an exogenous variable that affects the treatment but
influences the outcome only indirectly through the treatment. For example, in medicine, ge-
netic markers are often used as instrumental variables to estimate the causal effects of drugs on
a disease [5]. Nonetheless, choosing appropriate instrumental variables can be challenging and
poor selection can lead to biased estimates.

The choice of a causal inference method should depend on the complexity of the research ques-
tion, the characteristics of the data, the amount of data available, and the overall objectives of
the study. In some cases, combining multiple causal inference approaches can provide deeper or
more refined insights. Regardless of the method employed, careful evaluation of the underlying
causal assumptions and rigorous assessment of data quality are essential to ensure that the
results are both reliable and interpretable.

While the methods discussed above offer valuable tools for causal inference in medical contexts,
causal flows, have several advantages [24]. Firstly, as generative models, they can generate new
samples that preserve causal relationships and that can be used for other tasks or purposes,
while safeguarding patient privacy (a critical consideration in healthcare applications). Sec-
ondly, unlike most non-generative approaches, including probability-based approaches, match-
ing methods, regression-based techniques and balancing methods, causal flows can compute
counterfactual queries. This allows researchers to estimate the potential outcomes of alterna-
tive treatment scenarios. Thirdly, once trained, causal flows enable inference on any causal
query without the need for model retraining. It is also important to note that most of the
above methods, excluding generative methods, implicitly assume that all variables except the
treatment and the outcome are confounders. While this assumption eliminates the need for an
explicit DAG, it is often incorrect in practice. Including variables that are not true confounders,
such as mediators or colliders, can increase variance and introduce structural biases. In con-
trast, causal flows leverage the explicit causal structure encoded in the DAG, properly assigning
each variable its appropriate role, whether as a confounder, mediator, or collider, allowing for a
more precise modelling of complex causal relationships. All these properties make causal flows
well-suited to the problem addressed in this work.

4.1.1    LLM-Based Causal Discovery

LLM Selection & Configuration

This framework leverages the semantic reasoning capabilities of LLMs to identify and validate
causal relationships. Selecting and configuring the appropiate model is the critical first step.
This section justifies the models chosen for this research and provides a rational for selection
based on specific project needs and available resources.

The selection of the underlying models was driven by a multi-dimensional analysis of in-
fraestructure, cost, context capability, performance benchmarks and speed. This
selection process was further shaped by the project’s corporate context.

Model Access and Deployment

These are the primary methods to access and deploy LLMs:


   • Closed-Source Models (via API): Proprietary models such as OpenAI’s GPT series
     [88] or Google’s Gemini [89]. They offer high reasoning performance and require no infras-
     tructure management, though they involve recurring costs and sending data to external
     servers.

   • Open-Source Models (via API): Accessing open-weight models like Llama [90] or
     Mistral [91] through third-party providers (e.g. Hugging Face [92]). This provides a
     balance between high-speed inference and lower costs without managing local hardware.

   • Open-Source Models (Local): Deploying models on private infrastructure or locally.
     This option offers maximum data privacy and customization, but it requires substantial
     GPU resources and ongoing maintenance.


A fundamental architectural decision in this work was to use proprietary models via API rather
than deploying open-source models. This choice is justified by the following factors: First,
deploying open-source models requires significant GPU orchestration and specialized resources.
In a corporate environment, leveraging managed APIs allows the research to focus on causal
discovery logic rather than hardware maintenance. Secondly, specific tasks in this work, such as
quantifying hallucinations and uncertainty, require access to raw token probabilities (logprobs).
While most API providers (such as Anthropic or Google Gemini in its standard tier) abstract
these values away, OpenAI provides stable and access to logprobs through its API. This trans-
parency is used for calculating the uncertainty metrics detailed in subsequent sections and we
needed to select one Provider to adjust all our code methods. Lastly, OpenAI’s GPT family cur-
rently sets the industry benchmark for instruction-following. This ensures that the experimental
results are representative of the current potential of LLMs in complex reasoning tasks.

The core API implementation relies on PyWhy-LLM [93]. In terms of APIs the library uses
Guidance, which is an efficient programming paradigm originally developed by Microsoft for
steering language models [94].

While the initial setup utilized Guidance, secure and scalable API connections were established
[95] to access to OpenAI via Portkey. Portkey [96] is a gateway that acts as an intermediary
                                                                                              36


layer, enhancing reliability and observability. This integration provides a flexible API layer,
allowing us to connect to several LLM providers (e.g., OpenAI, AWS). As justified previously,
we selected OpenAI.

For the specific task of quantifying uncertainty and reducing hallucinations, the API connections
we modified: we bypassed the Guidance framework and utilized a direct interface via the native
OpenAI client [88] (through the Portkey gateway). This approach enables direct access to the
raw token probabilities via one parameter, that will be used for calculating the uncertainty
(described in the next sections).

Cost

When we use an LLM model through a provider such as OpenAI, we pay for the amount of
text we process. This text is measured in tokens and we are billed for two components:


   • Input Tokens: The content, data or context we send to the model (the prompt, context
     plus any attached document).

   • Output Tokens: The response generated by the model that is sent back to the user.


As a general rule, output tokens are significantly more expensive than input tokens, this is
because generating new, coherent text requires more computation than simply reading existing
text.

Context window

Every AI model has a limit to how much information it can read and remember within a
conversation. We call this its context window. Smaller context limits imply that the model will
forget the beginning of a long conversation or will not be able to process too many documents
at once. Larger context limits imply that the model will be able to keep long conversations and
process long documents.

Beyond input, while the context window limits the tokens an LLM can process as input, a sep-
arate limit governs the maximum number of tokens it can generate as an answer: the maximum
generation length. For perspective, a 100,000-token window is roughly equivalent to 75,000
words; 150 pages of single-spaced text.

Benchmarks

The most common way to judge an LLM’s raw capability is through standardized tests, often
referred to as benchmarks. These benchmarks work in a straightforward manner: they supply a
task that an LLM must accomplish, evaluate model performance according to a certain metric
and produce a score based on that metric. In this way, models are benchmarked based on their
capabilities, such as coding, common sense and reasoning.

While there are multiple benchmarks available, a popular one is Massive Multitask Language
Understanding (MMLU) [97], a score that reflects a model’s general knowledge and reasoning
across 57 academic subjects (like math, history and law). As a general rule, a higher MMLU
indicates a model that is better at understanding complex, nuanced instructions and can draw
on broader, more accurate knowledge. However, while MMLU is a good starting point, the
real-world performance of a model depends entirely on the specific task and on the domain of
the data.
                                                                                                 37


Speed

We refer to the overall time a user waits for an answer as end-to-end latency. Latency is the
direct metric and we have it measured in our experiments but also there are other metrics
commonly used for model selection:


   • Prompt Processing (Time to First Token, or TTFT): How long it takes the model to
     “read” and “understand” the prompt. This is heavily influenced by the length of the
     input.

   • Token Generation (Time Per Output Token, or TPOT): How quickly the model writes
     its response, measured in tokens/second. This is heavily influenced by the model’s size
     and architecture.


The speed usually means a low TTFT and a high TPOT. Model creators optimize families
for different goals. They use techniques like knowledge distillation, pruning, and training for
smaller sizes to create models that are explicitly fast and cheap. When we see a name like
“Flash,” “Haiku,” “Mini,” or “Nano,” this is a direct signal from the creator that the model’s
primary purpose is speed.

Pairwise Cause-Effect Prediction

To establish a baseline for causal discovery, we extended the pairwise prediction approach from
the pywhyllm library. This approach decomposes the multivariate causal discovery problem
into a series of isolated pair tasks.
                                                                                                 40


For a Full-graph use case, we enhanced this pairwise methods to be context-aware. While the
discovery process still iterates through variable pairs (using all possible combinations), the input
prompt is dynamically modified to include the list of all other variables currently in the analysis.
This modification ensures that the LLM does not evaluate the pair (X, Y ) isolated; instead, it
explicitly considers whether any of the other variables could explain the relationship.

The model is tasked with classifying the relationship into one of three categories (A, B, C):

A) X → Y : Variable X causes variable Y directly.

B) Y → X: Variable Y causes variable X directly.

C) No Direct Link / Mediated: Neither variable causes the other directly, or the relationship
is mediated by one of the other variables provided in the context list.

Prompting Strategies

To maximize the reasoning capabilities of the LLMs, we iterated over different prompts, keeping
in mind some prompting strategies:

In-Context Learning (ICL): We leveraged ICL to guide both the output format and the reason-
ing of the model. By embedding task-specific demonstrations directly in the prompt, the model
adapts its behavior without any parameter updates. In particular, for tasks involving the iden-
tification of potential mediators variables that explain the relationship between an independent
and a dependent variable, a one-shot example is included that illustrates how to detect and
reason about third variables within a causal graph.

Chain of Thought: Chain of Thought (CoT) prompting is used to encourage the LLM to output
a series of intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at the final answer. This strategy is
known to improve the accuracy and interpretability of LLM outputs, especially for complex
logical tasks like causal discovery, by forcing a thought process.

To mitigate the risk of hallucination and superficial pattern matching, we forced a Chain of
Thought strategy. Rather than asking for a direct classification, our prompts explicitly instruct
the LLM to “Provide reasoning” before committing to a final answer and to generate a series of
intermediate logical steps: Evaluating the mechanism and plausibility of each causal direction:
Option A, B and C, then evaluating the contextual variables provided to identify potential
mediators. Only after these factors are weighed is the final decision enclosed in specific XML
tags (e.g., <answer>A</answer>) to facilitate automated parsing.

Implementation Summary

The baseline causal discovery pipeline utilizes a custom Python class, SimpleModelSuggester.
This class orchestrates the interaction between the causal discovery process and the backend
LLMs, managing prompt construction, API communication, and response parsing.


  1. Iterative Causal Discovery: The method is designed to perform full-graph causal
     discovery using only a given list of variable names as input. The algorithm automates
     the graph construction by iterating through all unique variable pairs in the provided
     list. For each pair (X, Y ), it dynamically constructs a prompt that injects the remaining
     variables from the list as potential mediators. The final output is a dictionary of pairwise
     relationships (representing the direct edges A → B), effectively allowing us to reconstruct
     the complete causal structure solely from the initial set of variables.
                                                                                              41


  2. Output Parsing via Regex: Since we employ CoT prompting, the model generates
     extensive free-text reasoning before providing a classification at the end of the reasoning
     process. To correctly extract the final decision embedded in the output text, we imple-
     mented parsing mechanisms using Regular Expressions (Regex). The system enforces a
     specific output format, instructing the model to wrap its decision in <answer> tags, with
     the expected enum labels (“A”, “B”, or “C”).
     Structured generation, the process of producing content in standardized formats like JSON
     and XML, is widely utilized in real-world applications to extract key output information
     from LLMs [101]. Although this kind of frameworks (e.g., Pydantic [102]) were considered,
     we opted for this tag-based approach to preserve direct access to the raw token probabil-
     ities of the answers that might be affected by the previous reasoning, instead of isolating
     the answer token. Also, it has been found by some studies (see citations [101] and [103])
     that the specific probability of the answer token can often be obscured by strict format
     constraints and performance degradation in reasoning tasks can be caused as a result.


Method 2: Tool-Augmented LLM Discovery (RAG)

LLMs have achieved remarkable success, though they still face significant limitations. To over-
come these challenges, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [43] enhances LLMs to dynam-
ically access and retrieve real-time, contextually relevant information by referencing external
knowledge from multiple sources as described in the Theoretical Foundations in our work.
Therefore, in this framework, we implement Tool Augmentation via Retrieval-Augmented Gen-
eration (RAG).

Pywhy-LLM Extension: The Augmented Suggesters Component

In pywhy-LLM library [93], the functionality of the Augmented Suggesters class extends the
standard Suggesters components described, by incorporating RAG. This approach grounds the
LLM’s suggestions in external, specialized knowledge bases, moving beyond the model’s internal
training data. The initial RAG pipeline leverages the CauseNet dataset as its primary external
knowledge source.

We implemented several custom functions within the Suggesters and AugmentedSuggesters
components to extend the baseline library capabilities. These developments involve the modifi-
cation of prompts, specialized API integration settings, the extraction of logits and confidence
scores (concepts described in Section 4.1.2), as well as the regex-based extraction of specific
answer tokens among others to enable Method 2. The technical implementation, logic, and
theoretical justification for these functional enhancements, essentials for our uncertainty quan-
tification are described in detail throughout the subsequent subsections of this methodology
chapter. Concretely, for the external connections, we detail here the main contributions.

Our contributions:

   • RAG Data Source Expansion: We extended the AugmentedSuggesters by incorporating a
     new knowledge source into the RAG pipeline: PubMed.
   • Live Connection and Retrieval: This enhancement involves establishing a live connection
     with the PubMed API for retrieving and dynamically embedding relevant scientific articles
     based on the user’s input (input variables).

External knowledge sources Integration
                                                                                               42


1. PubMed Connection

The connection to PubMed, a database of biomedical literature [104] was established in order
to retrieve relevant scientific abstracts. This information provides expert evidence to the LLM
about potential causal relationships between medical or biological variables, particularly useful
for the Framingham Heart Study, where relying on general knowledge might be insufficient.
The connection is live, accessing references and abstracts on life sciences and biomedical topics.

A dynamic retrieval system was implemented. This system queries the PubMed API with
several optimised queries, using disjunctive and conjunctive operators for every pair of variables
being analysed (e.g. BMI and cardiac ischemia).

To optimize the retrieval of relevant papers, we implemented a multi-query search strategy.
This involves a cascading approach with over nine variations per variable pair. For instance,
queries range from highly specific searches, such as combining variables with field restrictions
(“variable1” AND “variable2”) to broader queries including terms like “causal,” “association,”
and “risk factor.” The system iterates through these queries to retrieve PubMed IDs (PMIDs)
and the full metadata and abstracts, until a target count is reached. We track PMIDs to prevent
duplication. The extracted metadata (Title, Abstract, Authors, and Publication year) is unified
before being passed to the next RAG stage.

2. CauseNet Connection

Finding relevant causal pairs requires matching user-given variable names, which can be seman-
tically similar to specific CauseNet entries. We address this with a two-stage hybrid search.
Stage 1 uses BM25 (Best Matching 25 [105]) ranking on normalized variable names to provide
fast initial filtering, retrieving the top 50 candidates based on term frequency. Stage 2 refines
this pool by using a neural embedding model, Sentence-BERT (all-MiniLM-L6-v2 [106]), to
compute cosine similarity between the query and the candidate entries. A similarity threshold
of 0.35 was set.

When a match is found, all supporting sentences from the CauseNet sources are extracted as
contextual evidence. Notice that if no relevant matches are found, we proceed with trusting
LLM internal knowledge like in the baseline method.

RAG Pipeline Implementation

Our concrete RAG workflow is built with the LangChain Framework [107] and demonstrated
in Figure 18:

Stage 1: Parallel Search. This initial stage queries both external knowledge sources. The
CauseNet Search involves variable normalization, BM25 keyword search, and semantic re-
ranking to extract causenet text. Simultaneously, the PubMed Search generates query vari-
ations, executes PubMed API calls, aggregates abstracts, and formats them with metadata.

Stage 2: Document Processing and Vectorization. The retrieved text is processed to create a
vector store. First, the complete CauseNet text and PubMed text are combined into a single
corpus. This text is then broken down using a LangChain chunking method [108] (Recur-
siveCharacterTextSplitter). These chunks are converted into embeddings using the Sentence-
Transformers model [109] (all-MiniLM-L6-v2 [106]) and stored in an in-memory Chroma vector
database. This setup ensures that the retrieval is performed on context-aware chunks of relevant
information.
                                                                                                43


Stage 3: Semantic Retrieval at Query Time. When the system needs to analyze a causal pair
(the user query), the query is encoded using the same embedding model. A similarity search is
performed against the Chroma vector store, calculating the cosine similarity between the query
embedding and all chunk embeddings. The system retrieves the top k = 7 (by default) most
relevant chunks, which are then concatenated to form the context variable. Chroma [110] was
selected for its open-source availability, local deployment capability, high retrieval speed, and
ease of integration with Python-based pipelines.

Stage 4: LLM Query with Augmented Context. The final stage uses the LangChain RAG Chain
to orchestrate prompt construction and generation. The context will be formed by the system
prompt, which defines the LLM’s role as a causal reasoning assistant, along with the retrieved
chunks. The augmented prompt is then sent to the LLM. The model is configured with a
low temperature (0.3) for deterministic output and instructed to provide a structured response
containing the reasoning, the final answer classification (<answer>A</answer>) and confidence
scores. If both external sources fail to return relevant text (fail because no relevant information
is found), the system uses the LLM’s internal knowledge to do completion, logging the event
for transparency.

4.1.2   Uncertainty Quantification Methods

This section details the methodologies implemented to assess hallucination risks and quantify the
uncertainty in LLM-based causal discovery. We explore three approaches for measuring model
reliability: Self-Reported Confidence, Token Log-Probabilities, and the HallBayes framework.
                                                                                                 45


Method 1: LLM Confidence Scores

Our baseline method for assessing model certainty is Self-Reported Confidence. In this approach,
we explicitly instruct the model to introspect and output a scalar value ∈ [0, 1] representing its
certainty for each predicted causal edge.

To ensure this value is captured accurately, the prompt is augmented with a specific instruction
requiring the model to encapsulate its judgment within <confidence> tags. This provides
the model with a clear scale of reference, defining high certainty (e.g., 0.9) versus significant
ambiguity (e.g., 0.5), and explicitly asks the system to evaluate its confidence regarding the
selection of the correct relationship among the available categories (A, B, or C).

Finally, the LLM’s response is parsed using Regular Expressions (Regex) to extract the causal
classification and the confidence score. This process returns a structured object containing both
the causal claim and its associated metadata.


Method 2: Token Probability Extraction

To address the subjectivity of self-reported scores created by an LLM, we use token log-
probabilities. These are direct measurements of the model’s internal probability distribution
over the vocabulary for the next token prediction, often used as a proxy for predictive uncer-
tainty.

For a given token t in the vocabulary V, the log-probability is derived from the softmax layer:
                                                      exp(zt )

where zt represents the logits from the final transformer layer. The actual probability is:

Unlike self-reported scores, this metric is deterministic and directly reflects a probabilistic mea-
sure.

These probabilities are extracted via the OpenAI API by enabling the logprobs parameter.
We specifically isolate the log-probability of the Answer Token (e.g., “A”, “B”, or “C”) using
Regex since the model ends its reasoning by stating “Therefore, the final answer is “A”, “B”,
or “C”. This provides an objective measure of how much probability the model assigns to the
chosen causal relationship compared to alternative hypotheses. Recall that, as established in
our classification schema, option A represents X → Y (X causes Y), option B represents Y → X
(Y causes X), and option C corresponds to no direct Link or a mediated relationship.

For the implementation, we first execute a completion request to the OpenAI API with the
logprobs parameter enabled. Next, we parse the model’s text response to identify the chosen
answer (using Regex to find the content within <answer> tags) and locate that specific token
in the response metadata to extract its exact log-probability value.


Method 3: HallBayes Framework

HallBayes is a post-hoc calibration framework designed that quantifies hallucination risk in
LLMs, without requiring model retraining. HallBayes converts an LLM prediction into a mea-
                                                                                                46


surable bounded hallucination risk. It is based on the theory established in the paper “Pre-
dictable Compression Failures: Why Language Models Actually Hallucinate” [111] and the
assciated HallBayes GitHub repository [112] providing a mathematical framework for model
reliability.

We implemented HallBayes specifically as a refinement layer for the estimated causal graph
(or causal edges). Each candidate relationship is treated as a hypothesis that must be verified
independently by the language model. This serves as a “double-check” mechanism, which is
designed to reduce the risk of falsely establishing causal edges. In summary, HallBayes treats
each proposed relationship as a query to be validated and provides a quantified risk that it is a
hallucination.

To perform this validation, HallBayes constructs a set of content-weakened prompts, also called
“rolling priors”: given an original prompt, a set of modified prompts {Sk }m    k=1 is generated by
applying semantic masking, which systematically replaces entities or key terms with placehold-
ers. In summary, the system generates multiple masked versions of the prompt to test if the
model’s answer relies on specific entities or generic patterns. It generates prompts where entities
are progressively removed. Examples:


     - Original: “Does smoking cause lung cancer?”
     - Mask 1: “Does [Entity A] cause lung cancer?”
     - Mask 2: “Does smoking cause [Entity B]?”
     - Mask 3: “Does [Entity A] cause [Entity B]?”


This technique removes critical information while preserving the overall structure of the query.
The goal is to observe how the model’s predictions change when important information is
gradually removed.

Although the knowledge required to answer the query is contained within the model’s inter-
nal parameters, the prompt plays a critical role in constraining which parts of that internal
knowledge are selected (like a search query, the more specific the query, the more restricted
the search space becomes). Semantic masking does not measure how much the model knows;
rather, it measures how much the prompt restricts the space of possible answers. When key
entities or variables are masked, the model relies more on generic patterns, which increases un-
certainty about whether the original relationship was supported by specific internal knowledge.
If masking has little effect on the prediction, it suggests that the answer was weakly constrained
and likely the result of guessing or hallucination. Thus, masking provides a method to assess
whether a proposed causal relationship is grounded in precise internal knowledge or arises from
random guessing and pattern completion.

an incorrect or fabricated response increases in a quantifiable way.

4.1.3   Building Probabilistic Graphs

In Bayesian statistics, probability is used as a fundamental measure of uncertainty [113]. Within
this paradigm, after applying HallBayes as the refinement method, each candidate causal edge is
associated with an estimated RoH. This quantifies the presence of the causal relationship and the
risk that the edge is a hallucination. To capture the uncertainty in the language model’s causal
predictions, we convert this risk estimate into a probabilistic edge representation. Specifically,
the probability of existence for each edge is defined as the complement of its RoH, actually
recovering the probability defined in the Hallbayes Framework, pmax :


                                                     ¯ q̄)
                                     RoH = 1 − pmax (∆,                                       (17)

To ensure the stability of our results, we execute the HallBayes procedure three times for each
input. For a given edge X → Y validated over n independent runs, the probability is calculated
as the average over all runs:

                                                   n
                                              1X
                                 P (X → Y ) =    Pi (X → Y )                                  (18)
                                              n
                                                  i=1


where Pi = 1 − RoHi if the edge is validated (accepted) by the model, and Pi = RoHi if it is
rejected. This approach ensures that a rejection with high uncertainty does not eliminate the
edge, but rather assigns it a probability proportional to the hallucination risk.

This averaging process accounts for the stochastic nature of LLM outputs and provides a more
reliable estimate of the hallucination risk and its associated probability. This probability serves
as the Bernoulli parameter for the subsequent sampling phase. For example, if HallBayes
estimates that the edge “A causes B” is valid and has a 20% RoH, the corresponding probabilistic
edge weight becomes 0.8, reflecting the 80% confidence in the relationship.


Monte Carlo Graph Sampling

Once all edges are represented probabilistically, we employ Monte Carlo sampling to generate a
collection of causal graphs. Each edge is independently sampled according to its probability of
existence, producing a diverse set of graph estimates. In our experiments, we sample 100 causal
graphs from the probabilistic structure, each consistent with the LLM-derived probabilities.

Each potential edge is treated as an independent Bernoulli random variable:

                                Edgeij ∼ Bernoulli(P (Xi → Xj ))                              (19)


This collection allows downstream analyses to incorporate not only estimates of causal relation-
ships but also their associated confidence.

By representing probabilistic edges and leveraging Monte Carlo sampling, this approach provides
a framework to quantify uncertainty in causal graph construction, reflecting both the limitations
of LLMs and the potential fragility of individual edges as identified by HallBayes.
                                                                                                50


Topological Ordering and Adjacency Matrix Construction

A critical requirement for causal flow models (such as CausalNSF) is that the input graph must
be a valid DAG, represented as an upper-triangular adjacency matrix. To enforce this, we
implement Kahn’s Algorithm to linearly order vertices such that for every directed edge u → v,
vertex u comes before v. The algorithm proceeds as follows:


  1. Count incoming edges (in-degrees) for all nodes. Add “roots” (variables with in-degree 0)
     to a processing queue.

  2. Remove a node from the queue, add it to the topological order, and decrement the in-
     degree of its neighbors.

  3. Repeat iteratively. If a neighbor’s in-degree drops to 0, add it to the queue. Continue
     until the queue is empty.

  4. If the final list contains all variables, the graph is a valid DAG. We then construct the
     adjacency matrix A according to this order, ensuring it is strictly upper-triangular.


A summary of the steps involved in the complete workflow for probabilistic causal graph con-
struction with HallBayes:


  1. HallBayes Refinement: Each candidate edge is evaluated by HallBayes to estimate the
     Risk of Hallucination (RoH). Multiple runs are performed to reduce stochastic variability.

  2. Probabilistic Edge Assignment: The complement of the averaged RoH, P (X → Y ) =
     1 − RoH, is used as the probability of existence for each edge. For example, a 20% RoH
     for edge A → B corresponds to a probability of 0.8.

  3. Monte Carlo Sampling: Each edge is sampled independently according to its probabil-
     ity to generate a collection of causal graphs. Repeating this process 100 times produces
     diverse graph realizations, capturing the uncertainty inherent in the LLM-derived causal
     predictions.

  4. Topological Ordering & Matrix Construction: To ensure each sampled graph is
     a valid DAG, we topologically order the variables such that causes precede effects. We
     construct strictly upper-triangular adjacency matrices based on this order.

  5. Estimated Graphs: The resulting collection of estimated graphs enables representation
     of the causal structure under uncertainty that will be used in the causal inference tasks.

4.2.1    Datasets

Our work utilizes two main datasets for evaluating the proposed causal discovery methods. We
require data with known ground truth: known causal directions to evaluate the performance of
LLMs in identifying causal relationships. We explore two dimensions:


   • Pairwise relationships: The focus is on the causal direction between isolated pairs of
     variables. The goal is to perform the intial experimentation on general pairs due to their
     their simplicity compared to full-graph CD. This is why the Tuebingen dataset will be
     used.

   • Full-graph causal discovery: The focus is to identify the complete structure of a multi-
     variable system. This dimension serves as a bridge to causal inference. For causal in-
     ference, the dataset should contain multiple variables and a complete causal structure,
     referred as the “ground truth graph”. The ground truth graph and the dataset are re-

     Study is utilized as a real-world clinical use case to demonstrate full-graph discovery in

Tuebingen Dataset

The Tuebingen dataset [114] is a well-known benchmark in the causal discovery community, de-
signed to evaluate the ability of algorithms or models to determine the causal direction between
two observed variables. The fundamental objective is to distinguish between X → Y (X causes
Y) and Y → X (Y causes X), where the ground truth causal direction is known.

Regarding the structure, the dataset is heterogeneous and diverse, presenting a challenge for
causal inference methods. It consists of 108 cause-effect pairs extracted from approximately
37 different sources, including repositories like the UCI Machine Learning Repository. The
pairs cover a wide range of domains and inclue mixed data types: continuous, discrete, and
time-dependent variables:


   • Meteorology: e.g., Altitude → Temperature

   • Biology/Medicine: e.g., Urinary GAG concentration → Age

   • Economics: e.g., Age → Stock dividends

   • Physics/Engineering: e.g., Pressure at t → Pressure at t + 1


Each pair is provided as observational samples in a text file with two columns representing the
variables. The ground truth is contained as a binary label for each pair indicating the causal
direction (X → Y or Y → X), established based on expert domain knowledge and physical
laws.


Framingham Heart Study - Clinical Real World Use Case

The Framingham Heart Study (FHS) is a crucial resource for identifying and studying cardio-
vascular disease (CVD) risk factors. For causal discovery research, it is frequently used as a
real-world benchmark to evaluate the clinical relevance of causal structure learning algorithms.
The Framingham Heart Study is available online at http://www.annemergmed.com.

The study utilizes a CSV dataset comprising 4,434 patient records and includes the following
variables: randid (patient identifier), bmi (body mass index), age, diabetes1, cvd (major acute
cardiovascular event), mif or chd (cardiac ischemia), sysbp (systolic blood pressure), and diabp
(diastolic blood pressure). This work prioritizes the use of continuous variables over discrete
ones whenever possible to maximize the information density available for the causal inference
process.

Since the true causal graph for complex medical data is generally unknown [20] specially in the
medical domain, this work adopts the causal graph proposed by Kyriacou et al. [22], who utilize
established medical and epidemiological knowledge to serve as a Ground Truth Reference for
evaluation:

Ground truth Graph: A constructed DAG published by Kyriacou et al. [22], based on
validated medical knowledge derived from the FHS itself and related studies. This graph encodes
the current consensus on how variables like BMI, blood pressure, diabetes affect outcomes like
cardiovascular disease.

4.2.2   LLM-Based Causal Discovery Evaluation: Pairwise Relationships

The evaluation is performed for the methods proposed in the Causal Discovery Framework:


   • Method 1: Standalone LLM.

   • Method 2: LLM Tool Augmented - Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG).


We evaluate the methods for pairwise relationships based on standard classification metrics,
since the answer can be A, B or C. In this research, Accuracy is selected as the primary
metric for pairwise evaluation. This metric represents the percentage of correctly predicted
causal directions across all pairs. The Tuebingen dataset is used for evaluating CD Pairwise
relationships.

Application in LLM Research: Addressing the Reversal Curse.

The Reversal Curse [55] describes the difficulty that LLMs face in bidirectional reasoning, such
as inferring a relationship when the order of entities is reversed (e.g., inferring ”B is A’s child”
when trained on ”A is B’s father”). When evaluating LLMs using pairwise relationship datasets,
researchers often employ a reverse causal discovery [4] to explicitly test the models’ ability to
generalize logically, thereby targeting the Reversal Curse limitation.

To test an LLM’s causal reasoning, the original set of pairs is doubled. For each pair (X, Y )
where the ground truth is, for example, X → Y :

   • Forward Sample (Correct): The model is prompted with a query asking for the causal
     direction based on the data presented as (X, Y ). The correct answer is X → Y .

   • Reverse Sample (Reversed Order): The model is prompted with an identical query, but
     the data is presented in the reversed order as (Y, X). The correct causal answer derived
     from the data itself is still X → Y , but the spatial/presentation bias (Reversal Curse)
     often leads the model to incorrectly predict Y → X.

This transformation effectively creates a dataset of N × 2 samples, where N is the number of
original pairs.


4.2.3   Uncertainty Quantification Methods Evaluation

The evaluation of the three proposed uncertainty quantification methods: Self-Reported Confi-
dence, Token Log-Probabilities, and the HallBayes framework, is conducted using the Tuebingen
causality pairs benchmark [114]. In this way, the methods’ performance can be assessed across
diverse causal-effect domains.

In Bayesian statistics, probability serves as a fundamental measure of uncertainty [113], these
three methods derives the probabiliy through distinct mechanisms:


  1. LLM Confidence Scores (Baseline): The probabilities are the model’s self-reported
     confidence scores. This method serves as a baseline to evaluate predicted confidence and
     identify overconfidence bias, a phenomenon where LLMs frequently assign high confidence
                                                                                                54


        to incorrect hallucinations [115]. Since models are often natively overoptimistic, this
        method primarily highlights the limitations of internal self-assessment and justifies the
        need for more mathematically grounded uncertainty metrics.

  2. Token Log-Probabilities: This method extracts raw token log-probabilities to access
     the statistical likelihoods assigned during text generation. The log-probability is converted
     to a standard probability score by calculating p = elog(p) . This approach provides a more
     granular view of the model’s internal state than subjective self-reports, enabling direct
     comparison between the statistical probability of a token and the empirical success rate
     of causal predictions. It allows determining whether the statistical likelihood correlates
     effectively with the objective correctness of the predicted causal direction.

  3. HallBayes Framework: As discussed in the Hallbayes section, the probability is derived
     as 1 − RoH, representing the inverse of the Risk of Hallucination (RoH) [111].


To ensure a consistent comparison, for all three methods each predicted probability score is
compared against the actual outcome (i.e., the model’s causal response versus the ground truth
on the Tuebingen benchmark). The evaluation relies on constructing calibration curves,
which plot predicted confidence levels against the empirical accuracy.

Calibration measures how well a model’s predicted probabilities reflect the true likelihood of
outcomes, providing a way to quantify and trust model uncertainty [115]. In a perfectly cali-
brated system, the calibration curve coincides with the diagonal identity line, so that a predicted
confidence of 80% corresponds exactly to an 80% success rate. The closer a method’s curve lies
to this line, the more reliable its probability estimates.

Based on the analysis and the calibration curves, the most robust method to be subsequently
applied to our primary, real-world medical use case is selected.


4.2.4     LLM-Based Causal Discovery Evaluation: Full-Graph

Benchmark: the PC algorithm

To provide a benchmark for out LLM-based approach, the PC (Peter-Clark) algorithm was im-
plemented as a traditional CD baseline. The PC algorithm [50] is a constraint-based CD method
that infers causal structures by testing conditional independence relationships in observational
data, without relying on external knowledge or language models.

The PC algorithm operates in two main phases. First, it constructs an undirected skeleton
by iteratively testing conditional independence between all variable pairs using statistical tests.
Second, it orients edges into a DAG by identifying v-structures (colliders) and propagating
orientations based on acyclicity constraints. This process is purely data-driven, making it really
different from our LLM-based knowledge-driven approach.

Regarding the implementation, the logic of the the PC algorithm is contained in a function,
accepting a pandas DataFrame as input and returning a structured dictionary containing the
estimated DAG, skeleton graph, edge dictionary, adjacency matrix, and associated parameters.
The implementation utilized the stable variant of PC from the pgmpy library [116], a standard
tool for graph modelling.
                                                                                             55


We configured the PC algorithm with a significance level of α = 0.05 for conditional indepen-
dence testing, using chi-square tests as the statistical criterion.

The PC algorithm was applied to the same Framingham dataset. After the graph estimation,
the adjacency matrix was extracted to be compared against the ground truth.

Constraint-based methods, like PC, differ from score-based approaches [50] (e.g. Greedy Equiv-
alence Search) which optimize a global score over possible DAGs; for further comparison, we
could implement a score-based method such as GES in future work.


Full-graph metrics

In order to evaluate advancements in LLM methods for full-graph discovery, we compared our
LLM-based approaches with the PC algorithm. For this validation, we used the Framingham
Heart Study, which consists of seven variables and fourteen edges in the ground truth. The eval-
uation standard metrics for graph reconstruction were selected as follows: Normalised Structural
Hamming Distance (nSHD), the confusion matrix elements, precision, recall and accuracy.

To evaluate the structural accuracy of the discovered graphs, we need metrics that compare
the estimated structure [21]. The first metric presented is the normalized Structural Hamming
Distance (nSHD)[117]. Before defining the normalized version, it is necessary to introduce the
Structural Hamming Distance (SHD) [118], a standard metric in causal discovery, which is
defined as the minimum number of edge insertions, deletions, and reversals that are needed in
order to transform the estimated graph into the true one. Due to the specific density of this
graph, where 14 edges are distributed across a relatively small set of variables, we report the
normalized version to account for graph density. The nSHD is also a widely used metric [21] to
compare two graphs in Causal Discovery. Let A, B ∈ {0, 1}m×m be the adjacency matrices of
two graphs GA and GB on m vertices. The nSHD distance is defined as:

This represents the number of operations needed to adjust graph A in graph B, normalized by
the total number of possible edges. The distance is zero if and only if GA and GB coincide,
and it is at most one. Therefore, a lower value indicates higher structural similarity. This
normalization accounts for graph density and allows for a rigorous comparison between the
data-driven PC algorithm and our knowledge-driven LLM approaches.

Beyond structural distance, we treat graph reconstruction as a binary classification task to
evaluate the presence and direction of edges. These evaluations are based on the confusion
matrix elements:


   • True Positives (TP): Causal edges correctly identified in both existence and direction.

   • False Positives (FP): Predicted edges that do not exist in the ground truth (structural
     “hallucinations”).

   • False Negatives (FN): Actual causal relationships that the model failed to identify.

   • True Negatives (TN): Correctly identified absences of causal relationships.

Using these elements, we derive Precision (the reliability of predicted edges), Recall (the abil-
ity to capture all true causal links), and Accuracy (the overall proportion of correct predictions
across all possible variable pairs). This set of metrics ensures that both the structural topology
and the classification accuracy of the discovered graphs are rigorously assessed.

4.3.1    Data Pre-processing

Transitioning from the LLM-based discovery phase to the training of causal generative models
requires the application of standard machine learning techniques, including Exploratory Data
Analysis (EDA), data cleaning, and normalization.
                                                                                                 57


First, we address data quality issues by handling missing values; specifically, empty samples
found in the BMI variable are imputed using the mode (few empty values). Then, we should
mention that our dataset is heterogeneous, containing both continuous variables (such as BMI,
systolic pressure, and glucose levels) and discrete binary variables (such as diabetes).

To ensure compatibility with the Causal Flow model, we apply specific preprocessing techniques
tailored to each variable type:

Continuous Variables: We standardize all continuous features to facilitate model conver-
gence. Using a StandardScaler, variables are transformed. Then, we apply the transformation
on the full dataset to establish these scaling parameters. Afterwards, we apply inverse transfor-
mations during the intervention stage to recover the original clinical data scale for interpretabil-
ity.

Discrete/Binary Variables: These variables are not normalized. However, since causal flows
work better with continuous variables, discrete variables can have some challenges. To mitigate
this, the Causal Flow models used [119] incorporate independent noise (e.g., from a normal
distribution), such that the real distribution is still recoverable [24].

As prerequisites for training, we need the full Framingham dataset and the adjacency matrix,
which gives the causal structure. The data columns are reordered to strictly match the variables
ordering defined by the adjacency matrix of each discovered graph. This alignment between
the full dataset and the adjacency matrix is needed, as it allows the causal flow to correctly
map each variable to its causal parents during the learning of the structural equations. Since
we have 100 different graphs, the order might be changing so we adapt this for each loop and
graph.


4.3.2   Generative Model Selection and Training

We use CausalFlows [119], a Python package that implements Causal Normalizing Flows in
PyTorch. Currently, it is essentially a wrapper of the Zuko library [120]. Concretely, the
CausalNSF class creates a causal flow using a Neural Spline Flow (NSF) [25] as the base model.

The key advantage of using causal flows is their ability to learn the SCM directly from obser-
vational data, given a causal graph, and then use this learned model to answer interventional
queries. The theory of this arquitecture was explained in detailed in Chapter 2.

Our integration design respects the causal structure discovered by the LLM. To achieve this, the
adjacency matrix obtained from the causal discovery process is used to constrain the CausalNSF
architecture. Specifically, the structure of the flow is configured such that each variable xi is
modeled as a function of only its causal parents pa(i), as defined by the adjacency matrix.

Since our HallBayes framework produces probabilistic graphs rather than a single deterministic
output, to handle structural uncertainty, we train a separate CausalNSF model for each of the
100 graphs sampled via Monte Carlo methods. This approach allows us to propagate uncertainty
from the discovery phase through to the inference phase, providing not just point estimates but
distributions over causal effects that account for structural uncertainty.

The training orchestrates the training of multiple CausalNSF models in parallel. The pipeline
incorporates several best practices for training deep generative models:

Data Splitting Strategy: We use a three-way stratified split: Training (80%), Validation
                                                                                              58


(10%), and Test (10%). Due to the significant class imbalance (in diabetes) in our dataset, we
stratify on the diabetes mellitus variable to mantain an identical class distribution across all
subsets. The validation set is explicitly used to monitor convergence and trigger early stopping
during the learning process. The Test set is held out from the training loop.

Model Architecture: The configuration was selected based on empirical experimentation,
and finalized with the following architecture configuration. It balances model complexity with
computational efficiency, allowing for the capture of complex causal relationships while avoiding
overfitting.


   • Flow Type: CausalNSF with Neural Spline transformers

   • Hidden dimensions: Two hidden layers with 64 units each [64, 64]

   • Batch dize: 128 samples per batch

   • Learning rate: 5e-4 with Adam optimizer

   • Maximum epochs: 2000 (with early stopping)


Training objective: The models are trained by maximizing the negative log-likelihood of the
observed data under the flow distribution, as detailed in the theory of causal models.

Training is optimized using the Adam optimizer, which computes gradients via backpropagation
through the flow architecture.

To prevent overfitting and reduce unnecessary computation, we implemented an early stopping
mechanism based on the performance of the validation set. This strategy reduces training time
while ensuring optimal generalization performance.


   • Patience: 100 epochs (stop if there is no improvement for 100 consecutive epochs).

   • Minimum delta: 1e-4 (the minimum improvement in validation NLL required to be con-
     sidered progress).

   • Best Model Checkpoint: When early stopping is triggered, the model state from the epoch
     with the best validation NLL.


4.3.3   Experimental Design

To ensure statistical consistency and fair comparison with the 100 LLM-based causal graphs,
the experiment execution is designed to train four distinct categories of models (100 models
per category, for a total of 400 models). This approach enables us to compare the distributions
of causal effects rather than single point estimates, ensuring both structural uncertainty and
training variance.

The four model categories are defined as follows:

1. Ground Truth Graph Models (100 runs): To establish a fair baseline, we train 100
separate instances of the flow models using the expert-validated Framingham Heart Study causal
graph, our ground truth graph. Repeating the training 100 times ensures that the comparison
                                                                                                  59


with the LLM models captures the variance in neural network training, preventing a comparison
between a distribution of graphs and a single ground truth point.

2. LLM-based Graphs Models (100 runs): We train one causal flow model for each of the
100 LLM-based causal graphs generated by our Causal Discovery pipeline.

3. No-Graph with Ground Truth Order Models (100 runs): We also train 100 causal
flow models using the correct topological order derived from the Ground Truth Graph, but
without enforcing the adjacency matrix.

Theoretically, as formalized by Khemakhem et al. (2021) [121], given the correct causal ordering
π, the structural equations can be rewritten as functions of all predecessors xi = fi (x<π(i) , ni ).
With infinite data, this setup is sufficient for the causal flow to learn the underlying causal
mechanism. However, in practice, since data is finite, enforcing the explicit adjacency matrix is
necessary to ensure robust learning.

It is important to outline that providing the correct variable ordering constitutes a massive
informational advantage. In the field of Causal Discovery, identifying the correct topological
sort is the most computationally challenging step due to the factorial search space of possi-
ble permutations (d!). By providing the Ground Truth order, we are effectively solving the
“directionality” of all causal relationships for the model a priori.

4. No-Graph with Random Order Models (100 runs): Since the used architectures
require a sequential variable ordering as input, we assign a random permutation of variables
for each run without imposing any adjacency matrix constraints. This serves to measure model
performance when no causal knowledge is available.

Conversely to Point 3, random order places the model at some disadvantage. A random order
may result in a model that struggles to even approximate the true observational distribution;
in some cases, falling outside the observational space. However, since assuming access to the
Ground Truth order is unrealistic in real-world tasks, we include this “worst-case” scenario
to provide a complete and fair lower bound. To be fair and consistent, the utility of all four
alternatives (Points 1–4) is evaluated using the same causal generative model architecture.

Parallelization: Due to the computational demand of training over 400 distinct models, we
used parallel processing to speed up the training. Using the joblib library [122] with 13 workers,
enabled us to train multiple models simultaneously on different CPU cores. Since each model
trains independently, they can be processed in parallel without conflict. Once a model finishes
training, we immediately save it to disk, preparing it for subsequent intervention experiments.

Detailed code methods of the training implementation, including data loading, preprocessing,
splitting, normalization, training loop, early stopping, model evaluation, parallelization, and
model persistence, are provided in Annex D (Section D.4).


4.4   Causal inference Pipeline Evaluation

Once the Causal Inference pipeline is implemented, the final stage of the methodology involves
a practical validation of the discovered causal structures. While the metrics employed in the
Causal Discovery Evaluation (Section 4.2) measure structural similarity and accuracy, evaluat-
ing the pipeline through interventions determines if the discovered graphs can accurately predict
the effects of real-world changes in medicine. The evaluation is conducted by performing in-
terventions on the generative models and comparing the resulting Average Treatment Effects
                                                                                                  60


(ATE) against those produced by the Ground Truth (GT) model. Since these effects are ex-
pressed as intervals to account for uncertainty, the primary metric for this evaluation is the
Intersection over Union (IoU), which quantifies the degree of alignment between the estimated
and true ATE intervals.


4.4.1     Causal Interventions (ATE)

After training, the causal flow models can be used to answer interventional queries of the form
P (Y | do(X = x)). To estimate the Average Treatment Effect (ATE), we apply the theoretical
concept of intervention defined in the Theoretical Foundations Chapter. The ATE is formally
defined as:


                            AT E = E[Y | do(T = 1)] − E[Y | do(T = 0)]                          (21)

The do-operator is implemented by physically intervening on the generative process, specifically,
during sampling. We leverage the bidirectional nature of the causal flows, therefore, for an
intervention on variable Xi , we follow these steps:


  1. Standardize Intervention Values: Since the model was trained on standardized data,
     the intervention values (e.g., BMI = 30) must first be transformed using the Standard-
     Scaler parameters to obtain the corresponding standardized values x∗ .

  2. Generate Samples: To simulate the intervention do(X = x∗ ), for each intervention
     value (xlow and xhigh ), a batch of samples (10,000) is generated. As a generative model,
     the Causal Flows can directly generate observational and interventional distributions by
     drawing samples from the learned structural equations. When generating interventional
     samples, the value of the intervention variable Xi is overridden, forcing it to x∗ regardless
     of its parents’ values [24].

        and interventional distibutions.

  3. Inverse Transformation: Once the data samples are generated, we extract the outcome
     variable Y and apply the inverse standard scaling to map the values back to their original
     clinical scale.

  4. Effect Estimation: For each of the N = 100 trained causal flow models, we compute the

A key advantage of training multiple models (one per LLM-discovered graph) is that we

uncertainty of the causal structure in our results. Once we have our list of ATE intervals,
we can define some metrics.


4.4.2   CI Evaluation Metrics Description (IoU)

To quantify the similarity between the causal effect estimates, we introduce the Intersec-
tion over Union (IoU) metric.
The IoU is a standard evaluation metric in Computer Vision, such as in object detec-
tion, [123], it is often used to measure the overlap between predicted and ground-truth
bounding boxes. Here, it is adapted to quantify the alignment between average treat-
ment effect (ATE) intervals in Causal Inference. This metric was selected because, if
the LLM-estimated graphs accurately capture the underlying causal mechanisms, the re-
sulting interventional predictions should produce ATE intervals that significantly overlap
with those derived from the ground truth model. Calculating the IoU of these intervals
provides a continuous measure of how well the ATEs are estimated with some uncertainty,
moving beyond point-estimate comparisons.
Since each model category produces a distribution of ATE values (from 100 trained in-
stances), we first construct confidence intervals for the ATE distributions for each model
category and for a given confidence level (typically 95%). For the estimated models (e.g.,
LLM-based graphs models), the interval is defined as:

                                    CIestimated = [le , ue ]                          (23)
where le is the 2.5th percentile and ue is the 97.5th percentile of the ATE distribution.
The ground-truth interval (CIground truth = [lg , ug ]) is defined analogously.
The IoU metric is then defined as the ratio of the intersection to the union of these two
intervals:

                         Intersection   max(0, min(ue , ug ) − max(le , lg ))
                 IoU =                =                                               (24)
                            Union          max(ue , ug ) − min(le , lg )

Therefore, the Intersection over Union (IoU) quantifies the degree of alignment between
the estimated and true ATE intervals. For each intervention-outcome pair, we compute
the IoU metric across all the categories:

(a) LLM-based Graphs vs. Ground Truth: Directly measures how well the discovered
    graphs flow models perform.
(b) No-Graph with Random Order vs. Ground Truth.
 (c) No-Graph with Ground Truth Order vs. Ground Truth.

The IoU metric has several desirable properties for our evaluation. Unlike metrics such
as coverage, which only consider whether the true ATE samples lies within the estimated
bounds, but does not account for the size of the interval, an excessively wide interval may
achieve high coverage while providing little informative value. The IoU metric addresses
                                                                                      62


this limitation by explicitly penalizing large interval lengths through the union term,
while simultaneously penalizing samples that fall outside the predicted interval via the
intersection term. Moreover, this metric is easy to be interpreted and visualize since it
ranges from 0 to 1. As illustrated in the scenarios in Figure 22, an IoU of 1 indicates
perfect alignment, whereas 0 means no overlap.

distributions that closely match the true causal mechanism. This would suggest that
models trained with LLMs’ causal graphs can successfully estimate causal effects in a
manner similar to that of the real graph.

LLM vs. Traditional Causal Discovery: The PC algorithm demonstrates significantly
lower performance across all metrics, most notably in Recall (20%) compared to LLM-
based methods (93% baseline, 61% probabilistic), where it failed to identify 12 of the 15
true causal relationships. Its high nSHD (0.310) likely stems from a reliance on conditional
independence tests, which struggle with complex confounding patterns in medical data,
non-linear relationships, and measurement noise in real-world health records (like this
one). In contrast, LLM-based methods leverage domain knowledge encoded during pre-
training or retrieved during RAG, to identify plausible causal pathways even with limited
data, achieving 3× higher recall while maintaining better precision (70-73% vs. 60%).
Edge Directionality: A critical distinction emerges in edge directionality; LLM-based
methods achieve perfect causal orientation with zero edge reversals (0 relationships in-
correctly oriented), while the PC algorithm shows 1 of 5 predicted relationships with re-
versed direction (20% reversal rate). This demonstrates the LLM’s superior understanding
of causal directionality, which is essential for clinical applications where the direction of
causality determines intervention targets.
Deterministic LLM vs Probabilistic LLM: While the deterministic LLM achieves the
lowest nSHD (0.167) and highest recall (93%), it predicts a substantial number of false
positive edges (FP = 6), reflecting hallucinated relationships not present in the ground
truth. Introducing probabilistic constraints through HallBayes reduces these spurious
edges to 3.4 on average (43% reduction), improving precision (0.726) at the cost of slightly
lower recall (0.612) and a modest increase in nSHD (0.221 ± 0.030). This trade-off favors
more conservative, reliable causal discovery.
The conservative nature of the probabilistic approach (FN = 5.8 > FP = 3.4) reflects
the HallBayes refinement method’s preference to exclude uncertain edges rather than risk
false relationships in clinical contexts. Despite having slightly lower accuracy (81.1% vs.
85.7% for baseline), the probabilistic method provides distributional information unavail-
able in deterministic baselines: confidence intervals, edge probability distributions, and
uncertainty quantification for each causal relationship. This enables sensitivity analyses
(e.g., “use only edges with probability > 0.7”) and explainable decision-making, answering
the critical question that deterministic methods cannot address: “How confident should
we be in this causal relationship?”

5.3   Limitations

While this study successfully demonstrates an end-to-end workflow for automating causal
discovery and causal inference, several limitations were identified during the experimental
and validation phases.
Computational Scalability
As evidenced in the results, introducing the RAG pipeline increased the processing time
per variable. Since our pairwise approach scales quadratically (O(N 2 )) with the number
of variables, this method becomes computationally expensive for large-scale causal graphs
with dozens or hundreds of nodes.
While effective for our 7-variable use case, the current architecture requires optimization,
such as pre-indexing the vector database rather than performing real-time retrieval to be
viable for high-dimensional biological networks. Furthermore, our experiments observed
that the performance difference between the selected model GPT-4.1 and the efficient
gpt-4o-mini was not that significant for this specific task. Therefore, for systems requiring
scalability to larger variable sets, we recommend utilizing gpt-4o-mini and Method 1 of
our framework to significantly reduce latency and cost without compromising accuracy.
LLM Hallucinations and Uncertainty Calibration
Since our workflow relies fundamentally on LLMs, it inherits their native limitations, most
notably the risk of hallucinations. While we implemented several strategies to mitigate
this, precise hallucination detection remains an open challenge in the state-of-the-art.
Our analysis of uncertainty quantification revealed distinct trade-offs. As noted in the
results, raw token probabilities exhibited systematic overconfidence. Due to RLHF, models
tend to assign high probability mass to their outputs even when incorrect, making logits
an unreliable sole metric for calibration.
Conversely, the HallBayes method, while offering improved reliability over logits, erred on
the side of being too strict. Although it effectively filtered hallucinations, it introduced
a trade-off in recall by discarding valid edges that the model was uncertain about. This
structural loss directly impacted downstream Causal Inference performance for those spe-
cific variables. This demonstrates that the pipeline is highly sensitive to false negatives
generated during the discovery phase; missing a single edge can disrupt the generative
model’s ability to propagate effects correctly.
Hidden confounders
Finally, our current model assumes causal sufficiency, meaning it operates under the hy-
pothesis that there are no external unobserved hidden confounders influencing the system.
In complex biomedical contexts, this is a strong assumption, as genetic, environmental,
or psychosocial factors not present in the dataset may affect the observed variables. In
particular, psychological and social factors are frequently excluded in causal inference
studies within medicine, including randomized controlled trials. Although these factors
are difficult to quantify, we believe they can act as strong confounders in real-world clin-
ical settings, and their influence may be substantial. The current pipeline does not yet
account for such latent variables, highlighting a key limitation in the model’s applicability
to complex healthcare scenarios.
Some of these challenges are addressed through proposed actions in Future Work (Section
6.2).

                        6. Conclusions and Future Lines

6.1   Conclusions

Implementation of a Generalizable End-to-End Causal Inference Pipeline: In-
tegrating LLM-based CD Framework with Causal Generative Models
The results show initial evidence that the integration of LLM reasoning with causal gen-
erative models offers tangible advantages. First, LLM reasoning augmented with acces-
sible knowledge for Causal Discovery (CD) achieves higher performance than traditional
methods such as the PC algorithm on real world datasets. Specifically, LLM-based meth-
ods achieved a three times higher recall (93.3% vs 20.0%) while maintaining superior
precision compared to the PC baseline in the Framingham Heart Study. LLMs act as
“meta-experts” capable of aggregating vast domain knowledge. These findings highlight
the promise of LLM-based causal discovery while pointing to future research directions:
enhancing reproducibility across models and extending evaluation to a broader range of
datasets to assess generalizability.
However, LLMs often struggle with precise quantitative estimation. Conversely, causal
generative models (specifically causal flows) require a causal structure but then provide
rigorous mathematical logic for interventions. Our pipeline successfully bridges this gap,
showing that models trained on LLM-derived graphs produce accurate Average Treatment
Effect (ATE) estimates and significantly outperform models lacking causal structure (even
those given the correct topological ordering) achieving a Mean IoU of 0.723 and a mode
of 0.825 which represents significant overlap with models trained with the ground truth
graph. This hybrid pipeline demonstrates that the future of automated causal analysis
lies not in standalone AI systems, but in carefully orchestrated workflows that combine
language understanding, statistical learning, and causal theory.
Furthermore, the modular architecture of this framework ensures that the pipeline is
extensive to other contexts beyond the biomedical domain, as cross-industry generalization
was a primary goal intended in its design.
How ‘Extra Edges’ Question Simplification: Uncovering Complexity
Building upon the technical results, this work reveals a profound but delicate insight:
the LLMs’ “false positives” lead us to critically examine the nature of the “ground truth”
itself. While the expert-defined graph is the gold standard method in causal discovery, it is
sometimes questioned in the literature as a simplified model of reality (“expert knowledge
is often incomplete or inconsistent, especially when multiple experts provide divergent
views on variable relationships”) [126], [127]. It represents a concise summary of well-
established knowledge, frequently emphasizing interpretability and dominant pathways,
but it is possible that an expert omitted an edge because the strength of the relationship
is marginal and not dominant.
When the LLM suggests a direct edge from ‘BMI’ to ‘major acute cardiovascular event’,
bypassing the mediator ‘diabetes’, we may question that it is not merely hallucinating.
Biologically, BMI influences cardiovascular risk through a variety of mechanisms: systemic
inflammation, hypertension. . . Many of these operate in parallel to diabetic pathways. By
aggregating literature, the LLM may be exposing these alternative, complex, or overlap-
ping causal routes that the simplified ground truth graph consolidates into a single, clean
path. This does not invalidate the expert model but it does challenge its simplicity. It
suggests that our probabilistic graphs may reveal the tensions and complexities that are
obscured in causal diagrams. Ultimately, the choice is not between the LLM’s graph and
the expert’s graph, but rather an informed dialogue between them.

A Blueprint for the Future of Causal AI
This thesis concludes that the native human desire to understand the “why” can now be
significantly augmented. By integrating the world knowledge of LLMs with the mathe-
matical foundations of causal inference, we build a practical bridge from textual knowledge
to testable causal models. The work provides a reproducible, extensible framework that
lowers the barrier to causal analysis in specialized domains.
Looking forward, the future of causal AI lies in hybrid systems, rather than purely LLM-
driven ones. Our pipeline offers a concrete blueprint, demonstrating how to leverage the
transformative potential of LLMs while designing mitigations against their limitations.
Automation is Possible, but Human-in-the-Loop Validation Remains Critical
The work demonstrates that while significant automation is achievable (the pipeline is
technically possible), full automation is neither feasible nor desirable for highly sensi-
tive domains such as healthcare. The probabilistic graphs produced are not final truths,
but high-quality initial hypotheses. They solve the “cold start” problem, providing a
structural hypothesis, on which researchers can use and integrate in their work, such as
posterior causal algorithms. It reframes the researcher’s role from builder to validator
and interpreter. Thus, the ultimate role of this workflow is to augment and acceler-
ate the human expert. It provides an initial phase for expert interrogation: highlighting
high-confidence edges, flagging low-confidence or novel edges for further investigation, and
enabling rapid simulation of interventional scenarios. The final validation, interpretation,
and decision-making responsibility remains firmly with the clinician or researcher.


6.2   Future Lines

Hybrid Causal Discovery Algorithms
Not only to address the computational scalability limitation of the O(N 2 ) complexity of
pairwise LLM queries, but also to align with emerging state-of-the-art methodologies, fu-
ture work should explore hybrid approaches. Recent literature [128, 15] has systematically
examined how LLMs can be effectively integrated with statistical causal discovery meth-
ods, demonstrating that such hybrid architectures obtain good accuracy while preserving
computational efficiency. These methods aim to combine the semantic reasoning capabil-
ities of LLMs with the statistical rigor of traditional causal discovery algorithms. This
synergy can be implemented bi-directionally to leverage the strengths of both paradigms.
In an LLM-guided search approach, the model is utilized first to generate structural pri-
ors, defining constraints such as “required edges” based on domain knowledge. These
constraints then narrow the search space for statistical algorithms, improving both ef-
ficiency and accuracy on high-dimensional datasets. Conversely, a refinement approach
could employ statistical methods to first discover an initial graph skeleton from the data,
which is subsequently processed by the LLM to resolve undirected edges or correct spu-
rious correlations using semantic context. This combination would merge the speed of
statistical methods with the semantic understanding of LLMs.
Advanced Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
For concrete use cases in one specific domain, to reduce system latency and improve the
recall of relevant scientific evidence, future implementations should move from real-time
indexing to pre-indexing specific medical sub-domains. Furthermore, the pipeline could
be enhanced with Advanced RAG components. Implementing a Reranker would re-order
retrieved documents by relevance before they reach the LLM, ensuring the model focuses
on the most critical evidence. Additionally, Contextual Compression techniques could be
                                                                                        82


employed to summarize retrieved text, reducing the token count and noise in the context
window.
Handling Hidden Confounders with Deconfounding Models
Our current workflow operates under the assumption of causal sufficiency, presuming that
no unobserved hidden confounders influence the system. In complex biomedical contexts,
this is rarely true. A critical next step is to integrate Deconfounding Causal Models, such
as DeCaFlow [23], into the pipeline. This would create a system where the LLM can
qualitatively suggest the existence of latent variables (e.g., “Genetic Predisposition” or
“Environmental Stress”) based on literature, even if they are missing from the dataset.
The generative model could then mathematically model these latent confounders (Z),
allowing for more robust causal effect estimation in the presence of unobserved common
causes.
Robust Uncertainty Quantification
To further mitigate hallucinations and improve the reliability of the LLM-based discovered
graphs, future research could extend the uncertainty quantification framework. In this
work, we deliberately avoided standard “LLM-as-a-Judge” evaluation procedures [129],
which are increasingly common but suffer from the native limitation of validating one
language model’s output using another LLM, which could be another potentially biased
model. Instead, we opted for the HallBayes framework to provide a grounded numerical
measure of uncertainty based on information consistency.
Building on this, future iterations could explore ensemble approaches, where multiple
LLM instances (potentially assigned different “expert personas”) independently evaluate
causal pairs. The consensus among these is usually measured using statistical metrics
such as Krippendorff’s Alpha [129]. High inter-rater agreement would serve as a stronger,
external signal of validity than individual confidence scores.
Benchmarking and Generalization
While this study validated the methodology on the Tübingen benchmark and the Fram-
ingham Heart Study, extending the evaluation to diverse datasets is essential. Future
experiments should test the pipeline on datasets from non-medical domains (e.g., eco-
nomics, climate science). This would help determine if the conclusions deliberated and
observed here hold consistent across different types of causal structures and data distri-
butions.
Open Source Contribution
Finally, consistent with the open-science nature of this project, we aim to contribute
our developments back to the community. Specifically, we plan to submit the PubMed
integration and the RAG-based enhancements developed in this work to the PyWhy-
LLM repository. Integrating our AugmentedModelSuggester enhanced class into the core
library would allow other researchers to easily leverage biomedical literature for causal
discovery.

Appendices

       A. Ethical, economic, social and environmental aspects


A.1    Introduction

This appendix offers an opportunity to reflect on the potential societal, economic and
environmental impact of this work. It focuses on how the proposed framework fits into
the current medical and AI ecosystems, and on the responsibility of researchers and the
sustainability of technology.


A.2    Ethical, economic, social and environmental aspects

Ethical Aspects

  • Hallucination Risks: A central ethical concern is the tendency of LLMs to generate
    “hallucinations”. In a medical context, an incorrect causal link could affect the
    estimation of the effect on patient treatment. The implementation of hallucination
    mitigation techniques was specifically designed as an ethical safeguard to quantify
    this risk and provide a bounded measure of uncertainty.
  • Explainability: Although LLMs are considered black box systems, in this work
    we try to prioritize transparency by preserving the LLM’s intermediate reasoning.
    This ensures that causal assertions are traceable and can be interrogated by clinical
    experts.

Consequently, this research concludes that technical feasibility must be governed by an
ethical imperative for human-in-the-loop oversight. This hierarchy ensures that the clin-
ician retains the ethical ownership of the outcome, using AI-generated causal links as
supportive evidence rather than definitive commands.
Economic Aspects
Any research capable of identifying treatments for a disease more swiftly and effectively
can mitigate the impact of that pathology on both public health and the global economy,
reducing operational costs and improving patients’ labor productivity.
Regarding the project cost, when leveraging LLM’s APIs, the study evaluated different
models based on their pricing structures. Gpt-4o-mini was identified as a highly cost-
efficient option for almost all the experimentation, costing significantly less than frontier
models while maintaining competitive accuracy.
Social Aspects
Causal inference allows for a more accurate evaluation of treatment efficacy. Socially,
this translates to more reliable preventive protocols and better patient-specific treatment
plans. It also allows for a transition from simple pattern recognition to reasoning about
“what would happen” under specific interventions. This capability is essential for guiding
critical decisions in public health and social sciences, leading to more effective clinical
strategies.
Moreover, the framework reframes the researcher’s role from a manual builder of causal
structures to a validator and interpreter, which accelerates scientific discovery.
                                                                                         91


Environmental Aspects
The environmental impact of AI is directly related to the computational power required
for inference. The thesis advocates for the use of smaller models (like the “mini” or
“flash” families) which require less energy to run than frontier-scale models with trillions
of parameters. Beyond the immediate energy consumption of the software, the application
of causal inference offers a secondary environmental benefit, finding an optimal treatment
for a disease can reduce drug wastage due to overuse of drugs.


A.3    Conclusion

Integrating LLMs with causal theory represents a significant advance in this area of re-
search. This project demonstrates the importance of balancing technical success with
ethical and sustainable rigour. We believe that this work will predominantly have a posi-
tive ethical, social and environmental impact.

    D.2     Uncertainty Quantification Methods

    This section provides a summary of the code methods for uncertainty quantification,
    covering confidence scores, token probability extraction, and the HallBayes refinement

    D.2.1    Confidence Score Implementation

    In the baseline method, the confidence extraction logic was implemented directly in the
    Suggesters component. In contrast, the Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) approach
    uses the Augmented Suggesters, and invokes the query llm function (adapted from the
    PyWhyLLM library) to perform its inference. We modified both the core Suggesters logic
    and the query llm function to support the extraction of self-reported confidence scores
    via structured prompting.
                                                                                             102


     The example illustrates the modification of the query llm function for the RAG method
     (the Suggesters class follows a identical logic):
     def query_llm(variable1, variable2, source_text=None,
                   retriever=None, llm=None, return_prompt=False,
                   confidence_level=False):


     If confidence level=True, the system prompt described previously is appended with the
     confidence instruction block. The LLM’s response is parsed using Regex to extract:
     <answer>: The causal classification (A, B, or C) and in this case also the <confidence>:
     The scalar value (0.0 - 1.0). It returns a structured object containing both the causal
     claim and its associated metadata.


     D.2.2    Token Log-Probability Extraction

     The logprobs parameter of the OpenAI API’s is used to access the raw token probabilities.
     We reconstruct the text to find the exact token that corresponds to the final classification
     (A, B, or C) token and extract its log-probability.
     For the implementation, we first execute a completion request to the OpenAI API with
     the logprobs parameter enabled. Next, we parse the model’s text response to identify
     the chosen answer (using Regex to find the content within <answer> tags) and locate that
     specific token in the response metadata to extract its exact log-probability value.
 1   # 1. OpenAI API call with logprobs enabled
 2   response = openai_client.chat.completions.create(
 3       model=model_name,
 4       messages=[
 5           {"role": "system",
 6            "content": "You␣are␣a␣helpful␣assistant␣for␣causal␣reasoning."},
 7           {"role": "user", "content": prompt}
 8       ],
 9       temperature=0.0,    # Deterministic output
10       logprobs=True,      # Enable token probability extraction
11       top_logprobs=3,     # Capture top 3 alternatives
12       max_tokens=200
13   )
14
15   # 2. Parse the text content to find the model’s final decision
16   text_response = response.choices[0].message.content
17   match = re.search(r"<answer>(.*?)</answer>", text_response)
18   final_decision = match.group(1).strip() if match else None
19
20   # 3. Extract the log-probability for that specific token
21   logprobs_data = response.choices[0].logprobs.content
22   answer_token_logprob = None
23
24   if final_decision:
25       for token_item in logprobs_data:
26           if token_item.token.strip() == final_decision:
27               answer_token_logprob = token_item.logprob
28               break



     D.2.3    HallBayes Framework Implementation

     We use the OpenAIPlanner to orchestrate the semantic masking process. The system
     generates multiple masked versions of the prompt to test if the model’s answer relies on
                                                                                             103


     specific entities or generic patterns. It generates prompts where entities are progressively
     removed.
 1   from hallbayes import OpenAIPlanner, OpenAIItem, OpenAIBackend
 2
 3   # 1. Initialize HallBayes backend
 4   hallbayes_backend = OpenAIBackend(
 5       model="gpt-4o-mini",
 6       api_key=os.getenv("OPENAI_API_KEY")
 7   )
 8
 9   # 2. Create validation prompt for suggested relationships
10   validation_prompt = f"""
11   Medical␣Knowledge␣Assessment:
12
13   Claim:␣"{variable1}"␣causes␣"{variable2}"
14
15   Question:␣Based␣on␣established␣medical␣and␣scientific␣knowledge,
16   is␣this␣causal␣relationship␣scientifically␣valid?
17
18   Answer:␣Yes/No␣with␣brief␣justification.
19   """
20
21   # 3. Configure refinement item
22   item = OpenAIItem(
23       prompt=validation_prompt,
24       answer="Yes",
25       validation_answer="Yes",
26       skeleton_policy="closed_book"
27   )
28
29   # 4. Execute HallBayes evaluation
30   planner = OpenAIPlanner(hallbayes_backend, temperature=0.3)
31   result = planner.plan(item)
32
33   # 5. Extract risk metrics
34   RoH = result.hallucination_risk     # Risk in [0, 1]
35   edge_probability = 1 - RoH          # P(X -> Y)
