        (iv) Complicate the prompts, by generating more complex diagrams, e.g. by using computer code.
Since DEF-1 can also be programmed, this could, in principle, lead to a systematic (benchmark) test for
this type of causal reasoning. Our preliminary tests suggest that the causal reasoning of existing LLMs
breaks down under sufficient diagram complexity. Perhaps it is interesting, notably in an interdisciplinary
effort with computer scientists, to do a systematic study of the test score of an AI as a function of diagram
complexity. For diagram complexity several numerical measures could be defined; the simplest ones are
the number of neurons and the number of columns, i.e. time steps, in the diagram; others are the number
of forks, of blocked neurons, of crossings, etc. In the most interesting scenario this would be an easy,
quantitative way to get insight into the general depth-of-reasoning of given AIs. For assessing an AI’s
answers in the case of complex diagrams, an encompassing definition is necessary, one that can be
programmed, such as DEF-1.


5. Conclusion


        In the AI community there is a popular belief that making AI ‘think causally’ might be a key
paradigm for its future development, and perhaps even for reaching AGI (e.g. Pearl 2019, Zečević et al.
2023); a belief also held by philosophers (Buckner 2024, p 74). Several types of causal reasoning tests
have been developed by computer scientists and others, in order to monitor and quantify the progress. At
the same time, defining the concept of cause is an eminently philosophical topic. Even alone for this
reason, an investigation of possible cross-disciplinary fertilization seems in place. Here we proposed a
method for testing abstract causal reasoning that is directly inspired by philosophical expertise, gathered
since about fifty years (compiled in Paul and Hall 2013), and condensed in neuron diagrams. We showed
that the method can well be applied to LLMs as ChatGPT, DeepSeek and Gemini 2.0 Flash. We noted
that these LLMs can sometimes give correct causal verdicts in scenarios the interpretation of which is
debated in the literature (e.g. Table 2). Now, in order to evaluate results reliably and possibly automatically
by a computer algorithm, the method requires an objective standard to compare the AI’s answers to. Hence
