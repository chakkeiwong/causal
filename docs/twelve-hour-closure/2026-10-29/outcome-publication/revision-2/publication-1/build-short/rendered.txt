Chapter 1

Buying the information that missing
customers take away

A bank cannot recover the experience of customers who disappear merely by fitting a more
elaborate model to those who remain. It can sometimes change the evidence. A sample
of incomplete customer histories may be recovered from archived servicing records, linked
payments or carefully conducted follow-up. The economic question is how much reliable
information that extra work buys.
  This example continues a generated servicing comparison in which dropout depends on an
unseen future shock. Resampling a fitted transition model can reproduce that selection error
and produce misleadingly narrow intervals. Random recovery repairs the missing-information
problem under a clear condition: the histories actually recovered must form the specified
random sample. Random invitations are insufficient if successful recovery remains selective.
Even a correct estimate can be too imprecise to support a decision. Here a separately fitted
outcome model reduces interval width substantially, but the additional pilot information is
costly and a positive policy improvement often remains statistically unresolved.


1.1     A known design, applied to a sequential decision
Following up a subsample with missing outcomes is usually called double sampling. Levis and
colleagues show how noninformative successful second-stage recovery identifies the full-data
distribution despite informative original missingness. Their Proposition 1 treats a missing
outcome; Appendix A extends the argument to general coarsening of a record. Their mean-
effect estimators also use regression to improve efficiency under explicit conditions (Levis et al.,
2023).
  This changes the information without exempting the analysis from selection assumptions.
Levis and colleagues distinguish invitations from obtaining the missing information, and discuss
nonresponse and recall error. Sun and colleagues develop a related construction for weighted
quantile treatment effects, with conditions appropriate to quantiles (Sun et al., 2025). A
mean-reward comparison does not thereby qualify a tail-loss estimate. Averaging conditional
quantile effects does not in general recover a population quantile effect.
   The present contribution is a worked composition: recover missing pieces of a sequential
policy-value score, derive the precision consequence, and test it in the servicing law. The
two-phase idea and sequential regression assistance are inherited methods. This does not
establish an efficient estimator for every longitudinal missing-data model.

1.7     What this changes in the proposal
A prototype can now be proposed around a specific design: accept declared recovery proba-
bilities, retain the initial customer denominator, estimate paired policy improvements, and
display the extra information burden and remaining uncertainty. It should distinguish dropout
weights from recovery weights and show when an outcome model improves only precision.
Where recoverable histories do not exist, sensitivity or partial-identification analysis is the
appropriate next step.
   These results concern independent generated customers, known action probabilities and
fixed policies. They do not settle calendar dependence, unmeasured treatment confounding,
erroneous or partially recovered histories, estimated treatment probabilities, or uncertainty
after policy learning. Normal intervals are assessed rather than declared exact. The simulation
keeps every unaudited missing value unavailable to fitting and estimation. The generated
evidence does not establish that real histories can be recovered accurately, or that the collection
expense is worthwhile.
   The remaining question is whether a feasible collection design can deliver accurate histories
in sufficient numbers to separate the economic alternatives at an acceptable total cost.
