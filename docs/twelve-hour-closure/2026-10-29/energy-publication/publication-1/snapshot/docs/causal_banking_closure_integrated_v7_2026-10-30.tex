\label{reading:part-decision}

The first task is to define the action, the alternative and the financial outcome.
Identification then determines what the data and assumptions can reveal;
estimation measures that quantity and its uncertainty.

\chapter{Learning a causal model from observational data}
A discovery method can narrow the plausible economic relationships without
settling the effect of a proposed action. The comparisons here follow that
remaining question through specific implementation choices. A supplied graph
can support a checked adjustment candidate; a learned structural model adds
assumptions about functions and disturbances; hidden borrower circumstances
change the graph information that must be retained. The practical choice
depends on those assumptions as well as the computation.

The first studies compare a restricted pgmpy adjustment route, causal-learn
search and the different maintenance and numerical obligations of CausalNex,
DAGMA and Causica. Native and other-language studies then examine graph
representations, latent-variable methods and the meaning of their scores.
The final bnlearn and LiNGAM comparisons carry the argument into intervention,
counterfactual and bootstrap summaries. Each answers a different part of the
same banking request; successful graph construction alone does not complete
that request. The detailed DAGMA study includes bounded execution of a saved
source module, while the other passages retain their stated source-inspection
and exact-reference evidence scopes.

\chapter{From a learned graph to a defensible calculation}
A graph becomes useful to a bank when its statistical meaning survives
the next calculation. Directed, partially directed and mixed graphs encode
different information. The Tetrad bridge comparison first follows variable
identity, categories and endpoint marks across languages. Weka and Banjo then
show why a predictive factorization or a lagged search still needs a separate
intervention argument. The remaining studies examine calculations under a
supplied model: libDAI distinguishes conditioning from intervention and
numerical stability from accuracy; aGrUM connects identified formulas to
support and counterfactual coupling; OpenGM distinguishes marginal inference
from joint optimization. Together these comparisons ask which part of a
banking request a numerical result actually answers.

\chapter{Learning relationships over time}

A funding decision has a date, an information set and an outcome horizon.
A learned temporal relationship has these attributes only if the observation
and estimation procedure preserve them. Yesterday's rate may predict tomorrow's
balance because the rate changes retention, because both respond to a shared
liquidity shock, or because aggregation conceals their within-day order. The
useful question is what the observed sequence and any intervention information
allow the bank to distinguish.

The first comparisons follow that question through R, Python, Java, native
engines, Julia, Rust and MATLAB. They distinguish temporal restrictions,
structural innovations, supplied dynamic models, predictive information and
changing mechanisms. The detailed Tigramite study examines how statistics,
endpoints, intervention contrasts and resampling can change meaning within
one package. The later studies examine discovery delegates, reinforcement and
attention-based search, evaluation truth and discrete score helpers. They
connect a method's mathematical claim to the inputs and results that an
implementation actually preserves. Throughout, the worked examples keep
monetary consequences visible while distinguishing an exact construction,
a source finding and an executed experiment.

\chapter{Dynamic panels and structural funding shocks}
A deposit response evolves over time and may coincide with changes in
monetary conditions or customer demand. Dynamic-panel moments, restrictions
on structural shocks and state-space forecasts use different sources of
information. A forecast alone does not establish the effect of changing the
bank's policy.

The dynamic-panel foundation first develops moment validity, instrument choice,
calendar identity and selection. The language comparisons then examine how
implementations preserve or change those objects, followed by structural-shock
and state-space alternatives. Several use the same stipulated calendar gaps,
rotations or missing-state laws as matched comparisons; repeating their
arithmetic does not provide independent empirical corroboration.

\chapter{Hidden states and Bayesian comparisons}

An uncertain customer state can be useful for a banking decision only if its
meaning, observation law and action mechanism are specified. This chapter first
examines state filtering and probabilistic model surgery through Dynamax, PyMC
and NumPyro. It then compares the resulting requirements across languages and
commercial engines. The comparison keeps causal identification, parameter and
state uncertainty, predictive variation and numerical approximation distinct.

repetition is not additional empirical evidence about a package or a bank.
