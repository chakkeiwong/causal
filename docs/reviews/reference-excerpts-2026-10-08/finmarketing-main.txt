\chapter*{Prologue}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Prologue}
\markboth{Prologue}{Prologue}

A household buying a home may need a mortgage, somewhere to keep its
remaining savings, and a way to pay for furnishing the property. Within a
bank, these needs may fall to different teams. Each team can make an
apparently sensible offer, yet the combined result depends on how those
offers interact. A deposit campaign may bring in new funds or merely move
money between the bank's accounts. A purchase incentive may increase
spending or subsidize a purchase that would have happened anyway.

These are economic questions as well as prediction problems. A bank needs
to understand how customers choose, what an offer changes, and how the
resulting benefits and costs unfold over time. Vehicle finance, home lending,
payments, investment advice and institutional relationships each require
their own treatment. They also share methods for learning from incomplete
observations, measuring effects and comparing decisions.

This book develops the scientific foundations and design of FinMarketing,
a data-science library accompanied by applications for bank marketing. The
proposed library brings together methods for demand estimation, causal
analysis, customer value, pricing and sequential decisions. Its initial
applications have two purposes: to perform useful banking work and to show
how the underlying components can be combined.

The design also addresses experienced users who build applications of
their own, including those working with coding agents. Reuse requires
components that users can find, understand and adapt. Their assumptions
and financial meaning must remain intelligible as they move from one
application to another.

For analysts, the book explains how to choose and evaluate a method. For
application builders, it connects those methods to concrete banking tasks.
For decision makers, it develops the questions that determine whether an
application is worth adopting. Worked examples using synthetic data make
the calculations explicit and show where the available information leaves
a decision unresolved.
