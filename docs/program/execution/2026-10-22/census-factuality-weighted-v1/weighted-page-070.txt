    Thus, CIw∗ is robust in the sense of simultaneous coverage, with the caveat that inferences
in the two-sided case come at a lower confidence level relative to uniform coverage. In settings
where simultaneous inferences are more relevant than uniform ones, this additional cost may be
worthwhile. To see how, I draw connections to the literature on partial identification by viewing
Λ(θ) as the identified set for an estimand with unknown weights in Λ. From this perspective,
uniform coverage (20) corresponds to coverage of points in the identified set, while simultaneous
coverage (36) corresponds to coverage of the identified set—for precise definitions in a general
partial identification setup, see Molinari (2020, Section 4.3).
    Following Imbens and Manski (2004), coverage of points in the identified set is relevant when
there is a true estimand λ′0 θ. For example, suppose that λ′0 θ represents the ATE for a target
population that one has partial information about and Λ represents the class of populations
consistent with that information. In such cases, the relevant error is the exclusion of λ′0 θ from
CIw∗ , which occurs with probability Pθ {λ′0 θ ∈
                                               / CIw∗ }. Since CIw∗ provides the uniform coverage
in (20), this error rate is uniformly bounded over the potential values of λ0 ∈ Λ.
    Following Henry and Onatski (2012), coverage of the identified set is relevant when there
is concern for robust decision-making. For instance, suppose Λ represents readers λ who will
use CIw∗ to make downstream decisions and the researcher is concerned about the worst-case
loss from those decisions across readers.34 In such cases, Henry and Onatski (2012) show that
the relevant error is the exclusion of any reader’s estimand λ′ θ from CIw∗ , which occurs with
probability Pθ {Λ(θ) ̸⊆ CIw∗ }. The simultaneous coverage in (36) bounds this error rate.
    In summary, the relevant notion of coverage depends on the type of error that one wishes to
mitigate. In my setting, uniform coverage appears most appropriate. For example, a setup with
a partially known target population λ0 from the researcher’s perspective is consistent with the
mapping of my framework to multisite experiments discussed in Section 2.2. Moreover, even in
a decision-making setup where Λ is taken to represent readers, it is plausible that each reader is
only concerned about the exclusion of their own estimand, in which case simultaneous coverage
may be too strong. With these examples in mind, I therefore focus on uniform coverage as the
relevant notion of robust coverage.
