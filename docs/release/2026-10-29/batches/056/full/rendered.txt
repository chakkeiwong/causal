What the bank should buy first

A bank considering a better deposit rate must pay for money it already holds as well as money
the offer attracts. A collections team must account for how today’s contact changes tomorrow’s
information. A discovery algorithm may return several explanations of the same observations,
with opposite implications for a pricing decision. An apparently clean customer table may still
conceal misrecorded treatment or incorrectly linked accounts. These are four different reasons
why a persuasive numerical answer can fail to improve a decision.
   An increase in one account’s recorded balance can be a transfer from another account at
the same bank. The funding outcome must aggregate the intended household and distinguish
new external money from internal movement. Competitor repricing can also change balances
during the same period. A lender’s threshold comparison concerns the applicants affected at
that margin, while a treasury shock analysis must separate the external shock from the bank’s
own response. These economic distinctions determine which comparison is useful before a
statistical model is chosen.
   The proposal is to make a small class of defensible studies easier to conduct and compare.
The first investment should be a comparative integration using established methods, a precise
study description and a report that preserves uncertainty. A capable economist using existing
tools is the reference alternative. The proposed service earns a place only if the completed
study is better or cheaper at equivalent quality. Broad replacement of the bank’s statistical
estate is not justified by the evidence presented here.
   That conclusion follows from the examples, rather than from an assumed advantage of
a common interface. In the constructed offer study, a population balance effect is carried
through sampling uncertainty into annual contribution. The servicing calculation shows why a
correctly fitted regression can still answer the wrong policy question. The graph example gives
identical observed laws with different intervention values. The observation example shows how
additional measurement knowledge can reverse a financial conclusion. Each case tells us what
a service must preserve and what it must decline to infer.
   The evidence has different roles. Established causal results justify inference under stated
assumptions. The project’s exact derivations and generated studies expose target errors,
weak support and unresolved models. Public data reveal realistic timing and measurement
difficulties. Local interface executions establish that particular requests retain their meaning
through particular clients. None of these substitutes for an independently reviewed study on
authentic bank information. Their combined value is to identify a useful and bounded next
experiment before undertaking that more expensive work.

   A bank earns a return by changing a decision, not by observing a correlation. The first
task is to define the action, the alternative and the financial outcome. Identification then
determines what the data and assumptions can reveal; estimation measures that quantity and
its uncertainty.

1.1     More business is not necessarily more value
Consider a deposit offer to a defined group of households. The bank can send the offer
or leave its current terms unchanged. For household i, let Ci (1) be contribution over the
following year if the offer is assigned and Ci (0) contribution over that same year under the
alternative. Contribution includes the agreed funding value, interest expense and servicing
costs. Assignment, delivery, acceptance and the resulting price are distinguished in Chapter 2.
The average assignment effect is
                                    τC = E{Ci (1) − Ci (0)}.
The expectation averages over households, rather than accounts or branches. That choice
prevents a household with several accounts from receiving several votes merely because of the
way the records are stored. The definition would change if management wanted the average
effect per pound of existing deposits. The formal potential-outcome development follows in
Chapter 6.
   A simple financial calculation illustrates why the outcome needs care. For the price rule used
here, assignment delivers the offer and reprices the eligible balance automatically. Suppose
the resulting rate increase attracts an additional average balance ∆B for one year. Let m be
the contribution per pound of that extra balance before the cost of the rate increase, ∆r the
increase in the annual rate, B0 the existing balance that also receives it, and c the direct cost
of the offer. Under the illustrative assumptions that balances remain constant during the year
and that the increase applies to both existing and additional balances, net value is
                                V = m∆B − ∆r(B0 + ∆B) − c                                   (1.1)
                                  = (m − ∆r)∆B − ∆rB0 − c.                                  (1.2)
The first term in the second line is the contribution from additional funding after repricing.
The second is the cost of paying more on balances the bank already had. The last is the direct
cost. A large balance response can still leave V negative. The example omits changes in credit
risk, capital needs, competitor responses and balances within the year; a business case that
depends on those quantities must include them in the outcome definition.

1.2     Selection can imitate an effect
Comparing customers who received an offer with those who did not can mix its effect with the
reasons for targeted delivery. Comparing acceptors with nonacceptors introduces a separate
selection problem. The constructed comparison below concerns receipt, denoted A, and
balances, denoted B. The distinction can be seen without a complicated model. Suppose,
as a constructed example, that half the eligible customers have high underlying demand for
deposits and half have low demand. Their balances without an offer would be £200 and £100
in the units of the example. Assume the offer adds £20 for every customer. If 80% of recipients
are high-demand customers but only 20% of nonrecipients are, observed average balances are

                           E[B | A = 1] = 0.8(220) + 0.2(120) = 200,
                           E[B | A = 0] = 0.2(200) + 0.8(100) = 120.

The observed difference is £80, although the specified effect is £20. The remaining £60 is a
difference in the composition of the two groups. The calculation assumes equally sized recipient
and nonrecipient groups, consistent with the population shares. Random assignment in this
example would make the groups comparable in expectation; an observational analysis needs
an additional argument about demand and treatment selection.
   This is why identification precedes precision. A more accurate estimate of £80 would
not answer the stated question. Nor does predicting balances well reveal which part of the
difference the bank can change. Randomized invitations, eligibility rules, sufficiently rich pre-
offer information and credible structural restrictions offer different ways forward. They identify
different quantities under different assumptions, developed in Chapters 6 and 7; Chapter 2
carries the assigned-offer target into estimation and contribution.

1.4     Uncertainty has an economic consequence
A confidence interval describes sampling uncertainty under stated assumptions. It does not
establish those assumptions. A set of effects compatible with several causal models answers a
different question: how much ambiguity remains even if the observed relationships were known.
Both can affect the decision. Their difference is developed formally in the identification and
inference chapters; it should also remain visible in a management report.
  If the plausible values all favour the same action, greater precision may be worth little.
When the preferred action changes across plausible values, better information may be valuable.
The relevant information could concern customer identity, assignment to an offer, a missing
hardship measure or the financial cost of an intervention. It need not come from a larger sample

1.5     The case for a shared analytical service
The proposal follows from this economic sequence. A common service could help analysts
retain the population, action, information date and financial outcome as a study moves from
formulation to estimation and interpretation. Existing methods should be reused where their
assumptions and capabilities fit. An analysis that finds insufficient information should explain
the missing comparison and, where possible, the measurement or experiment that would resolve
it.
   The value of language assistance must be assessed within that sequence. A clearer formulation
or a useful criticism of a causal diagram can save analyst time. A fluent but unsupported
assumption can make the study less reliable. The comparisons in Chapter 27 concern particular
research tasks and their errors, rather than general impressions of intelligence. Public evidence
can test some of these tasks before confidential bank data are needed.
  The scientific and investment questions meet at the same point: whether a better-supported
decision is worth its cost. The chapters that follow supply the causal arguments, statistical
methods, software comparisons and evidence needed to make that judgment.

2.1     Fix the intervention before estimating its value
The preceding accounting calculation defines the question for the first complete study. Let Zi
denote assignment of the offer to eligible household i, Ri its delivery, Di acceptance, and ri
the paid annual rate. These variables describe successive events, not synonyms. Let Bi (z) be
the household’s average eligible deposit balance over the following year under assignment z,
and let Ci (z) be contribution over that same year. For a population fixed before assignment,
the policy targets are

                      τB = E[Bi (1) − Bi (0)],       τC = E[Ci (1) − Ci (0)].

A statement about the effect among acceptors generally answers neither target. Acceptance
can depend on intentions that also determine balances. Dividing an assignment effect by
an acceptance rate additionally requires an instrumental variable argument and changes the
population to the relevant compliers; the derivation and restrictions are in Chapter 7.
   Here the constructed design makes a narrower intervention explicit. Every assigned offer
is delivered, and assignment automatically reprices the eligible balance for one year. Thus
R = Z and the price rule is r = r0 + Z∆r; acceptance does not gate the payment. A voluntary
activation campaign would need a different expense equation. Assume no interference across
households, no lost outcomes, and unchanged funding margin and direct cost. If the expected
no-offer balance is B0 , the incremental expected contribution is

                                τC = (m − ∆r)τB − ∆rB0 − c.                               (2.1)

To obtain this expression, subtract mBi (0) from (m − ∆r)Bi (1) − c, then take expectations.
It charges the rate increase on the balance that would have remained without the intervention.
The familiar accounting shortcut is justified by this particular price rule. It would overcharge
a campaign that paid only newly acquired funds and would misdescribe a campaign whose
delivery itself failed.
