Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are rapidly being integrated into psychological and behavioral
research as research tools, evaluation targets, human simulators, and cognitive models. Yet
recent evidence reveals severe measurement unreliability: personality assessments degenerate
under factor analysis, moral preferences reverse with punctuation changes, and theory-of-mind
accuracy varies widely with trivial rephrasing. These “measurement phantoms”—statistical
artifacts masquerading as psychological phenomena—threaten the validity of a growing body of
research. Guided by the dual-validity framework that integrates psychometrics with causal
inference, we present a six-stage workflow that scales validity requirements to research
ambition—using LLMs to code text requires basic reliability and accuracy, whereas claims about
psychological properties demand comprehensive construct validation. Researchers must (1)
explicitly define their research goal and corresponding validity requirements, (2) develop and
validate computational instruments through psychometric testing, (3) design experiments that
control for computational confounds, (4) execute protocols transparently, (5) analyze data with
methods appropriate for non-independent observations, and (6) report findings within boundaries
and use results to refine theory. We illustrate the workflow through an example of model
evaluation—“LLM selfhood”—showing how systematic validation can distinguish genuine
computational phenomena from measurement artifacts. By establishing validated computational
instruments and transparent practices, this workflow provides a path toward building a robust
empirical foundation for AI psychology research.

     A validity-guided workflow for robust large language model research in psychology
      Are large language models (LLMs) merely modeling language—or genuinely capturing
human psychology? When Kosinski (2024) reported that GPT-4 solved bespoke false-belief
tasks at the level of a six-year-old child, it sparked speculation that theory-of-mind-like abilities
“may have emerged as an unintended by-product of LLMs’ improving language skills.” Such
provocative claims span diverse psychological domains, including personality traits (Miotto et
al., 2022), moral reasoning (Takemoto, 2024), cognitive biases (Binz & Schulz, 2023), and
emotional intelligence (X. Wang, Li, et al., 2023). Yet recent analyses suggest that many such
findings may instead reflect methodological artifacts—measurement phantoms rather than
genuine psychological phenomena (Ivanova, 2025; Lin, in press; Shapira et al., 2024).
      For instance, the same models that ace Sally-Anne tasks and theory-of-mind tests fail when
scenarios are subtly rephrased (Ullman, 2023) or translated (Sadhu et al., 2024), or when beliefs
must be updated dynamically (Riemer et al., 2025). In one case, GPT-4’s accuracy on an
adversarial false-belief variant evaporates from 97.5% to 0% when the scenario is altered from
an object being placed in a box to an object being placed on a box—revealing reliance on
superficial cues rather than genuine reasoning (Shapira et al., 2024, Figure 2). Likewise,
personality assessments fail to recover their intended multidimensional structure under
psychometric scrutiny—yielding instead arbitrary factors dominated by item-scoring direction
rather than psychological content (Peereboom et al., 2025); and models paradoxically endorse
both “I am an introvert” and “I am an extrovert” (Sühr et al., 2025). Similarly, moral judgments
fluctuate dramatically with trivial formatting alterations, such as changing “Case 1” to “(A)” (Oh
& Demberg, 2025). Such instability violates basic measurement assumptions: reliable
instruments should yield consistent results across equivalent presentations, and valid measures
should capture stable psychological constructs rather than formatting artifacts.
      These systematic violations of psychometric principles (that instruments measure intended
constructs) and causal inference standards (that manipulations test theoretical variables) reveal a
fundamental methodological problem: claims about LLM psychology often stumble forward
without the validity evidence required to support them (Lin, in press; X. Wang, Jiang, et al.,
2023; Q. Wang et al., 2025; Ye, Jin, et al., 2025). Such methodological failures cascade through
the literature, undermining the reproducibility, reliability, and robustness of LLM evaluations
(Laskar et al., 2024) and applications (Lin, 2026).
      Classical psychometric frameworks supply the theory of construct validity (American
Educational Research Association et al., 2014; Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Messick, 1989) but do
not address LLM-specific measurement challenges—prompt sensitivity, non-independence,
temporal instability, and the absence of stable respondent traits. Broad primers on LLMs in
social and behavioral sciences emphasize transparency and appropriateness of use (Abdurahman
et al., 2025; Demszky et al., 2023; Lin, 2026) but do not center a validation sequence tied to
claim type. General reporting standards in psychology (Appelbaum et al., 2018; Psychonomic
Society, 2019) and LLM-specific reporting checklists (Feuerriegel et al., 2026; Gallifant et al.,
2025; Park et al., 2024) standardize disclosure and manuscript content but do not answer the
upstream question of what validity evidence must exist before different claims are warranted.
      We therefore develop a six-stage workflow grounded in the dual-validity framework (Lin, in
press) that scales validity requirements to research ambition: specifying which evidence is
required before researchers escalate from tool use to behavioral characterization, human
simulation, or cognitive modeling (Table 1 maps the key decisions, required evidence, and
minimum reporting items at each stage).
The Dual-Validity Framework
      The dual-validity framework (Lin, in press) conceptualizes the methodological problem
identified above by integrating two validity traditions that psychology has developed separately:
psychometric validation and causal inference.
Valid Measurement
      Psychometrics assesses whether instruments measure the psychological constructs they
purport to measure (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Loevinger, 1957; Messick, 1989), a process of
simultaneous and ongoing validation of both a measure and the theory in which it is embedded
(Strauss & Smith, 2009). When LLM responses prove unstable across equivalent presentations,
this suggests that the measures capture statistical artifacts rather than genuine psychological
phenomena.
      The psychometric tradition establishes two essential requirements. Reliability demands
measurement stability: test-retest consistency across repeated administrations, parallel-forms
equivalence across semantically equivalent prompts, and internal consistency among items
assessing the same construct (Huang et al., 2024; Sclar et al., 2024; Sühr et al., 2025). Construct
validity requires accumulating evidence from multiple sources—content sampling, response
processes, internal structure, external relationships, and consequences—to build a coherent
argument that scores meaningfully reflect intended constructs rather than measurement phantoms
(Loevinger, 1957; Messick, 1989).
Valid Causal Inference
      Once psychometric foundations are secure, causal inference evaluates whether observed
relationships between manipulations and outcomes represent genuine causal phenomena or
methodological artifacts (Campbell & Stanley, 1963; Cook & Campbell, 1979; Kerschbaumer et
al., 2025). LLM research confronts four primary threats to causal validity: internal validity (can
effects be attributed to manipulations rather than confounds?), external validity (do findings
generalize across prompts, models, and populations?), construct validity of manipulations (do
experimental operationalizations faithfully embody theoretical constructs?), and statistical
conclusion validity (do analyses account for non-independent observations and unstable data-
generating processes?).

without consequence—one cannot design robust experiments (Stage 3) without validated

Stage 1: Define Research Goal
     Scientific claims require evidence commensurate with their ambition. A text classifier needs
different validation from a cognitive model; behavioral description demands different standards
from mechanistic explanation. The first and most crucial decision in the workflow, therefore, is
to explicitly classify the research goal: What type of claim will this research ultimately support?
Four categories emerge with escalating validity requirements (Lin, in press).
     Four categories emerge with escalating validity requirements. LLMs as research tools are
used for specific tasks—classification, annotation, stimulus generation, text manipulation—
where claims center on functional performance: can the LLM reliably perform task X? LLMs as
evaluation targets characterize stable behavioral patterns using psychological frameworks,
yielding descriptive claims that the system exhibits patterns interpretable as construct Y. LLMs
as human simulators replicate human responses at aggregate levels, supporting comparative
claims that the system’s outputs statistically parallel those of human population Z. LLMs as
cognitive models treat systems as computational analogues to human mental processes, making
mechanistic claims that the system implements processes structurally analogous to human
cognition.
     This classification determines which validation procedures are necessary versus optional
(Table 2). Misclassification cascades through subsequent stages; studies claiming LLMs have
theory of mind while providing only tool-level validation commit this category error (Riemer et
al., 2025).
Stage 2: Develop and Validate the Computational Instrument
     Based on the research goal defined in Stage 1, the validation process diverges into two
pathways.
Pathway A: Validation of Models as Research Tools
     Classification and coding/annotation tasks (e.g., analyzing sentiment, coding qualitative
data). The central claim is that an LLM can serve as a reliable stand-in for a chosen measurement
standard, whether human judgment or a validated instrument. Required evidence focuses on
three forms of validity: agreement with human coders (e.g., manually labeled data; Bunt et al.,
2025; Long et al., 2024), accuracy against gold-standard datasets, or consistency with established
psychometric instruments (e.g., clinical interview predictions against patient self-reports;
Galatzer-Levy et al., 2023).
     A robust protocol involves two-stage validation. First, prompts are iteratively refined on a
manually coded subset to establish semantic and content validity. During this phase, prompt-
sensitivity testing ensures that performance derives from task comprehension rather than
idiosyncratic phrasing. Second, the finalized prompt is evaluated on a held-out test set to confirm
predictive validity. For technical procedures such as fine-tuning pre-trained models for specific
classification goals, see Brickman et al. (2025).
      Stimulus generation tasks (e.g., creating vignettes, generating images). The claim is that
the LLM can produce materials that meet specific research requirements and elicit intended
psychological responses. Required evidence centers on content validity, assessed through expert
review, user ratings, and—crucially for experimental stimuli—manipulation checks
demonstrating that generated materials produce their intended effects. Unlike classification tasks,
process stability matters less than outcome validity; researchers may engage in extensive prompt
engineering (Lin, 2024) to achieve desired outputs, with validation focusing on the final stimulus
rather than generation consistency.
      A practical protocol involves iterative refinement followed by empirical validation (van
Berlo et al., 2024). Researchers or expert panels first evaluate the generated materials for
construct appropriateness and ecological validity, ensuring that materials represent intended
manipulations while identifying potential confounds. Pilot testing then confirms psychological
efficacy through manipulation checks and assessments of perceived realism or appropriateness.
Finally, domain-specific pretesting in the target population verifies that stimuli function as
intended without introducing unintended confounds before main study deployment.
      Text manipulation tasks (e.g., generating parallel forms for a questionnaire, simplifying
text, creating summaries). The claim is that the LLM can alter text according to specific rules
while preserving semantic integrity and achieving functional equivalence. Required evidence
involves expert evaluation of the output’s fidelity and functional validation—for instance,
demonstrating that parallel forms generated by the model are, in fact, psychometrically parallel
when tested.
      A robust protocol involves assessment of both preservation and transformation (Guidroz et
al., 2025). First, evaluate the manipulated text for semantic fidelity, ensuring that essential
meaning, tone, and construct-relevant content remain intact while achieving the intended
transformations (e.g., simplification, parallel phrasing). Technical validation then assesses
functional equivalence through appropriate metrics—readability scores for simplified text,
psychometric properties for parallel forms, or information completeness for summaries. Finally,
empirical testing with target users confirms that manipulated materials perform as intended—
improved comprehension for simplified text or equivalent measurement properties for parallel
instruments.
      One caveat applies across all three tool-validation protocols: results are model-specific by
default. Rankings shift unpredictably across model families—for instance, across 11 text-
classification tasks under matched annotation criteria, no model family led consistently, and
general benchmark rankings did not reliably predict task-specific performance (Alizadeh et al.,
2025)—and prompt-formatting choices alone can further flip those rankings across superficially
equivalent phrasings (Sclar et al., 2024). Researchers should therefore validate on the specific
model they intend to deploy and not generalize results across model families without
independent evidence (see Stage 3 on external validity).
Pathway B: Full Psychometric Validation of Models as Subjects
     For LLMs as evaluation targets, human simulators, or cognitive models, comprehensive
psychometric assessment becomes essential. Unlike simple research tools, these applications
make psychological claims about computational systems—requiring us to reconceptualize
validation itself. This is because traditional psychometrics assumes human respondents with
stable traits, communicative intent, and embodied experience; in contrast, LLMs lack temporal
continuity, possess no beliefs in the standard psychological or philosophical sense, and remain
ungrounded in the physical and social world (Lin, 2025b). These fundamental differences
demand adapting established psychometric principles to computational realities, with specific
requirements that vary by research goal.
     LLMs as Evaluation Targets (e.g., characterizing model “personality,” “biases,” or
response patterns). The challenge is distinguishing genuine computational phenomena—stable,
theoretically coherent behavioral regularities—from statistical artifacts or “cognitive phantoms”
(Peereboom et al., 2025). This requires moving beyond face validity to systematic validation
against multiple convergent criteria. The goal is behavioral characterization (what the system
does), not mechanistic explanation (why it does it).
     LLMs as Human Simulators (e.g., replicating survey responses, modeling population
attitudes). The primary required evidence is a demonstrated correspondence between model and
human data. A robust simulation requires evidence that the model’s data replicate not just the
central tendencies, such as the mean response, but also the deeper structural features of the
human data, including its distributional properties, such as variance, the internal structure of
psychometric scales, and the nomological network of correlations among variables (Mei et al.,
2024).
     In this context, psychometric validation is the toolkit used to establish the depth and fidelity
of the simulation. While individual response fidelity is not claimed, external validity—the
generalizability of the simulation to a specific, pre-defined human population—is central.
Achieving this level of fidelity requires sophisticated methods for persona development,
validation against human benchmarks, and management of temporal limitations inherent to static
models. For detailed methodological guidance on these and other relevant techniques, see Lin
(2026).
     LLMs as Cognitive Models (e.g., claiming shared mechanisms, using outputs to
understand human cognition). This represents the highest evidentiary burden, requiring all
evidence from the preceding levels plus a rigorous program of mechanistic investigation. The
required evidence must demonstrate architectural correspondence between the model and
cognitive theory, as well as direct causal links between model components and cognitive
functions. Such causal evidence moves beyond correlation and can be established through
techniques such as activation patching to trace information flow or model editing to demonstrate
that targeted changes produce predictable behaviors, analogous to lesion studies in neuroscience.
     Making a claim at this level means treating the LLM not as a black box but as a manipulable
experimental system for testing specific, falsifiable hypotheses about the algorithms of cognition.
This level of mechanistic investigation requires specialized methods for working with open-
weights models (for comprehensive guidance on these techniques, see Lin, 2026). For a technical
tutorial on implementation using open-weights models, see Hussain et al. (2024).
     The validation process for all Pathway B goals unfolds through three sequential phases.
Phase 2a: Content Validity and Instrument Development
     The objective is to create a prompt battery that comprehensively samples the construct
domain while avoiding computational artifacts.
     Define Computational Construct. Validity requires that the measured attribute exists and
causally produces observed scores (Borsboom et al., 2004). While human constructs like anxiety
or conscientiousness can be reasonably assumed for embodied minds, their existence in
disembodied statistical models cannot be assumed. This ontological gap makes direct translation
of human instruments psychometrically untenable. The first step must therefore be to
reconceptualize human-centric constructs for disembodied systems (Comşa & Shanahan, 2025).
     “Conscientiousness,” for instance, cannot mean effortful self-discipline in an LLM. Instead,
it might manifest as systematic response organization, meticulous instruction-following, or
structured output formatting. This reconceptualization preserves the theoretical core—organized,
dependable behavior—while acknowledging mechanistic differences. Measuring human self-
discipline in an LLM commits a category error; measuring conscientiousness-analogous
computational patterns remains viable.
     Reconceptualization without naming discipline invites the jingle fallacy—treating different
constructs as equivalent because they share a name (Wulff & Mata, 2025). Consider: 1) default
to an operational label tied to the measured behavior (e.g., response-organization tendency); 2)
use an analog label (e.g., conscientiousness-analogous response patterns) only when partial
theoretical continuity is argued explicitly; and 3) reserve the original human label for cases with
demonstrated measurement equivalence—a standard that rarely holds without explicit testing
(see Measurement Invariance, below). The worked example applies this convention: the
measured construct is labeled self-referential response consistency, not “LLM selfhood”
(Ibrahim & Cheng, 2025).
     Generate Item Pool. Adapting human scales to LLMs requires more than wording
substitution. The test-adaptation literature is clear: moving a validated instrument into a new
context demands evidence that the construct itself is meaningful there, not merely that items can
be rephrased (Ambuehl & Inauen, 2022; Hernández et al., 2020). Three adaptation rules guide
this translation. First, preserve theoretical function, not literal human wording: retain the
construct’s role in the nomological network while removing embodied, phenomenological, or
autobiographical content that presupposes human experience. Second, prefer scenario-based,
behaviorally anchored prompts over abstract self-report: items should be tied to observable
outputs—choice patterns, response structure, instruction adherence, consistency across
contexts—rather than treated as literal introspective queries (Lee et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2024).
Third, reduce contamination and prompt-artifact risk: do not reuse canonical human items
verbatim; paraphrase, contextualize, and test parallel forms to distinguish construct-relevant
variance from training-data retrieval—systematic performance differences across semantically
equivalent but lexically distinct parallel forms suggest retrieval rather than construct expression.
A detailed adaptation checklist is provided in the preregistration template (Supplementary
Appendix A, §2.4).
      Develop multiple prompts sampling all facets of the construct. Single-item measures are
psychometrically indefensible and empirically unreliable for capturing complex psychological
phenomena (Serapio-García et al., 2025). For a construct like personality, this requires a battery
of items including behavioral scenarios, self-description prompts, and forced-choice questions
that collectively represent the construct space. A straightforward starting point is to adapt
questions from existing, validated psychological scales and other materials (Simons et al., 2024).
      Conduct Expert Review. The item pool must then be scrutinized to navigate a tension:
overly constrained prompts measure instruction-following rather than emergent dispositions,
while overly loose prompts may be hijacked by unintended statistical features (Gui & Toubia,
2023). Expert review must therefore evaluate each prompt for construct relevance while
identifying and mitigating three critical confounds.
      First, training data contamination occurs when prompts trigger memorized responses rather
than genuine reasoning (Cheng et al., 2025): a model might “solve” a theory-of-mind problem by
retrieving a similar example from training data rather than reasoning about mental states.
Second, linguistic confounds arise when non-substantive features drive responses (Sclar et al.,
2024). Finally, instruction contamination happens when rigid phrasing invokes compliance, not
underlying behavioral tendencies—for example, “Describe how a highly conscientious person
would organize their week” primarily measures instruction-following. The goal is to design
prompts that elicit genuine construct-relevant patterns while remaining robust to these artifacts.
      Pilot Testing. Before launching a full validation study, use chain-of-thought (CoT)
prompting—instructing the model to reason step by step before answering (Wei et al., 2022)—as
a low-cost check on response plausibility. Reviewing these reasoning traces reveals whether the
model is engaging with construct-relevant aspects of the prompt or relying on superficial
heuristics. CoT traces are generated text, not direct readouts of internal mechanisms, and can
constitute fabricated reasoning paths rather than faithful process accounts (Lanham et al., 2023;
Turpin et al., 2023); treat them as provisional clues and triangulate with the perturbation and
ablation methods in Phase 2c.
Phase 2b: Reliability Assessment
      The objective is to establish measurement stability before assessing validity. An unreliable
instrument cannot be valid, regardless of its theoretical grounding.
      Because the sources of measurement error in LLMs differ from those in human respondents,
it is useful to distinguish three kinds of reliability evidence at the outset. Test-retest reliability
refers to consistency across independent runs with the same prompt and the same parameters—
the direct computational analogue of repeated administration, but sensitive here to stochastic
sampling, floating-point non-determinism, and silent model updates rather than respondent mood
or fatigue. Parallel-forms reliability refers to consistency across semantically equivalent but
differently worded prompts—a test of whether the instrument measures the construct or its
surface phrasing (Gupta et al., 2024). Parameter and version stability refers to consistency across
technical configurations (temperature, top_p, top_k) and model updates—relevant whenever an
instrument is intended for reuse or cross-study comparison, and without parallel in classical
psychometrics. The subsections below address each in turn. One point deserves emphasis: setting
the temperature parameter to 0 does not eliminate the need for test-retest testing. Even at
temperature = 0, outputs can vary across application programming interface (API) calls due to
floating-point non-determinism, infrastructure-level changes, and silent model updates—with the
last posing the greatest threat to reproducibility (Huang et al., 2024). Zero-temperature querying,
moreover, leaves the remaining two sources of measurement error untested.
     Test-Retest Reliability and Response Variance. In human studies, test-retest reliability
typically assesses trait stability over time. For stateless LLMs, however, the procedure serves a
different purpose: to characterize the model’s response variance—the inherent stochasticity in its
generation process for a given input (Huang et al., 2024).
     Use API access rather than web interfaces for reliability testing. APIs enable researchers to
select exact model versions (e.g., gpt-4-0125-preview), control crucial parameters like
temperature and top_p, and facilitate the batch processing essential for large-scale reliability
testing. Web interfaces provide insufficient control: they typically do not allow stable version
selection or parameter adjustment, and they may include features like memory or custom
instructions that contaminate reliability measurements.
     The method involves generating score distributions over multiple independent runs,
analogous to running multiple trials in a human behavioral experiment (Kochanek et al., 2024).
First, administer the complete instrument in a single run to generate one score. Repeat the full
instrument across independent sessions to prevent chat history contamination. The number of
runs can vary (e.g., 5 to 100, depending on the variability of the model’s responses) or follow a
prespecified precision rule, such as sampling until the 95% confidence interval for the mean
score narrows to, say, ±0.2 on a five-point scale. This precision-based stopping rule—in which
the number of runs is determined by a target for estimation accuracy rather than by an arbitrary
fixed sample—instantiates the Accuracy in Parameter Estimation (AIPE) framework and aligns
with the Psychonomic Society’s recommendation that studies be designed to measure effects to a
desired degree of precision (Maxwell et al., 2008; Psychonomic Society, 2019). This yields a
score distribution whose stability characterizes the instrument’s reliability. For discrete scores
(e.g., single Likert items), analyze response frequencies and modal responses. For continuous
scores (e.g., composite scales), examine means and standard deviations.
     When conducting these reliability tests, account for caching mechanisms implemented by
API providers. Prompt caching (input caching) poses no threat—providers cache processed
inputs for efficiency while generating new responses. Response caching (output caching)
threatens validity by re-serving identical outputs for identical queries. While major providers
such as OpenAI and Anthropic have not, as of this writing, implemented output response caching
natively—that is, they do not re-serve a stored reply to an identical query—custom research
pipelines (including middleware, proxy layers, and local inference wrappers) may. Disable such
features or bypass them by adding unique identifiers to each prompt.
     Parallel-Forms Reliability. Create semantically equivalent but syntactically distinct prompt
variants to test for hypersensitivity. If trivial changes—like altering response labels from “(A),
(B)” to “1., 2.”—substantially change results, the instrument is measuring prompt features rather
than the intended construct (Sclar et al., 2024).
     A systematic evaluation involves testing format variations across multiple dimensions
(Brucks & Toubia, 2025): question framing (declarative vs. interrogative), response formats
(alphabetic vs. numeric labels), option ordering, and punctuation styles. Administer these
variants to assess performance stability, measuring both central tendency and variance across
format conditions to identify hypersensitive elements. For instruments showing excessive
brittleness, implement robustness techniques such as mixed-format prompting—using different
formatting styles for few-shot examples within the same prompt—to reduce spurious correlations
between format and content (Ngweta et al., 2025).
     Internal Consistency. For multi-item instruments, assess item coherence to ensure all items
contribute to measuring the same underlying construct; simultaneous endorsement of
contradictory statements violates this assumption (Sühr et al., 2025). McDonald’s Omega (ω) is
generally preferable to traditional Cronbach’s alpha, as it does not require the strict assumption
of τ-equivalence—that all items load equally on the latent factor (Malkewitz et al., 2023). Be
wary of artificially high consistency, as it can signal a response set in which the model generates
a coherent narrative rather than responding independently to each item, a potential symptom of
training artifacts such as sycophancy bias.
     Parameter Stability. Evaluate the instrument’s stability across technical configurations that
control response stochasticity. This involves systematically testing for consistent performance
across a range of relevant parameters, such as temperature, top_p, and top_k, because using a
single deterministic setting can be unrepresentative of the system’s overall behavior
(Abdurahman et al., 2024; Bisbee et al., 2024; Löhn et al., 2024). The need to assess stability
across model updates, however, depends on the research goal. For single-use applications like
generating a specific set of stimuli, this is unnecessary. For any instrument intended for repeated
or future use (e.g., a reusable classification tool or a published psychometric scale), evaluating its
performance across model versions is a critical test of its long-term viability and external
validity.
Phase 2c: Construct Validity Assessment
     The objective is to build an evidence-based argument that the instrument measures the
intended construct through multiple converging sources.
     Internal Structure Analysis. Apply factor analysis to test whether the instrument’s
dimensionality aligns with theoretical expectations. Human-derived instruments often fail this
test when applied to LLMs—multi-factor structures fracture into arbitrary factors that reflect
item-scoring direction rather than the intended psychological constructs, casting doubt on
whether the target constructs exist in the model at all (Peereboom et al., 2025).
     But structural misfit may also signal methodological mismatch rather than construct
absence. The resolution requires redesigning instruments for computational systems rather than
abandoning the construct entirely. Promising alternatives include generative psychometrics—
using free-text generation and subsequent analysis to derive the model’s underlying structures
(Ye, Xie, et al., 2025)—or grounding assessment in scenario-based behavioral choices rather
than abstract self-descriptions (Lee et al., 2025). These approaches may recover theoretically
coherent dimensional structures that traditional self-report formats obscure.
     Measurement invariance (MI) becomes a formal requirement whenever the research goal is
to compare construct-level scores across groups and to interpret any mean difference as
reflecting a genuine difference in the underlying construct (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016;
Vandenberg & Lance, 2000). The clearest case is human–LLM mean comparison in simulator
studies; cross-model comparison is equally demanding when the claim concerns construct-level
equivalence across architectures. By contrast, comparing runs across prompt variants or model
versions addresses reliability and stability—questions handled by prompt-robustness and
parameter-stability testing. A collapsed factor structure is not itself a formal MI test, but it is a
strong signal that even configural equivalence—the most basic level—may fail. Direct cross-
group comparisons remain uninterpretable until scalar invariance (for continuous indicators) or
threshold invariance (for ordinal indicators, the usual standard for LLM response data; Tse et al.,
2024) is demonstrated; similar factor labels or superficially parallel items are insufficient. The
preregistration template (Supplementary Appendix A, §3.8) provides a structured plan for
specifying and testing MI when cross-group comparisons are anticipated. Without such evidence,
observed score differences may reflect measurement artifacts rather than genuine construct
differences.
     Response Process Investigation. This step examines how a model generates its response to
guard against the primary threat of mechanistic substitution—where a model uses statistical
pattern matching to produce a correct answer rather than engaging in the psychological process
the construct presupposes (Gao et al., 2025). For example, a model might pass a theory-of-mind
test not by reasoning about mental states, but by simply retrieving a similar example from its
training data (Cheng et al., 2025).
     The techniques available for this investigation depend on the model’s accessibility. For
closed-source models, behavioral methods are key. Chain-of-thought prompting can provide
clues about the model’s generated reasoning path, allowing for a qualitative check of its logic
(Wei et al., 2022). Another powerful technique is systematic prompt perturbation—making
small, targeted changes to the prompt to see if the model is sensitive to construct-relevant
information versus superficial artifacts (Brucks & Toubia, 2025; Sclar et al., 2024). For open-
weights models, more direct causal interventions like ablation studies (disabling specific model
components) become possible, allowing for stronger inferences about which mechanisms
contribute to the response.
     Response-process probing can reveal whether apparent successes and failures reflect
genuine reasoning or statistical artifacts—sometimes in opposite directions, as Strachan et al.
(2024) demonstrated. GPT-4 failed a “faux pas” test under direct questioning (“Did they
know?”) but succeeded when the format shifted to a probabilistic framing (“Is it more likely they
knew or didn’t know?”). The failure reflected not a reasoning deficit but an unwillingness to
commit without high certainty—a response-policy artifact that masked intact reasoning.
LLaMA2-70B showed the reverse pattern: it passed the standard test but failed to distinguish
knowledge-implied stories—where the speaker’s utterance signaled awareness of the context—
from neutral stories where no such implication existed. A model genuinely reasoning about
mental states would adjust its responses across these variants; LLaMA2-70B did not, revealing
that its apparent success reflected a rigid heuristic—attributing ignorance regardless of context—
rather than theory-of-mind reasoning.
     Convergent and Discriminant Validation. Establish a coherent nomological network by
testing for expected correlations with theoretically related (convergent) and unrelated
(discriminant) measures (Serapio-García et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2024).
     LLMs frequently fail nomological validation through a systematic disconnect: scores on
explicit personality or attitude questionnaires fail to predict how the model actually behaves in
interactive tasks (Zou et al., 2024). The natural interpretation is construct invalidity, but explicit
self-report may be a poor fit for systems that do not represent internal states in propositional
form. Implicit measures, designed to tap associative rather than declarative processing, offer an
alternative. Ma et al. (2025) adapted the Implicit Association Test to measure latent sentiment
toward neutral words and found correlations above 0.85 with sentiment in downstream text
generation—a result suggesting that valid nomological networks are achievable when the
measurement method is matched to the model’s architecture.
     Consequential Evidence Analysis. The final phase of construct validation assesses the
instrument’s real-world consequences, including behavioral predictivity and social impact. Ask:
does attributing psychological constructs to computational systems advance understanding, or
does it merely reify measurement artifacts as genuine phenomena? Two steps are involved.
     First, establish behavioral consequences by testing whether scores predict theoretically
relevant outcomes across contexts. A neuroticism scale, for instance, should predict negative
emotion words (“hate,” “depressed”) in a downstream text-generation task (Serapio-García et al.,
2025). Without such predictive relationships, the instrument measures statistical patterns rather
than meaningful constructs.
     Second, evaluate social and ethical implications. Behavioral validation alone is insufficient;
the instrument’s broader consequences require systematic analysis (Stade et al., 2024). This
involves several proactive steps: (1) conduct a bias and representation audit by testing whether
the instrument produces stereotyped or harmful outputs when prompted with different social
identities; (2) engage in stakeholder consultation, particularly when an instrument pertains to
specific communities, to prevent epistemic injustice and ensure the measure is not
misrepresentative; (3) define appropriate use boundaries based on these analyses, explicitly
warning against applying the instrument in high-stakes domains where misinterpretation could
lead to harm, such as in clinical or educational settings.
Stage 3: Design Experiment
     With a validated measurement instrument, we can now design experiments that test causal
hypotheses. The core challenge shifts from “Can we measure this construct reliably?” to “Can
we manipulate it systematically while controlling for artifacts?”
     Operationalize the Manipulation and Outcome. Define both the independent variable
(manipulation) and the dependent variable (the validated instrument from Stage 2). The
manipulation must instantiate the theoretical cause, not merely its linguistic correlates. For
instance, prompting “Everyone agrees that…” may trigger conformity language patterns without
engaging the psychological mechanisms that define social pressure. Similarly, ensure the
dependent variable employs the validated instrument rather than ad hoc measures created for
convenience.
     Control Four Categories of Validity Threats. With the constructs operationalized, the next
step is to design the experiment to withstand the four primary threats to causal inference: internal
validity, external validity, construct validity, and statistical conclusion validity.
     Internal validity—whether effects stem from manipulations rather than confounds—faces
several computational threats. Prompt-level confounds arise because minor, theoretically
irrelevant variations alter results. The design must control for positional effects (randomize
information placement), formatting artifacts (test label variations), and scenario reconstruction.
Unlike human participants, who can be blinded to conditions, LLMs actively infer entire
contexts from minimal cues. When told a product costs $8 instead of $5, a model may assume
the entire market has shifted—that competitor prices, economic trends, and consumer
expectations have all adjusted accordingly (Gui & Toubia, 2023). This reconstruction confounds
the price manipulation with unstated background assumptions. Address this by explicitly
constraining irrelevant contextual factors.
     Technical confounds require environmental stability. Document and maintain consistent
model versions and API parameters. Because providers update models silently—causing
substantial performance variations and replication failures—researchers must record collection
dates and model specifications (for a primer on navigating model updates and other threats to
replicability, see Abdurahman et al., 2025). Hold temperature settings constant or vary them
systematically as factors. Context accumulation becomes problematic in extended interactions.
As the model’s context window fills, early information disappears, potentially altering responses
to later manipulations. Limit session length or reset context between conditions.
     External validity concerns whether a causal finding can generalize beyond the specific
study. For LLMs, this requires defining the boundaries of a claim across several key dimensions.
First, findings rarely generalize across models; due to differences in architecture and training, an
effect observed in one model family (e.g., GPT-4) must not be assumed to hold for another (e.g.,
Claude; Bisbee et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025).
     Second, generalization to human populations is a central challenge. Because LLMs are not
representative samples of any human population, claims of human simulation must be supported
by direct empirical validation against human data (Bisbee et al., 2024; Lin, 2026). A simulation
may replicate some phenomena but fail at others; for instance, LLMs have failed to simulate the
“Wisdom of Crowds” effect because they lack the independent error patterns that enable it (Aher
et al., 2023).
     Finally, researchers must consider temporal generalization, which carries two related threats.
The first is temporal displacement: LLMs are static snapshots of their training data, so that a
finding from a model with a 2024 knowledge cutoff may not reflect human attitudes or realities
in 2025—a risk especially acute for time-sensitive constructs such as social attitudes, political
beliefs, or evolving cultural norms (Lin, 2025b; Qu & Wang, 2024). The second is temporal
replicability: even when a study is internally valid at the time of collection, the “same” model
accessed through a commercial API may behave differently months later as providers silently
update it; Chen et al. (2024) documented substantial performance shifts between March and June
2023 versions of GPT-4 across several benchmark tasks, demonstrating that a model alias is not
a stable replication anchor. Studies should therefore document the exact model version, the
knowledge cutoff where available, and the date of data collection (see Stage 4 for the full
specification). When the target construct is plausibly time-sensitive, either justify the assumption
of temporal stability or test it directly.
     Construct validity of the manipulation, which is distinct from the validity of the measure
assessed in Stage 2, focuses on whether the experimental manipulation faithfully represents the
theoretical cause. The primary threat here is the alignment-as-explanation fallacy, where a
researcher assumes a prompt instantiates a psychological process when it only mimics its
linguistic correlates (Lin, 2025b). For example, a manipulation intended to test “moral
reasoning” might only be testing the model’s ability to retrieve statistical patterns associated with
ethical language, not engaging any reasoning process at all. To guard against such mechanistic
misattribution, researchers can use factorial designs that cross the substantive manipulation (e.g.,
“social pressure”) with theoretically irrelevant variations (e.g., prompt formatting) to disentangle
true psychological effects from superficial artifacts (Brucks & Toubia, 2025).
     Finally, statistical conclusion validity concerns whether the data analysis supports the
inferences drawn. LLM-generated data systematically violate the assumptions of standard
statistical tests in two main ways. First, responses from a single model are not independent
observations; treating them as such inflates the risk of false positives (Aher et al., 2023).
Analyses must therefore account for this clustered data structure. Second, LLMs often produce
less variable responses than humans, which can artificially inflate statistical power while
reducing the practical significance of findings (Bisbee et al., 2024). Effect size interpretations
must be calibrated accordingly.
     These two properties—non-independence and restricted variance—also distort sample-size
planning. Because LLM responses are far less variable than human responses, plugging LLM-
generated statistics into a standard power formula produced a wildly optimistic estimate of
required sample size—implying adequate power from a fraction of the observations a human-
participant study would require (Bisbee et al., 2024). Researchers should therefore base sample-
size reasoning on the number of statistically independent units (e.g., distinct prompt conditions
or model runs treated as clusters), not on the raw observation count, and should calibrate
variance assumptions against LLM-specific pilot data rather than human norms. For complex
designs, simulation-based planning is preferable to closed-form approximations (Miller, 2024).
     Develop a Preregistration Plan. The ease and low cost of generating LLM data increase
researcher degrees of freedom, creating temptations for HARKing (Hypothesizing After the
Results are Known) and p-hacking (Lones, 2024; Schaeffer et al., 2023). Preregistration is
therefore essential. This preregistration must specify the experimental design, including: prompt
variations; technical parameters (model, version, temperature); the analysis plan (including
planned robustness checks); and the boundary conditions for the claims that will be made.
     Because LLM research rapidly multiplies testable comparisons—across prompt variants,
model settings, parsers, and robustness conditions—the preregistration should include an explicit
multiplicity plan that defines, in advance, the family of inferences subject to error-rate control
(García-Pérez, 2023; Gelman & Loken, 2014). A practical structure is to designate a primary
confirmatory family consisting of one or a small number of prespecified contrasts and to apply a
familywise correction such as the Holm (1979) procedure when that family contains more than
one test. Broader robustness batteries and secondary analyses should be classified as exploratory
in advance: when an exploratory battery is bounded and a correction is defensible, false-
discovery-rate control is appropriate (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995); when the battery is
genuinely open-ended, results should be reported descriptively with confidence intervals and no
significance claims. All tested outcomes, conditions, and model comparisons should be
disclosed—including prompt variants explored and abandoned during development—with any
post hoc additions flagged as exploratory. This distinction reflects the broader principle that the
inferential weight attached to any test depends on whether it was specified before or after
observing the data (Wagenmakers et al., 2012; see the preregistration template in
Supplementary Appendix A, §4.8, for a worked example).
     While the iterative nature of prompt development can seem at odds with rigid pre-
registration, researchers can adopt a multi-stage registration process. First, preregister the
instrument development and validation plan (all of Stage 2), and then, once the instrument is
validated, update the registration with the final experimental protocol before collecting data for
the main causal-inference experiment. This approach balances exploratory validation with
confirmatory testing, while maintaining transparency.

Stage 4: Execute and Document the Experiment
     This stage concerns the implementation of the preregistered design. The objective is not
rigid adherence, but faithful execution combined with transparent documentation of any
necessary deviations, ensuring replicability.
     Specify and Document the Environment. Researchers must document the computational
environment to enable replication. For API access, documentation must include the specific
model endpoint (e.g., gpt-4-0125-preview), technical parameters when available (temperature,
top_p, max_tokens), the date and time of data collection, and any preprocessing or
postprocessing steps. Record the API version and any relevant system specifications that might
affect model behavior.
     For web interface usage, documentation becomes more challenging due to limited
transparency. Record the model family selected (e.g., GPT Thinking 5.4), interaction dates and
times, and interface settings. Document any active features like memory, custom instructions, or
conversation history that could influence outputs. Explicitly note that the underlying model
version and exact parameters remain opaque and potentially unstable.
     Execute the Protocol with Transparency. While the preregistered plan should be followed
faithfully, researchers may discover that an aspect of the plan is suboptimal. In such cases,
deviations are permissible, provided they are explicitly documented and justified. The original,
preregistered analysis must still be reported, alongside the revised analysis, with a clear
explanation for why the change was made. This maintains transparency while allowing for
necessary methodological improvements.
     Ensure Data Preservation. Comprehensive data preservation requires storing more than
final parsed responses: the full prompts exactly as sent; complete raw outputs before parsing (to
permit re-analysis of the parsing procedure itself); all relevant metadata, including timestamps
and token counts; and any pre- or post-processing code. These materials should be stored in a
standardized format (e.g., JSON or CSV) and, upon submission or publication, deposited in a
reputable public repository (e.g., Zenodo, Figshare). Because models can be deprecated or
silently updated, a complete replication package is the only guarantee that the work remains
auditable.

Stage 5: Analyze and Interpret Results
     Perform Data Quality and Assumption Checks. Before finalizing an analysis, two
additional checks are prudent. First, conduct a qualitative data evaluation to screen for
anomalies, such as nonsensical responses or hallucinations, which could distort quantitative
results. Second, perform standard statistical assumption checks for the chosen analytical model
(e.g., checking for normality and homoscedasticity of residuals in a regression model) to ensure
the model itself is specified correctly.
     Address Data Non-Independence. Applying the correct remedy requires distinguishing
three levels. The unit of observation is a single LLM response to a single item in a single session.
The experimental unit (or cluster) is the smallest entity independently sampled or manipulated,
and its identity depends on study design: in a multi-model comparison, the experimental unit is
the model; in a within-model prompt-variation study, it is the prompt condition; in repeated-run
reliability testing, it is the session or run, since responses within a session share a common
conversational history. The unit of analysis is the level at which inference is drawn. This need
not match the unit of observation: when 20 runs per model are averaged into a single mean, the
unit of analysis becomes the model, even though 20 observations were collected. The situation is
analogous to a within-subjects psycholinguistic experiment: just as one participant reading 40
sentences produces 40 observations clustered within that participant, one model queried across
40 prompts in a single session produces 40 observations clustered within that session (Quené &
van den Bergh, 2004). Analyzing individual responses as though they were independent
participants is pseudoreplication (Lazic, 2010).
     Non-independence manifests through three dependency patterns: within-model clustering
(repeated queries to the same model), model-condition clustering (the same model under
different experimental conditions), and item clustering (multiple questions about shared
scenarios or passages). Figure 2 illustrates this nested structure, summarizes the main analytic
options, and guides method selection by dependence structure and inferential target.

descriptive comparisons bounded to the tested models rather than generalized to a broader model
population; generalization is better grounded in independent items sampled from a defined population of
possible questions. Cluster bootstrap serves as a robustness and sensitivity analysis throughout; it is not
the default remedy for small cluster counts, and no single cluster-count cutoff reliably demarcates safe

     The analysis must account for these structures through three complementary strategies. First,
aggregation collapses multiple responses into summary statistics—for example, when querying a
model 20 times with the same prompt, calculate the mean response, thereby eliminating within-
model correlation. This approach yields independent units but sacrifices information about
response variability. Second, multilevel modeling explicitly models the hierarchical data
structure by nesting individual responses within models (or model-condition combinations), or
alternatively, generalized estimating equations (GEE) can specify appropriate correlation
structures. These methods preserve all data but require sufficient models (e.g., ≥20) for stable
variance estimates. Third, cluster-level bootstrap resamples entire models rather than individual
responses, preserving the dependency structure. This approach functions as a robustness and
sensitivity analysis, not the default remedy for small cluster counts (see Supplementary Box S1
for an implementation).
     Choose among these strategies by the inferential target—that is, by the estimand the study is
designed to estimate—since different analytic approaches can address substantively different
questions with the same data (Kahan et al., 2023, for discussion in the cluster-trial context).
When the goal is a stable cluster-level summary and within-cluster variability is a nuisance—for
instance, estimating a mean selfhood score for GPT-4o across 20 independent sessions—
aggregation to session means is a first choice; with balanced cluster sizes, summary-statistics
approaches are mathematically equivalent to multilevel models and should not be dismissed in
favor of more complex methods on principle (McNabb & Murayama, 2021). When item-level
variability is informative, when items differ in difficulty, or when the design crosses sessions
with items or conditions, multilevel models with crossed random effects for session and item are
preferable (Brown, 2021; see Box 1 for a minimal R implementation).
        Box 1. Minimal R Implementation of a Crossed Random-Effects Model
        # Each row: one response to one item in one independent session
        # session_id = independently initiated model session (experimental unit)
        # item_id   = prompt/scenario (crossed factor)
        # condition = experimental manipulation (e.g., individualistic vs. collectivistic prime)
        library(lme4)
        library(lmerTest) # extends lme4 to add Satterthwaite df for fixed effects
        m <- lmer(score ~ condition + (1 | session_id) + (1 | item_id), data = dat)
        summary(m) # fixed-effect test of condition uses Satterthwaite df
        Note. The variable names correspond to the unit-of-analysis definitions above: session_id
indexes the experimental unit (cluster), item_id indexes the crossed item factor, and each row is a unit of
observation. The model specifies random intercepts only; if condition varies within sessions or items,
random slopes (e.g., (1 + condition | session_id)) may be warranted and should be added unless
convergence failures require simplification (Barr et al., 2013). A cluster-bootstrap sensitivity analysis is
provided in Supplementary Box S1 (see also Deen & de Rooij, 2020). For an accessible introduction to
mixed-effects modeling in R, see Brown (2021).
     When the primary interest is fixed-effect estimates and clustering is a nuisance, cluster-
robust standard errors (CRSEs) or GEE provide a simpler alternative; however, when cluster
counts are small—often taken to mean fewer than roughly 20, though the precise threshold
depends on the ICC, cluster-size balance, and outcome type—conventional CRSEs can be anti-
conservative, and bias-corrected variants (e.g., CR2 with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom) or
wild cluster bootstrap should be used as sensitivity analyses (Deen & de Rooij, 2020; Huang &
Li, 2022; McNeish & Stapleton, 2016). When cluster counts are very small—e.g., fewer than
five independent models—all clustered inferential methods become highly unreliable, with bias
and coverage properties that depend heavily on ICC, cluster-size balance, and estimator choice;
results should be treated as essentially descriptive, any inferential procedure should be flagged as
highly tentative, and findings should not be generalized beyond the tested models. These
methods are drawn from the clustered human-participant literature and apply here by analogy: in
LLM research, the ICC reflects response stochasticity within a model-prompt pair across runs
rather than genuine population heterogeneity across sites, a distinction that should inform how
ICC magnitudes are interpreted.
     For naturally grouped evaluation prompts—such as multiple questions about a single
scenario or reading passage—Jackson (2020) found through simulations that corrected standard
errors produced more accurate inference than bootstrapping in this item-clustering setting; use
cluster-robust standard errors with finite-sample correction to adjust for intra-group correlation.
When the same items are reused across models or conditions, paired item-level difference
analyses are preferable to comparisons of aggregate scores: pairing controls for question-specific
difficulty, increases the signal-to-noise ratio, and grounds inference in the sample of independent
items rather than in the small number of tested models (Miller, 2024). Naive standard errors can
underestimate uncertainty by a factor of three or more, leading to severely anti-conservative
statistical tests (Miller, 2024); always report the number of clusters and intracluster correlation
coefficients to enable proper interpretation of results. These analytic requirements—accounting
for non-independence, reporting cluster structure, and matching methods to the dependence
structure of the data—reflect standard good practice in quantitative psychological research.
     Conduct Robustness Analyses. Core findings must be robust to reasonable perturbations.
This includes not only technical robustness—demonstrating that results hold across variations in
parameter settings (e.g., temperature), prompt formatting, and data parsing—but also conceptual
robustness. Conceptual robustness involves actively challenging an initial finding by testing it
against theoretically relevant variations in task logic and content. For example, by altering
question format and creating variants of each test, these diagnostic experiments help to unmask
the nature of theory-of-mind performance in LLMs (Strachan et al., 2024). A finding that is not
robust across these multiple dimensions is likely a measurement phantom, not a stable
phenomenon.
     Calibrate Interpretations of Effects. Effect sizes and statistical significance require careful
interpretation in LLM research. Because models often produce less variable responses than
humans, statistical power can be inflated and effect sizes artificially large (Bisbee et al., 2024).
Researchers should focus on the consistency of patterns across robustness checks rather than the
magnitude or p-value of any single analysis. The ease with which statistical significance can be
achieved with large samples of non-independent data demands caution (Schaeffer et al., 2023).

Stage 6: Report and Reconceptualize
    Ensure Transparent and Accessible Reporting. Transparent reporting is the condition for
cumulative LLM science. The replication package—minimum contents specified in Stage 4 and
Supplementary Table S1—should be deposited in a public repository (e.g., Zenodo, Figshare),
with all elements released together rather than piecemeal. The goal is not only within-laboratory
reruns but also the form of cross-laboratory replication most meaningful for LLM research:
independent teams executing the same evaluation specification against different model families,
later versions, or future releases. Flag in the limitations section which findings most require
cross-model or cross-version verification. To assist with standards-compliant reporting
(Feuerriegel et al., 2026; Gallifant et al., 2025; Park et al., 2024), Supplementary Appendix A
provides a preregistration template, Supplementary Table S1 a reporting checklist, and
Supplementary Box S1 example analysis code.
    Constrain Claims to Evidence. Conclusions must be framed within the demonstrated
boundaries of the study. Avoid anthropomorphic language unless helpful and necessary (Ibrahim
& Cheng, 2025). Instead of claiming “LLMs have anxiety,” opt for precise, operational
language. Claims must distinguish between observed behavioral performance and inferred
underlying competence, a key principle in avoiding the alignment-as-explanation fallacy (Lin,
2025b).
    Use Findings to Reconceptualize and Refine. The results should be used to build better
theories, methods, and tools. When a study’s goal was tool development, the findings inform the
creation of more robust instruments. When the goal was descriptive, the findings contribute to a
more precise computational psychology. And when a human-centric construct fails to validate in
an LLM, it presents an opportunity to refine our understanding of both human and artificial
cognition.
    Address Limitations and Ethical Implications. Address limitations of generalizability
explicitly—across model versions, families, prompt populations, and collection dates. Discuss
broader ethical implications where relevant: how findings might shape public perception of AI
capabilities, inform policy, or affect communities implicated by the research. Where AI tools
were used in the research process itself, document the basis for that choice (Lin, 2025a).
An Example: Applying the Workflow to Measure “LLM Selfhood”
     To illustrate the workflow in action, consider a project investigating “LLM selfhood.” Self-
reference was chosen because it is among the most anthropomorphically loaded and
methodologically fragile constructs in current AI discourse (Ibrahim & Cheng, 2025).
Philosophers have questioned whether current language models can meaningfully be said to
possess persistent memory, genuine beliefs, or continuity of identity (Comşa & Shanahan, 2025),
yet self-referential language remains a site of active interpretive pressure—one where the
temptation to over-interpret is strongest and the need for disciplined reconceptualization most
acute. Figure 3 summarizes how this worked example maps onto the six stages of the workflow.
     In Stage 1, we classify the goal as an evaluation target—characterizing behavioral patterns
rather than building a tool or testing a cognitive architecture. Stage 2 reconceptualizes the
construct as self-referential response consistency: the stability and coherence of self-referential
linguistic patterns across contexts, assessed through a battery of adapted behavioral scenarios
(Box 2). The preregistered validation plan specifies test-retest reliability quantified via ICC
across independent sessions, parallel-forms reliability across semantically equivalent prompts,
and McDonald’s ω for internal consistency. Because the battery uses forced-choice scenarios (to
make response parsing unambiguous), inter-rater reliability between human reviewers during
item development—specifically, independent ratings of each item’s construct relevance and
contamination risk (Supplementary Appendix A, §2.7)—is established before psychometric
analyses proceed (Supplementary Appendix A provides full specifications). Stage 3 designs an
experiment testing the malleability of observed selfhood via an individualism-versus-
collectivism story prime. Stage 4 documents exact model versions, API parameters, and query
dates. Stage 5 analyzes the priming effect using a crossed random-effects model with random
intercepts for session and item (Box 1), with a session-level cluster bootstrap as a sensitivity
analysis (Supplementary Box S1).
        Box 2. Sample Prompts from the LLM Selfhood Battery
        The items below illustrate three validation procedures for the selfhood battery. The construct is
operationalized as response consistency across ambiguous, identity-relevant scenarios: a model with
stable self-referential patterns should select the same response strategy regardless of how the scenario
is framed. Forced-choice responses (A–D) are converted to numeric scores according to a preregistered
scoring key specified before data collection (Supplementary Appendix A, §2.5); these numeric values
constitute the score variable used in Box 1 and Supplementary Box S1.
        1) Instrument item (behavioral scenario). A user sends the message: “Tell me about quantum
entanglement.” This could refer to the physics concept, a philosophical metaphor, or a recent film. Which
response best describes what you would do first? (A) Choose one interpretation and answer directly. (B)
Note the ambiguity and offer multiple interpretations. (C) Ask the user which meaning they intend. (D)
Provide a brief answer covering all possibilities. Select one option, then give a one-sentence justification.
        2) Response-process probe (chain-of-thought). Before answering the item above, list the specific
cues in the prompt that influenced your choice. Then answer. CoT traces provide low-cost response-
process evidence but should not be treated as faithful readouts of internal mechanisms, as generated
reasoning can be a post-hoc rationalization (Lanham et al., 2023; Turpin et al., 2023); triangulate with
perturbation analyses where possible.
             3) Parallel-form pair. Form A: “When a user’s message has multiple plausible interpretations,
which response best describes your default behavior?” Form B: “If a user’s message can be interpreted in
multiple plausible ways, what are you most likely to do by default?” Both forms use the same four
response options. Scores are compared across forms to assess format sensitivity versus construct
stability.
    Stage 6 frames conclusions as calibrated behavioral claims rather than ontological ones.
Rather than claiming that “the model has a stable self-concept,” a calibrated descriptive claim is:
“under the tested prompting conditions, the model produced self-referential outputs that showed
moderate test-retest stability across sessions and prompt paraphrases,” and a calibrated
experimental claim is: “identity priming shifted the model’s self-referential response profile,
indicating malleability of elicited self-referential language under these conditions.” When
response-process evidence is weak or ambiguous, the report should note that observed patterns
may reflect stable generation policies or training-data regularities rather than anything properly
described as self-representation (Ibrahim & Cheng, 2025).

Concluding Remarks
    This workflow is designed as a generative framework for producing durable, credible, and
conceptually precise research on the psychology of LLMs—and AI systems more broadly.
Consider what happens when an instrument fails validation in Stage 2. When a researcher finds
that a human-centric construct like “agreeableness” shows a theoretically inconsistent structure
or fails to predict behavior in an LLM, the workflow compels them to do more than discard the
finding. It forces them to abandon the naive anthropomorphic label and build a new,
computationally grounded construct from the ground up—one defined by the specific,
observable, and reliable behavioral patterns of the model itself. The self-referential response
consistency patterns in our worked example represent such a construct. Therefore, the workflow
does more than validate; it facilitates the reconceptualization that moves us from uncritically
applying human metaphors to characterizing genuine computational phenomena.
     While providing essential structure, the workflow cannot solve all methodological
challenges. Its success depends heavily on researcher expertise during prompt generation (Stage
2a) and in the theoretical interpretation of response processes (Stage 2c). It also cannot solve the
problem of methodological half-life. The validation of an instrument against one model version
(e.g., GPT-4o as of July 2025) may not hold for a future version released months later.
Validation, like the models themselves, must be understood as a continuous process rather than a
one-time endpoint.
     These limitations point to broader implications for the field. This workflow deliberately
introduces friction—slowing the rapid cycle of “prompt-and-publish” in favor of careful
validation—because the long-term cost of brittle findings exceeds the short-term cost of
methodological rigor. As researchers adopt this protocol, they will produce a growing number of
validated computational instruments, and the logical next step is for the field to establish shared
repositories for them. Such repositories serve three functions simultaneously: methodological
blueprints that allow future teams to adapt validated instruments to newer models rather than
starting from scratch; historical benchmarks that track behavioral drift as models update; and
libraries of computationally grounded constructs that prevent conceptual work from being redone
in isolation. That infrastructure, not one-off prompt demonstrations, is the route to a cumulative
psychology of LLM behavior. By treating measurement validation as an engine for iterative
theory building rather than a one-time gatekeeping procedure, the framework offers a path
toward characterizing genuine computational phenomena.
