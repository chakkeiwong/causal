                                  ABSTRACT

      I develop a structural model of mortgage demand and lender compe-

      tition to study how leverage regulation affects the equilibrium in the

      UK mortgage market. Using variation in risk-weighted capital require-

      ments jointly across lenders and mortgages with differential loan-to-

      values, I show that a one-percentage-point increase in risk-weighted

      capital requirements increases lenders’ marginal cost of originating

      mortgages by about 26 basis points (11 percent) for the average mort-

      gage product. I use the estimated model to study proposed leverage

      regulations. Counterfactual analyses show that large lenders exploit

      a regulatory cost advantage, which increases concentration of mort-

      gage originations by about 20 percent, and suggest that banning high

      loan-to-value mortgages may reduce large lenders’ equity buffer.


After the global financial crisis, several European countries and the US
have introduced new regulations to prevent excessive bank leverage and in-
crease the stability of the financial system. Prominent examples include
risk-weighted minimum capital requirements for lenders and limits to loan-
to-income and loan-to-value for borrowers (Acharya et al., 2014; Behn et al.,
2016a; DeFusco et al., 2020; Jiménez et al., 2017). While there is relatively
wide agreement on the necessity of such measures, a large debate has emerged
on the design of lender- and borrower-based leverage regulations and their
implications for the allocation of credit.
   In an ongoing effort to better link capital to risk, policymakers have also
allowed lenders to invest in internal rating-based models to calculate the
risk weights on their activities. Internal rating-based models may improve

           Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3247956
risk management practices by financial intermediaries, but they are costly
to develop and maintain. As a result, a two-tier system prevails, with large
lenders adopting internal rating-based models and small lenders opting for
the standardized regulatory approach. This heterogeneity across different
lenders and asset classes can have unintended consequences, such as potential
regulatory arbitrage and reduced competition in the market (Acharya et al.,
2013; Behn et al., 2016b; Greenwood et al., 2017; Buchak et al., 2018a).1
   In this paper I develop a structural model to quantify the cost of risk-
weighted capital requirements and to study the equilibrium impact of het-
erogeneous leverage regulations on interest rates, concentration and risk in
the mortgage market, which played a central role in the global financial cri-
sis (Mian and Sufi, 2011; Campbell and Cocco, 2015; Corbae and Quintin,
2015). To capture the richness in product differentiation, households’ choices
and lenders’ capital requirements in modern mortgage markets, I take an
approach inspired by the industrial organization literature on differentiated
product demand. I estimate my model using loan-level data on the uni-
verse of mortgage originations in the UK and a new identification strategy
that exploits variation in leverage regulation both across lenders and across
mortgages with differential loan-to-values.2

observed changes in regulations from contemporaneous unobserved changes

are endogenous, and often different regulations are adjusted simultaneously,
making identification more complex (Kovner and Van Tassel, 2018).
   Motivated by the descriptive evidence, I develop a workhorse structural
model that allows me to recover borrowers’ demand elasticities and lenders’
unobservable costs of originating mortgages. On the demand side, I assume
that borrowers have heterogeneous preferences over interest rates and other
characteristics (rate type, lender and maximum leverage) and choose the
mortgage product that gives them the highest utility among their individual-
specific menu. I depart from traditional individual-level discrete-only choice
models and also allow borrowers to choose the loan amount, following the
approach in Dubin and McFadden (1984). The discrete-continuous choice
allows me to decompose the elasticity of demand to the interest rate into a
product elasticity and a loan demand elasticity, both affecting substitution
patterns and lenders’ mark-ups.
   On the supply side, I model lenders as heterogeneous multi-product firms
offering differentiated mortgages and competing on interest rates, subject to
regulatory leverage constraints. I use the elasticity parameters from the de-
mand estimation together with lenders’ Lerner formula for profit-maximizing
prices and additional loan-level data on arrears, to back out unobservable

marginal costs of originating mortgage products.
   I estimate the model using the universe of mortgage originations in the
UK in 2015-2016. The UK represents an ideal laboratory to estimate my
model, given the rich variation in mortgage products and risk-weighted capi-
tal requirements. I estimate the demand side parameters exploiting variation
in product characteristics and borrower choices. I address endogeneity prob-
lems from the simultaneous nature of the discrete-continuous choice and from
omitted variables by instrumenting endogenous rates using pre-determined,
risk-weighted capital requirements as cost-shifters.
   I estimate the supply side parameters using variation in product charac-
teristics that affect marginal costs via default and interest rate risks. Most
notably, I exploit variation in risk-weighted capital requirements both across
lenders and across leverage levels within lender to identify the shadow cost
of capital regulation. Thus, my empirical strategy controls for differences
across lenders that are common among products (lender shocks), and differ-
ences across products that are common across lenders (market shocks).
   The estimated model captures several salient features of the data, such
as market shares across lenders and loan-to-values, and performs well in pre-
dicting these features out of sample using additional data for 2017. On the
demand side, I find an average loan demand elasticity of about 0.07 and a
product demand elasticity of 5.9 across all mortgage products, which strad-
dle the estimates from previous work using different approaches (Fuster and
Zafar, 2015; DeFusco and Paciorek, 2017), and related work using structural
approaches (Buchak et al., 2018b; Robles-Garcia, 2019).
   On the supply side, I find an average markup around 50 basis points,

data, and an average marginal cost of about 2.4 percent. Marginal costs are

   Next, I use the estimated structural model to investigate the equilibrium
effects of counterfactual leverage regulations in the mortgage market. Hold-
ing the estimated demand and marginal cost parameters fixed, I simulate
different leverage regulations, critically allowing lenders and borrowers to
best respond to the new regulations. These counterfactual simulations allow
me to explore the design and equilibrium effects of alternative policies that
are not observed in the data on mortgage rates, market structure and risk.
   In a first set of counterfactuals, I study the effects of removing the “reg-
ulatory risk-weights gap” between large banks and other competitors, moti-
vated by recent proposals to reform capital requirements (Basel Committee
on Banking Supervision, 2016a,b). Most notably, I simulate a counterfactual
in which all lenders calculate risk-weighted capital requirements using the
standardized approach and an alternative counterfactual in which small and
medium lenders also adopt a representative internal rating-based model.
   Imposing a common standardized approach to risk weights increases the
equity buffer of larger lenders and their costs, which are passed on to borrow-
ers with large changes in market shares and decline in both lenders’ profits

   Finally, I explore with the estimated model possible interactions between
capital requirements and limits to household leverage that have recently been
discussed and implemented in some countries (Consumer Financial Protec-
tion Bureau, 2013; Bank of England, 2014; DeFusco et al., 2020; Acharya
et al., 2018). I introduce a maximum loan-to-value limit that removes from
borrowers’ menu all mortgages with a leverage larger than 90 percent, both
in an economy with risk-weighted capital requirements and in a counterfac-
tual economy with homogeneous capital requirements (which was the case
before the financial crisis under Basel I).
   I find that a regulation targeting high loan-to-value can be effective in
reducing borrowers’ defaults, which decline by 3-5 percent, but has a nega-
tive impact on originations and consumer surplus, as first-time buyers value
mortgages with high leverage. Most notably, I show how the interaction of
regulations on borrowers and lenders can have distributional impacts and un-
intended consequences. Abolishing high loan-to-value mortgages has larger
effects on consumers’ surplus and lenders’ profits with homogeneous capi-

   Related literature. This paper contributes to several strands of lit-
erature. First, I provide a new framework to study households’ mortgage
demand, which complements existing approaches in household finance us-

2013) or clever empirical identification strategies (Fuster and Zafar, 2015;
DeFusco and Paciorek, 2017; Best et al., 2020).4 My structural approach

on differentiated product demand systems and on discrete-continuous choice
models (Lancaster, 1979; Dubin and McFadden, 1984; Berry et al., 1995; Hen-
del, 1999; Nevo, 2001; Thomassen et al., 2017). The characteristics approach
captures rich heterogeneity in household preferences and product availability
along several dimensions, which are otherwise hard to model together. The
discrete-continuous approach allows me to decompose the impact of interest
rates on households’ joint choice of lender, leverage and loan size. Within the

               I.     Institutional Setting and Data

A.        Institutional Setting

     While mortgages are extremely important in all countries, the details of
the mortgage system vary widely across countries (Campbell, 2013). Three
key institutional features differentiate the UK mortgage market from mort-
gage markets in the US, Canada and other countries in Continental Europe.
     First, in contrast for example to the US mortgage market, long-term fixed-
rate mortgages are unavailable in the UK (Badarinza et al., 2017; Cloyne
et al., 2019). The typical mortgage contract is characterized by a 25-years
amortization period, but a relatively low interest rate for a short initial pe-
riod, followed by a penalizing reset rate. The initial rate typically has a
duration of 2-5 years and this rate may be either fixed or floating. During
the initial period most contracts have large early repayment charges, typi-
cally 5 or 10 percent of the outstanding loan, which makes refinancing before
the end of the initial period costly.6 After the end of the initial period the
mortgage rate resets to a standard variable rate (SVR). The SVR is always
a floating rate and it is paid until the borrower refinance the loan or until
maturity. Given the high prepayment penalties and the large jump in the
reset rate, most borrowers in the UK refinance around the time when the
initial duration ends, making remortgaging a relatively frequent event in this

                     II.      Motivating Facts

   The UK mortgage market has several institutional features that make
it an excellent laboratory for investigating the relationship between leverage
regulation, mortgage pricing and market structure. In this section I provide a
brief overview of empirical facts that guide my assumptions for the structural
model of Section III and represent some of the main sources of variation used
in the identification strategy described in Section IV.

A.      Leverage Regulation

     The UK market has been at the forefront of policies to regulate both
lenders and households leverage. On the lender side, since the 1990s, the UK
micro-prudential regulator, the Financial Services Authority, has imposed
bank-specific, time-varying minimum capital requirements on the banks un-
der its purview (Aiyar et al., 2014). Additionally, since 2008 two approaches
to calculate risk weights coexist: the Standardized Approach (SA) and the
Internal Rating-Based approach (IRB). Under IRB, banks’ own internal mod-
els are used to estimate risk components such as probabilities of default and
loss given default, which are then used as inputs in the risk weight functions
defined by regulators. The largest six lenders in the UK all adopted IRB
models since 2008 when the capital regulation changed from Basel I to Basel
II. Medium and small lenders, with very few exceptions, opted for the stan-
dardized approach, mostly because of the large fixed compliance cost associ-
ated with IRB models (Competition and Markets Authority, 2015; Benetton
et al., 2016).

                       B.1. Discussion on Magnitude

   The baseline estimate in column (2) of Table V shows an increase in the
marginal cost of about 26 basis points for a one-percentage-point increase in
the risk-weighted capital requirement. Given an average marginal cost of 2.4
percent, marginal cost increases by approximately 11 percent for the average
mortgage product.
   To date there is no consensus on the impact of changes in capital require-
ments on the cost for lenders (Kovner and Van Tassel, 2018). Recent stud-
ies using different identification strategies and assumptions find estimates
ranging from 3 basis points (Kisin and Manela, 2016), to 25-45 basis points
(Hanson et al., 2011), up until 60-90 basis points (Baker and Wurgler, 2015).
To put my estimates of the cost of capital regulation into context, I simulate
an increase in capital requirement by 10-percentage-points, along the lines of



                                        75




          Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3247956
previous studies (Hanson et al., 2011; Firestone et al., 2017).54
    I find that increasing capital requirement by 10-percentage-points in-
creases marginal costs by about 60 basis points in the mortgage market. This
effect lies on the upper end of previous estimates and can be interpreted as an
upper bound to the cost of a common increase in capital requirements. First,
my estimates are not directly comparable because I do not impose a partic-
ular capital structure model or exploit a single observed regulatory change
keeping all else equal. Differently from most previous works on the topic, I
infer the perceived total cost of complying with the regulation only from the
rates lenders charge to mortgage borrowers after changes in risk-weighted
capital requirements. Second, I allow lenders to adjust to the new regulation
only through one margin: repricing mortgages. In reality lenders can lower
deposit rates, issue new equity and retain earnings, especially if the change in
regulation is spread over time. These additional margins of adjustment will
likely decrease the cost of increasing capital requirements (Elliott, 2009).

                         VII.         Conclusion

   Leverage regulation has been at the center of the academic and policy
debate since the global financial crisis and there is an ongoing effort to un-
derstand better the channels through which it operates and to evaluate its
effectiveness. In this paper I focus on leverage regulation in the UK mortgage
market, in which lenders with different capital regulations coexist and limits
to household leverage have been recently introduced. I develop a tractable
equilibrium model of the mortgage market that accounts for several features
characterizing borrowers’ demand and lenders’ competition, and estimate it
with a new identification strategy that exploits variation in risk-weighted
capital requirements both across lenders and within lender across mortgages
with differential loan-to-values.
   I quantify the cost of capital regulation for lenders and find that a one-
percentage-point increase in risk-weighted capital requirements raises lenders’


                                         91




           Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3247956
costs by 26 basis points or 11 percent for the average mortgage product, when
lenders can only react by adjusting their price. With the estimated model
I show that heterogeneous leverage regulation shapes market structure, in
particular removing the policy-driven difference in risk weights can promote
competition without worsening risk. Finally, I find that loan-to-value lim-
its reduce borrowers defaults, but have a large impact on originations and
consumers’ surplus, and can potentially reduce large lenders’ risk-weighted
equity buffers, thus affecting systemic risk.
   My paper can be extended in several directions. The lender problem
can be enlarged to account for the acceptance/rejection margin. This would
allow leverage regulation to affect the loan supply not only through changes in
loan rates, but also through changes in underwriting standards. So far, I have
captured this channel in a reduced form way through affordability constraints
and capital regulation affecting interest rates, as well as unobservable product
characteristics. A more comprehensive model and empirical strategy that
feature both the pricing and the rejection choices would be an interesting
avenue for future research. Moreover, in this paper I focus mostly on the costs
of risk-weighted capital requirements, their transmission on interest rates and
their implications for market structure. It would be interesting to enrich
the framework to account explicitly for endogenous default choices on both
the demand and the supply side. Adding the default option for borrowers
will allow a comprehensive measure of consumers’ surplus; while lenders’
bankruptcy choices will provide an explicit micro-foundation for leverage
regulation and a fully fledged quantification of the trade-offs.

   This section discusses some assumptions in the model, explores extensions
and shows additional derivations that have been omitted from the main text.
On the demand side, I discuss further some empirical facts that support my
assumptions of a static demand model. I then derive the demand elasticities
reported in Section V and the consumer surplus reported in the counterfac-
tual analysis of Section VI. On the supply side, I extend the lender problem
in the model of Section III.B to the case when a fraction of borrowers does
not refinance at the end of the initial period. I also sketch the case in which
lenders choose their risk weights.

   Borrower choice. I fix the interest rate set at origination, which im-
plies that borrowers expect future interest rates to reflect current interest
rates. This assumption holds for fixed rate mortgages until the end of the
initial period and is reasonable for variable rate mortgages, given the short
horizon before remortgaging. Second, I develop a static model, which does
not allow me to study issues related to the timing of purchasing, refinancing
or defaulting. This will complicate the analysis, given that the timing for
these choices will be affected by many additional factors not limited to the
mortgage market (e.g., housing and labor markets). At origination, the “par-
ticipation decision” of buying versus renting can be added as an additional
binary decision in the model, but will require additional data on renters and
an assumption on their targeted unobserved loan-to-value to construct their
counterfactual choice set. After origination, my static model assumption is
supported by the fact that the vast majority of households refinance at the

                                         14




           Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3247956
end of the initial period or shortly thereafter, to avoid paying the significantly
higher reset rate (see Section II.D, Cloyne et al. (2019) and Belgibayeva et al.
(2020)).
