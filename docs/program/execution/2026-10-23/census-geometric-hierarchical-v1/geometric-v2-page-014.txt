We implement the model in NumPyro, a probabilistic programming language, and perform auto-
mated approximate Bayesian inference using MCMC, the No-U Turn Sampler (NUTS) [Phan et al.,
2019, Bingham et al., 2019, Hoffman and Gelman, 2014]. We estimate the effect on the average
outcome of a⋆ = 1 versus a⋆ = 0 by computing the posterior mean and credible intervals for β a
(Equation (39)). Details are in Section C.
    The GCM provides an estimate of the causal effect (Figure 3d). As one comparison, we used a
conventional causal model, a Bayesian linear regression, that ignores interference and autocorrelation
(CM). This corresponds to setting length scales of η = λ = 0 in β a and κy . As another comparison,
we used a geometric model that accounts for spatial dependence but ignores confounding (GM).
This corresponds to setting β x = 0. The GCM provided a more accurate treatment effect, and
a credible interval that covered the true effect (Figure 3d). This performance gain held across 20
simulation configurations, with the GCM achieving lower average error (Figure S1a) and better
calibrated uncertainty (Figure S1b). In summary, GCMs can produce high quality causal effect
estimates by accounting for both non-i.i.d. structure and confounding in the data.

Variations. GCMs can also describe many variations and extensions of this spatial model. The
domain could be one-dimensional, three-dimensional, or higher. The space could be discretized into
a grid, Ω = Zd , rather than continuous. For time series data, we may work with the domain Ω = R+
and the group of shifts T. For spatiotemporal data, the product of space and time, Ω = R2 × R+
[Christiansen et al., 2022]. The probabilistic model used for estimation could also be more flexible,
e.g. using convolutions plus nonlinearities for the equivariant mechanisms f [Bronstein et al., 2021].
