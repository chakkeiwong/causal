where Score(·, ·) measures similarity (e.g., F1 score or another suitable metric).
As in practice, G⋆ is unknown, the objective cannot be optimized directly. We therefore propose to

4     Methods
4.1   Separation of edge existence and orientation

We choose to treat the questions of edge existence and orientation separately, motivated by two key
observations:

Observation 1 (Algorithm orientation ambiguity). In general, even under ideal conditions, it is
only possible to learn a Completed Partially Directed Acyclic Graph (CPDAG) from observational
data, representing a Markov Equivalence Class [Spirtes et al., 2000]. This CPDAG typically contains
some undirected edges for which the orientation cannot be determined from data alone. Furthermore,
given an ensemble of diverse algorithms working with limited data, it is reasonable to expect cases
where multiple algorithms identify that an edge exists while disagreeing on its orientation.

Observation 2 (Expert orientation accuracy). Prior research suggests that LLMs perform quite
well at determining the causal direction between pairs of variables [Kiciman et al., 2023], but can
struggle with distinguishing the existence of a causal relationship from mere correlation [Carro
et al., 2024]. Human experts are similarly subject to biases that result in conflating correlation with
causation [Msetfi et al., 2013, Matute et al., 2015]. Therefore, we only query the expert for edges
detected by the ensemble, not to introduce new edges.
Treating edge existence and orientation separately allows our method to rely more on data-driven
algorithms for detecting edge existence and experts for determining their orientation. Furthermore,
it allows for distinct, targeted strategies when soliciting expert opinions on existence and orientation.

4.2   Expert model averaging

We describe our dynamic expert-guided model averaging strategy below. Detailed pseudocode is
provided in Appendix D.
Inspired by the model averaging strategy of Constantinou [2019a], our method greedily adds edges
that avoid creating cycles by order of most occurrence in the component models G. However, cru-
cially, our method treats the existence of a direct connection separately from the orientation of that
connection: when determining the order in which edges will be added, any direct edge between two
nodes x and y (i.e. x → y, y → x, x − y) is weighed equally towards establishing that there is some
direct connection, irrespective of orientation.
We use a predetermined edge threshold θ1 that determines what percentage of component models
G must contain a connection between x and y for an edge to be added to Ĝ. When the percentage
is above θ1 but below 0.5 (a majority vote), we actively query the expert e to potentially reject the
presence of an edge between x and y.
In the event that only one directed orientation (x → y or y → x) is a valid (non-cycle inducing)
addition, that edge is added to Ĝ. When both directed orientations are valid, we first check if the
                                                        (m)
percentage of models with a particular orientation (Oxy = → or ←) out of the models containing
                        (m)
a direct connection (Zxy = 1) falls above a predetermined orientation threshold θ2 . If so, that
orientation is added to Ĝ. Otherwise, the expert e is actively queried to determine the orientation.
Finally, we include a query budget B, indicating how many queries can be posed to the expert. When
the budget is exhausted, the ensembling defaults to majority voting.
