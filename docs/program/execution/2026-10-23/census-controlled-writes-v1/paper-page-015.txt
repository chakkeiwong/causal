This appendix expands the comparison in Section 2, covering neural representations of logic, sym-
bolic execution, premise selection, and source checking.

A.1   F IRST WAVE : CONNECTIONIST– SYMBOLIC INTEGRATION (1990 S )

KBANN Towell & Shavlik (1994) compiled a propositional Horn rule set into the topology of a
feed-forward network and then refined the rules by backpropagation. It supported both rule inser-
tion and extraction from a neural model. CILP d’Avila Garcez & Zaverucha (1999) showed that
recurrent networks with semi-linear neurons approximate the fixed-point operator of propositional
logic programs, closing a learn–reason–extract cycle with sound extraction Besold et al. (2022).
These systems established ways to encode symbolic rules in neural networks. CPUNeSy builds
on the separation between rules and their neural representation, with additional checks on source
premises and their storage.

A.2   S TATISTICAL RELATIONAL LEARNING AND PROBABILISTIC LOGICS (2000 S )

Markov logic networks Richardson & Domingos (2006) softened first-order clauses with weights;
ProbLog De Raedt et al. (2007) gave logic programs a distribution semantics. These methods rep-
resent uncertainty over logical statements. Their use of probabilistic semantics addresses a different
aspect of inference from the source-admission checks studied here.

A.3   D IFFERENTIABLE DEDUCTIVE ENGINES (2016–2022)

TensorLog Cohen et al. (2020) compiled Datalog-style inference into differentiable matrix alge-
bra; end-to-end differentiable proving Rocktäschel & Riedel (2017) replaced unification by vec-
tor similarity; ∂ILP Evans & Grefenstette (2018) learned rules by gradient descent; DeepProbLog
Manhaeve et al. (2018) introduced neural predicates over probabilistic facts; NeurASP Yang et al.
(2020) embedded networks into answer-set programs; SLASH Skryagin et al. (2022) combined
neural predicates with probabilistic circuits. These works made predicate-level processing train-
able. The learned predicates connect neural predictions to logical inference. Reasoning shortcuts
can arise when those predictions fit the task labels while misrepresenting the intended concepts
(Appendix A.7).
