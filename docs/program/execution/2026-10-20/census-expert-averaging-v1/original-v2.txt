                                                                                        Abstract

                                                    Would-be practitioners of causal discovery face a dizzying array of algorithms
                                                    without a clear best choice. This abundance of competitive methods makes ensem-
                                                    bling a natural strategy for practical applications. At the same time, real-world use
                                                    cases frequently violate the assumptions on which common causal discovery algo-
                                                    rithms are based, forcing reliance on expert knowledge. Inspired by recent work
                                                    on dynamically requested expert knowledge and large language models (LLMs)
                                                    as experts, we present a flexible model averaging method that integrates selective
                                                    expert querying to ensemble a diverse set of causal discovery algorithms. Cru-
                                                    cially, we distinguish between edge existence and orientation, enabling the method
                                                    to leverage the complementary strengths of data-driven discovery and expert in-
                                                    put. We further consider the realistic setting of limited access to an imperfect ex-
                                                    pert, using disagreement among algorithms to query the expert in cases of greater
                                                    uncertainty. Experiments demonstrate that our method consistently outperforms
                                                    strong baselines on both clean and noisy data. Code and data are available at
                                                    https://anonymous.4open.science/r/expert-cd-ensemble-3282/.


                                        1    Introduction

                                        Applications across many domains require not just accurate predictive models, but a robust under-
                                        standing of the underlying causal mechanisms. Probabilistic graphical models such as Bayesian
                                        networks (BNs) provide a principled framework for representing causal relationships, offering in-
                                        herent interpretability and capacity for counterfactual and interventional reasoning [Pearl, 2009,
                                        Koller and Friedman, 2009]. Causal discovery aims to learn causal relations between variables from
                                        observational data, revealing the underlying structure of complex systems [Spirtes et al., 2000].
                                        Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are the gold standard for determining causal relationships, but
                                        conducting RCTs is often costly and difficult, if not impossible. Causal discovery can point the way
                                        towards the most useful trials to perform and provide insight in cases where trials are infeasible.
                                        However, would-be practitioners of causal discovery face a great diversity of methods, with no clear
                                        indicators of which method is the best for their use-case [Kitson et al., 2023]. This, combined with
                                        the realities of limited, noisy data, forces heavy reliance on expert knowledge in practice [Vonk
                                        et al., 2023, Mäkelä et al., 2022].
                                        We therefore seek to provide a general-purpose method for ensembling in causal discovery that
                                        can flexibly incorporate a diverse array of learning algorithms with expert domain knowledge. Our
                                        primary contributions are threefold:

                                                • We provide a model averaging strategy that uses dynamically requested expert knowledge
                                                  to mediate discrepancies between models. To our knowledge, our method is the first to
                                                  explore the use of expert knowledge for model averaging or ensembling in causal discovery.

         query the expert in cases of greater disagreement between models, seeking to maximize the
         value of limited access to an imperfect expert.

2     Related Work
2.1   Assumptions and data noise

The theoretical bases for many causal discovery algorithms rely on assumptions that are frequently
impossible to verify in practice, such as causal sufficiency (the absence of latent confounders). A
plethora of algorithms exist that attempt to address different possible assumption violations. For
example, the well-known FCI algorithm [Spirtes et al., 2000] works in the presence of latent con-
founders and selection bias [Spirtes et al., 2013]; the SAT-based approach of Hyttinen et al. [2013]
handles latent confounders and cyclicity; and the hill-climbing based approach of Liu and Constanti-
nou [2022] is shown to improve performance in the face of missing data, including data Missing Not
At Random. However, such approaches typically only address one or two assumptions while leav-
ing others untouched. Furthermore, analysis of the performance of well-known causal discovery
algorithms under data noise that violates various common assumptions reveals inconsistencies in
algorithm performance across metrics and noise types [Constantinou et al., 2021]. As no algorithm
consistently outperforms all others on real-world data, we opt for an ensembling approach that lever-
ages the potential advantages of a multitude of algorithms.

2.2   Ensembling in causal discovery

The performance of individual causal discovery algorithms can vary widely and unpredictably [Kit-
son et al., 2023], making the improved robustness provided by ensembling [Zhou, 2025] a natural


                                                  2
choice for applying causal discovery in practical settings. However, to our knowledge, little research
has been done exploring the best methods for performing ensembling or model averaging in causal
discovery. Constantinou [2019a] provides a method for model averaging that greedily adds edges
by order of the most counts, avoiding cycles as the graph is constructed. Aslani and Mohebbi [2023]
treat ensembling as an optimization problem, using a distance measure between graphs to maxi-
mize the marginal contribution of the edges in the final graph. Khatibi et al. [2024] present their
strongest results from a weighted ensemble of existing causal discovery algorithms. However, their
approach relies on access to unrealistic metadata about the ground-truth causal graph that would not
be available in realistic settings, and we therefore do not consider this scenario.

2.3   Expert knowledge

Prior work has typically focused on including expert knowledge as prior constraints [Constanti-
nou et al., 2023]. A recent contribution from Kitson and Constantinou [2025] explored using
dynamically-requested expert knowledge in the score-based tabu algorithm. Unlike our approach,
their method is specifically intended for use with a human expert, whereas our approach permits
a human or large language model (LLM) expert, and evaluation largely focused on a setting with
clean data and a perfect (simulated) expert.
There has also been much interest in using LLMs as experts to enable causal discovery [Liu et al.,
2025]. Vashishtha et al. [2025] present a strong case for restricting LLMs to answering causal
ordering questions, and provide a method for prompting LLMs to obtain a causal order over a whole
graph, to be used as prior constraints. Khatibi et al. [2024] introduce a general framework for using
an LLM to refine the output of a causal discovery algorithm. In line with the dynamic knowledge
method of Kitson and Constantinou [2025], Tao et al. [2025] use active requests to an LLM to
augment continuous optimization algorithms (e.g. Zheng et al. [2018]’s NOTEARS).
Inspired by these contributions, we explore the use of imperfect experts (such as LLMs) to dynam-
ically answer causal ordering questions, improving model averaging across an ensemble of varied
causal discovery algorithms. Unlike previous methods, we explicitly use expert knowledge to medi-
ate the ensembling process.

method on these formal assumptions that are often violated in practical settings [Montagna et al.,

vating an expert-guided ensembling approach for causal discovery. Given these assumptions and the
deliberate involvement of an (imperfect) expert, we correspondingly rest our method on empirical
validation rather than formal guarantees of performance in the infinite data limit.

3.3    Objective

Given a set of learned graphs G = {G1 , . . . , GM } and a limited query budget B for accessing an
imperfect expert, the goal is to learn a graph Ĝ that is as close as possible to the unknown ground-
truth graph G⋆ .

4     Methods
4.1   Separation of edge existence and orientation

We choose to treat the questions of edge existence and orientation separately, motivated by two key
observations:

Observation 1 (Algorithm orientation ambiguity). In general, even under ideal conditions, it is
only possible to learn a Completed Partially Directed Acyclic Graph (CPDAG) from observational
data, representing a Markov Equivalence Class [Spirtes et al., 2000]. This CPDAG typically contains
some undirected edges for which the orientation cannot be determined from data alone. Furthermore,
given an ensemble of diverse algorithms working with limited data, it is reasonable to expect cases
where multiple algorithms identify that an edge exists while disagreeing on its orientation.

Observation 2 (Expert orientation accuracy). Prior research suggests that LLMs perform quite
well at determining the causal direction between pairs of variables [Kiciman et al., 2023], but can
struggle with distinguishing the existence of a causal relationship from mere correlation [Carro
et al., 2024]. Human experts are similarly subject to biases that result in conflating correlation with
causation [Msetfi et al., 2013, Matute et al., 2015]. Therefore, we only query the expert for edges
detected by the ensemble, not to introduce new edges.
Treating edge existence and orientation separately allows our method to rely more on data-driven
algorithms for detecting edge existence and experts for determining their orientation. Furthermore,
it allows for distinct, targeted strategies when soliciting expert opinions on existence and orientation.

4.2   Expert model averaging

We describe our dynamic expert-guided model averaging strategy below. Detailed pseudocode is
provided in Appendix D.
Inspired by the model averaging strategy of Constantinou [2019a], our method greedily adds edges
that avoid creating cycles by order of most occurrence in the component models G. However, cru-
cially, our method treats the existence of a direct connection separately from the orientation of that
connection: when determining the order in which edges will be added, any direct edge between two
nodes x and y (i.e. x → y, y → x, x − y) is weighed equally towards establishing that there is some
direct connection, irrespective of orientation.
We use a predetermined edge threshold θ1 that determines what percentage of component models
G must contain a connection between x and y for an edge to be added to Ĝ. When the percentage
is above θ1 but below 0.5 (a majority vote), we actively query the expert e to potentially reject the
presence of an edge between x and y.
In the event that only one directed orientation (x → y or y → x) is a valid (non-cycle inducing)
addition, that edge is added to Ĝ. When both directed orientations are valid, we first check if the
                                                        (m)
percentage of models with a particular orientation (Oxy = → or ←) out of the models containing
                        (m)
a direct connection (Zxy = 1) falls above a predetermined orientation threshold θ2 . If so, that
orientation is added to Ĝ. Otherwise, the expert e is actively queried to determine the orientation.
Finally, we include a query budget B, indicating how many queries can be posed to the expert. When
the budget is exhausted, the ensembling defaults to majority voting.

queries on the basis of potentially indirect causal ordering. Our simulated expert therefore provides
answers based on presence of some chain x → . . . → y rather than merely a direct edge x → y.
From the perspective of graphical accuracy, a correct causal ordering answer from our simulated
expert may therefore not result in a correct edge in the final graph (i.e. if there is a chain but no
edge between x and y in G⋆ , when answering “correctly” our expert will not block an edge between
them). We choose to take this approach to better reflect the limitations of eliciting accurate expert
knowledge in practice [Vashishtha et al., 2025]. We analyze how our approach performs when using
a simulated expert with access to knowledge of direct edges in Appendix I, finding as expected that
such an expert provides better precision but identical recall. Finally, we note that when orientation
is queried for two variables with no causal relation in G⋆ , an arbitrary orientation is returned.

6   Limitations
Our work has several important limitations. Our method provides no formal guarantees of recovering
the true causal graph under ideal settings. We instead aim to combine the strengths of diverse
causal discovery algorithms with an imperfect expert under plausible real-world data conditions,
which may violate the assumptions of individual algorithms. We must therefore rely on empirical
validation. Furthermore, our experiments involve only synthetic, categorical data. While our model
averaging approach is general and should be applicable to other types of data (e.g. continuous or
mixed), provided appropriate choices are made for the component algorithms, this is not yet verified
empirically. Finally, while we include experiments on noisy data, the synthetic noise we use cannot
be expected to fully cover all real-world conditions.
As an ensembling approach, our method inherits the worst computational complexity of any com-     
ponent algorithm. If considering only the model averaging, the worst-case complexity is O N 3 ,
where N is the number of nodes, as every potential edge must be considered (N 2 ) along with a
cyclicity check (N ). Here we assume the cyclicity check is optimized with an almost-linear time
algorithm for incremental graphs [Chen et al., 2024], although we do not include this optimization
in our current implementation as we find that the runtime in practice is dominated by the latency of
querying an expert (e.g. an LLM). The expected runtime is therefore effectively Θ(L ∗ B), where
L is the average query latency and B is the query budget. In turn, the runtime of the ensembling
process is dominated by the component algorithms.

7   Conclusion
We provide a flexible approach for ensembling in causal discovery using dynamically requested
expert knowledge. We distinguish between edge existence and orientation and selectively query
an (imperfect) expert to resolve disagreement between algorithms. Experiments demonstrate the
improvements of our approach over both ensembles without expert knowledge and individual algo-
rithms using dynamically requested knowledge, on both clean and noisy data. To our knowledge,
this study constitutes the first method for leveraging expert knowledge in the ensembling of causal
discovery algorithms.
Future work can address certain limitations mentioned in Section 6 by employing our method on
different data types and noise distributions, including on real data. Furthermore, while the current
approach effectively gives every model equal weight during averaging, it may be possible to adapt
the weights given to different models, based on the predicted performance of different individual
algorithms. Additional possibilities include using our model averaging method across multiple runs
of the same chosen algorithm rather than across an ensemble of disparate algorithms, providing
improved stability (for non-deterministic algorithms or algorithms sensitive to variable ordering) or
efficiency (across different data partitions). Finally, while to our knowledge this work is the first to
explore the use of dynamic expert knowledge for model averaging, we foresee great potential for
future exploration along this avenue.

B     Assumptions

Below we discuss standard assumptions in causal discovery and provide more detail on several of
our assumptions, including empirical analysis.

B.1   Standard causal discovery assumptions

Common assumptions in causal discovery include:

Causal Markov condition. Let G⋆ be the ground-truth DAG over variables V, and let P denote
the joint distribution over V. The distribution P satisfies the Causal Markov condition with respect
to G⋆ if every d-separation in G⋆ implies a corresponding conditional independence in P :
                               Vi ⊥
                                  ⊥G⋆ Vj | S =⇒ Vi ⊥⊥P Vj | S,
for all disjoint Vi , Vj ∈ V and S ⊆ V \ {Vi , Vj }.The Causal Markov condition provides that d-
separations in the causal graph are reflected by independencies in the observed data.

Faithfulness. The distribution P is faithful to G⋆ if every conditional independence in P corre-
sponds to a d-separations in G⋆ . Formally:
                               Vi ⊥
                                  ⊥P Vj | S =⇒ Vi ⊥⊥G⋆ Vj | S,
for all disjoint Vi , Vj ∈ V and S ⊆ V \ {Vi , Vj }. Faithfulness provides that independencies in the
observed data are reflected by d-separations in the causal graph.

Observability (causal sufficiency). All common causes of the observed variables are themselves
observed - i.e., there are no latent confounders.

Acyclicity. The ground-truth causal graph G⋆ is a directed acyclic graph (DAG), i.e., it contains
no directed cycles.
Under these assumptions, many existing algorithms can be shown to recover the CPDAG corre-
sponding to the Markov equivalence class of the ground-truth DAG in the infinite data limit.

B.2   Algorithm competence

Condorcet’s jury theorem [de Caritat Condorcet, 1785] provides that if a group votes on a binary
outcome such that one outcome is correct and each voter has some independent probability p > 21
of voting correctly, the probability of the correct outcome being the result of a majority vote ap-
proaches infinity as the group increases in size. Treating the existence of a given edge as a binary
classification problem, the result trivially extends to the full graph. However, in practice the votes
of the algorithms in the ensemble cannot be expected to be independent, as the algorithms receive
the same observational data as input and operate under similar principles (typically conditional in-
dependence tests and/or a likelihood-based scoring function). The effectiveness of majority voting
diminishes as the correlation between votes rises [Ladha, 1995], suggesting as diverse a group of
algorithms as possible (e.g. constraint-based, score-based, and hybrid methods) should be used as
the basis of the ensemble.
Given some correlation will doubtless remain regardless of the diversity in chosen algorithms, the
performance of the ensemble will not improve arbitrarily with the addition of further algorithms.
The relative value of adding further algorithms will diminish after a certain point, depending on the
degree of correlation; how this behaves in practice may be an interesting topic for future research.
In a sense, the use of an expert diversifies the ensemble far more than simply adding additional
data-driven algorithms can be expected to. Furthermore, the expert may make our method robust to
minor violations of Assumption 1 (algorithm competence). Intuitively, if an algorithm that performs
worse than random may nonetheless uncover some true edges not otherwise detected by a majority
vote of the ensemble. Our method provides a path for these true edges to be added to the final model,
provided the expert approves their existence.

F     LLM case study

F.1   Setup

In addition to a simulated expert, we conduct experiments with an LLM expert, using OpenAI’s gpt-
5-nano-2025-08-07 for all experiments. While we average all results across 20 seeds (consistent with
our other experiments), calls to the LLM were cached across seeds and sample sizes for cost saving
purposes, such that only one call to each of acceptConnection and determineOrientation is
made for the same variables. To reduce ordering sensitivity, we query the LLM with both possible
argument orders, and use the LLM’s choice only if it is consistent across both orders; otherwise, we
fall back to a majority vote among the ensemble models G. We use our standard hyperparameter
settings: θ1 = 0.0, θ2 = 0.9, B = 2N .
We exclude two networks from the case study: the Pathfinder network due to the lack of semantically
meaningful variable names, and the Formed network due to a high number of diagnostic rather than
causal edges (e.g. Violence → Age, ViolentConvictions → Violence). We also test only
the clean versions of the networks. The structure of the noisy versions of the networks differs only
by the inclusion of latent confounders, which are not directly considered in the current prompts, as
we believe handling latent confounders with expert input deserves targeted experiment design—as
such, it poses an interesting avenue for future work.

Algorithm 1: Expert Model Averaging
Input: a set of causal models G, edge threshold θ1 , orientation threshold θ2 , query budget B,
        and expert e
Output: Ĝ, the final model
C ← connectionCounts(G);
n ← size(G);
Ĝ ← DAG();
q ← 0;
for x, y, c in sorted(C) do
    if c/n ≥ θ1 and (c/n > 0.5 or (B > q and e.acceptConnection(x, y))) then
        if c/n ≤ 0.5 then
             q += 1;
        xy ← not Ĝ.edgeCreatesCycle(x, y);
        yx ← not Ĝ.edgeCreatesCycle(y, x);
        if xy and yx then
             if edgeCounts(G, x, y)/c ≥ θ2 then
                 Ĝ.addEdge(x, y);
             else if edgeCounts(G, y, x)/c ≥ θ2 then
                 Ĝ.addEdge(y, x);
             else if B > q then
                 Ĝ.addEdge( e.determineOrientation(x, y) );
                 q += 1;
             end
        else if xy then
             Ĝ.addEdge(x, y);
        else if yx then
             Ĝ.addEdge(y, x);
        end
end
return Ĝ;
