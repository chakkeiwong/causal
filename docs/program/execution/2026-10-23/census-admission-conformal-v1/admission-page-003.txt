Propagation and benchmarks. Multi-agent systems adopt false beliefs collectively and consolidate them
into memory (Xu et al., 2026), harm amplifies under perturbation and ideas spread through interaction
alone (Rahman et al., 2026; Papadopoulos et al., 2026), majorities can correct misinformed agents (Becker
et al., 2026), judge panels err together (Kohli, 2026), and belief-based memory helps most under conflict
(Singh, 2026). Risk-weighted gating, abstention and stale inherited constraints are studied without shared
memory (Iyer and Babu, 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Nakayashiki, 2026). Memory benchmarks test retention and
retrieval by one writer (Maharana et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025), conflict benchmarks how
one model resolves contradictions (Su et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024; Xiang et al., 2026), and belief revision,
partial observability and claim-level correction have their own lines (Park, 2026; Liao et al., 2026; Kong
et al., 2026). None executes admission in a running team with logged shared-memory access and measured
adoption.


3     Method

3.1   Problem formulation

CPB measures admission to shared memory and its consequences, Figure 1. A team A shares beliefs St
at round t. Each agent observes its assigned sources, retrieves St and emits candidate claims, each candi-
date m with evidence Em . An admission policy π maps ⟨m, Em , St ⟩ to PROMOTE, REQUEST EVIDENCE,
KEEP PRIVATE or ABSTAIN , and only PROMOTE changes the store, making the claim retrievable by every
agent from the next round.
Candidates are processed in a fixed agent order within each round. Every policy receives the same scheduled
sources, but later candidates differ because agents retrieve different admitted beliefs. The policy sees neither
claim truth nor its future readers.
