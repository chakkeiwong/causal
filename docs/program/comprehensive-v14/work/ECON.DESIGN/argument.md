# Decision value and the cost of resolving uncertainty

The proposal should justify the next investment by the decision it could change. A public benchmark may establish that an implementation is plausible and identify failures cheaply. It cannot establish the return on deployment at a particular bank. The economic design therefore separates an illustrative investment calculation, a reproducible prototype comparison and a conditional bank experiment. Only the first is calculated here; all numerical assumptions are labeled.

Let a task be a decision opportunity with a fixed population, information cutoff, feasible actions and outcome horizon. The comparator must be the best available proposed baseline under the same information and resource budget: a documented manual process, a deterministic template or an existing package. Its actions and failure policy must be frozen before evaluation. Comparing an agent allowed additional information with a baseline denied it measures the information difference as well as the method; such an ablation needs its own interpretation.

For task j, let U_j(a) denote the monetary utility of action a, including the financial consequence of errors. Define the incremental gross decision utility D_j = U_j(a_new) - U_j(a_base). Utility is not an accuracy score multiplied by an invented price. It requires a declared value model and, for a real causal policy claim, an identified comparison or justified randomized design. A correct refusal may select the baseline action and have D_j=0, while still incurring computation or review costs. A misleading confident recommendation may have negative D_j. Every offered task enters the denominator, including unavailable outputs, reversions to manual work and losing cases.

For a one-year scenario, define incremental net value V = N E[D] - F - N c - R. N is the number of offered tasks, F is fixed incremental setup and operating cost, c is variable incremental cost per attempted task, and R is an additional risk cost not already included in D. Avoid double counting loss in both D and R. Hard constraints on unacceptable actions remain constraints; willingness to pay is not a substitute for satisfying them. Longer horizons need declared discounting, adoption schedules, obsolescence and a terminal-value policy.

The cost ledger separates public-data acquisition and cleaning, later bank extraction and secure access, identity/label audits, model tokens and tool calls, analyst formulation and review, integration and maintenance, compute, storage, platform operation, incident recovery and independent validation. Each row has unit, quantity, unit price, price date/version, source, uncertainty and whether it is incremental to the baseline. Every actual price is currently null. The numerical illustration fills a separate assumed-price column; it does not quietly populate the actual-price column. Local runtime cannot be converted to saved labor without a measured workflow and a validated substitution assumption.

To compare methods on a generated or public task suite, freeze the task manifest and all-request denominator. Use identical tasks, allowed information, loss function and failure handling. Retain paired differences, not merely separate average scores. Report per-journey failures, harmful false confidence, useful refusals, latency and resource use. Uncertainty should respect the true repeated unit: task family, institution or history, rather than treating paraphrases or repeated observations as independent. When causal truth is unknown, a public observational study supplies diagnostics and conditional estimates, not an observed decision-value ground truth. Known generated truth supports only the declared generating regime.

The numerical example uses N=10,000 tasks, assumed fixed incremental cost of USD 100,000 and assumed variable incremental cost of USD 6 per attempted task. Three possible regimes have gross gain per task of USD 40, USD 20 and minus USD 2. Incremental net values are therefore USD 240,000, USD 40,000 and minus USD 180,000. The assumed regime probabilities are 0.4, 0.4 and 0.2. Expected value without further information is USD 76,000. This is an assumed scenario, not a recommendation supported by bank evidence.

Suppose a proposed experiment produces a positive signal with probabilities 0.8, 0.5 and 0.1 in those regimes. Its positive-signal probability is 0.54. Bayes' rule gives expected net value of approximately USD 150,370 after a positive signal and minus USD 11,304 after a negative signal. Building only after a positive signal gives expected value USD 81,200 before paying for the experiment. The expected value of sample information is USD 5,200. At cost USD 8,000 the experiment loses USD 2,800 in expected information value; at cost USD 3,000 it gains USD 2,200 under the same assumed informativeness. A positive expected deployment value does not, by itself, justify an expensive experiment. Perfect information is worth at most USD 36,000 in this example, giving an upper bound on justified information spending under these assumptions.

The signal likelihoods are assumptions, just as the economic gains are. A prototype can help narrow them by exposing which methods fail, at what rate, and in which regimes. It cannot certify the bank-specific mixture of those regimes. The proposal should vary task volume, achievable gain, adoption, review burden, integration cost, tail loss and signal informativeness jointly; break-even curves are more useful than an unsupported point ROI. In the illustration, holding other inputs fixed, average gross gain must exceed USD 16 per attempted task to break even. Lower volume raises the fixed-cost contribution per task. Additional tail risk or required analyst review lowers value rather than disappearing from the accounting.

The conditional next experiment is selected only after a prototype identifies a consequential unresolved assumption. State the two decisions it distinguishes, the target population and horizon, expected signal distributions, losses from wrong decisions, available cheaper public/generated alternatives, proposed sample/precision and total experiment cost. Bank data and owner participation are requested at that point. Stop or narrow the proposal when no feasible experiment has plausible positive net information value, or when a hard constraint rules out the candidate regardless of average value. This is an evidence-seeking decision rule; it does not require a successful product claim to finish a scholarly proposal.

Author review: the calculation retains the adverse regime, treats information cost separately, uses the best action after each signal, and keeps the negative signal from being averaged into a mandatory deployment. No actual bank price, financial benefit, customer outcome or adoption is claimed. The calculator and JSON output reproduce the stated arithmetic. Empirical likelihoods, task value, actual prices, risk bounds and a justified sample design remain later evidence requirements for the particular decision, rather than global blockers of proposal writing.
