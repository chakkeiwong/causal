   3. Estimation. Based on the finite observational data we have at hand, we estimate the effect
      ψ(p(xVobs )) and quantify our remaining uncertainty.

   We extend this logic to GCMs.

   1. Observation. With a complete sample from the observational distribution, xVobs ∼ p(xVobs ),
      we could learn the underlying distribution.

   2. Identification. From the observational distribution and the model, we compute the effect of
      an intervention, ψ(p(xVobs )).

   3. Estimation. In practice we have finite observational data, meaning we observe only part of
      xVobs . Based on this data, we estimate ψ(p(xVobs )) and quantify uncertainty.

    What justifies this reasoning, and when can it be applied? Here, we summarize the theoretical
results from Section 6. Then we illustrate how to apply the machinery in practice.

5.1   Theory Overview
Observation. To learn a distribution from a single sample, xVobs ∼ p(xVobs ), it must have some
underlying regularity. This is captured mathematically by the notion of ergodicity, meaning intu-
itively that a single realization of the system reveals its underlying laws (Definition 11) [Sarig, 2023].
We show that GCMs are ergodic under general conditions, namely when the domain Ω and group G
are infinite, and the group’s action mixes elements of the domain (Assumption 2). Thanks to GCMs’
ergodicity, we can infer p(xVobs ) from a single infinite sample, xVobs ∼ p(xVobs ) (Proposition 2).

Identification. We can use do-calculus for identification in GCMs [Pearl, 2009]. Do-calculus
computes causal effects without making assumptions about the parametric form of the model.
Though built for the usual i.i.d. settings, it ports to GCMs unchanged (Proposition 3). We calculate
ψ(·) from the causal graph using standard do-calculus, then plug in the structured data distribution
p(xVobs ) to obtain the GCM’s effect. This works because GCMs follow a Markov decomposition,
just like usual i.i.d. models. Note this particular identification strategy assumes the causal graph
and the symmetry group are both known.

Estimation. Finally we estimate the effect ψ(p(xVobs )) from data. In practice we cannot observe
all of xVobs . Instead, we have a finite dataset, obtained by measuring a subset of the domain A ⊆ Ω.
    For estimation, the model must satisfy positivity, i.e. the intervention must have non-zero
probability. To enable positivity we assume finite interference: the value of xvω can only depend
on a finite number of other indices ω ′ in the causal parents of v (Assumption 3). This localized
dependence ensures the intervention can occur repeatedly without parametric restrictions on the
model.
    To guarantee consistent estimates, we employ Lindenstrauss’s ergodic theorem, an extension of
the law of large numbers to ergodic distributions with general group symmetries [Lindenstrauss,
2001, Austern and Orbanz, 2022]. It requires the group is amenable, meaning intuitively it contains
a stably increasing sequence of subsets (Assumption 4). It implies estimates converge to the true
ψ(p(xVobs )) as we observe more data, i.e. as A grows (Proposition 7).
    For uncertainty quantification, Bayesian methods are a natural choice. The reason is that if
we assume the data distribution is invariant, this implies the existence of a prior: the ergodic
decomposition theorem says the data must be generated as
