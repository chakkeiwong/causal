When a random invitation still
leaves selective recovery

A bank’s plan to recover missing customer histories can fail at the follow-up stage. A bank may
instead invite a random sample of customers whose records are incomplete and receive usable
evidence from only some of them. Customers with a resolved dispute may reply more readily
than those still in difficulty; a collections call can itself alter a later repayment. Randomizing
the invitation does not make the completed responses representative, nor does it make a later
outcome equal to the historical outcome the bank intended to recover. Both distinctions
change the conclusion that the bank can draw from the follow-up expense.
   Consider a simpler decision with a genuinely bounded endpoint. A servicing offer is
randomized before a fixed repayment date. The endpoint is whether the specified payment was
made by that date, and the target is the average effect of the offer in the experimental customer
population. The bank later tries to recover the historical payment status from incomplete
records. This example concerns a binary historical outcome and a single treatment. It does not
impose artificial finite bounds on unbounded Gaussian servicing rewards or claim to recover a
longitudinal policy value from a terminal indicator.


1.1       The information supplied by an invitation
Let A ∈ {0, 1} denote randomized treatment and Y (a) ∈ {0, 1} the historical outcome under
assignment a. Consistency and no interference give Y = Y (A); random assignment identifies
the population contrast τ = E{Y (1) − Y (0)} from the two full arm means if those outcomes
were observed. Let R indicate that routine records contain Y . For customers with R = 0, let
B indicate an invitation and let S indicate whether the specified follow-up would recover an
outcome if that customer were invited. An uninvited customer’s S is not observed. The design
requires
             B ⊥ (Y, S) | A = a, R = 0,     Pr(B = 1 | A = a, R = 0) = qa > 0.
The invitation probabilities are known. The outcome among completed follow-ups must be the
same historical Y , not a new measurement of a changed endpoint. Neither routine response
nor follow-up completion is assumed independent of Y .

Random invitation identifies za and ma through the observed conditional distribution of
follow-up response and outcomes among invitees. It does not identify the outcome mean in the
remaining mass. Binary support consequently gives arm bounds ℓa = va + za and ua = ℓa + ma ,
and effect bounds

                  I = [τL , τU ] = [ℓ1 − u0 , u1 − ℓ0 ],        τU − τL = m1 + m0 .                  (1.1)

This is the binary specialization of the double-sampling bounds developed by Coppock et al.
(2017). Their outcome-stability assumption permits arbitrary differences between initial
respondents and nonrespondents. It restricts the meaning of the recovered outcome, not the
selectivity of the initial response.
   The bounds are attainable within the declared model. Assign outcome zero to every
unresolved treated customer and one to every unresolved control customer to obtain the lower
endpoint; reverse those assignments for the upper endpoint. These assignments leave the
routine observations, invitation law, completion probabilities and observed follow-up outcomes
unchanged. The joint coupling of the two potential outcomes is otherwise unrestricted, so
the arm completions can coexist in a population. This establishes sharpness relative to these
observed data and assumptions; extra restrictions on outcomes or selection would define a
different model.
  Holding the follow-up procedure fixed, changing a positive invitation probability does not
change ma or the population identified set. It changes how precisely the available follow-up law
can be estimated. A more effective recovery procedure can reduce ma and hence identification
width. At qa = 0, the follow-up law is not identified in that arm, so the conclusion cannot
be extrapolated to no invitations. If increasing invitations changes staff attention, response
behavior or outcomes, it also changes the procedure; the fixed-law comparison no longer
applies.

   For completeness, the argument needs no estimated variance. If a random variable has range
length c, the second derivative of its centered log-moment-generating function is its variance
under exponential tilting. That variance is at most c2 /4: the squared distance from the range
midpoint is bounded by c2 /4, and the variance is no larger. Integrating twice from zero gives
a log-moment bound of s2 c2 /8. Independence then bounds either centered endpoint estimate’s
log moment by s2 D/8. Exponential Markov inequality, minimized at s = 4t/D, bounds either
adverse tail by exp(−2t2 /D). Applying this to overestimation of the lower endpoint and
underestimation of the upper endpoint, and summing the two failure probabilities, proves (1.3).
Dependence between the two endpoint estimates is allowed; independence of the customers
used in the product argument is not optional. Intersection with [−1, 1] preserves coverage
because the population identified interval is inside that support.

and a 1.96 multiplier on each outward standard error. Their behavior is empirical here; the
preceding finite-sample guarantee does not transfer to them. The original article’s Imbens–
Manski construction instead targets asymptotic coverage of the true effect, which is a different
criterion from simultaneous containment of both endpoints. A software label such as confidence
interval around the region does not erase this distinction. (Coppock et al., 2017, 2026)


1.3     Selective completion can reverse a point conclusion
The retained generated experiment has historical arm means .45 and .55, so the true effect
is .10. Routine observation probabilities, indexed by outcome zero and one, are (.8, .4) in
control and (.4, .8) in treatment. Both arms therefore have routine response probability .62,
but their observed success means differ sharply. Equal response rates do not imply equal
selection. The routine-only complete-case contrast is approximately .4194, while routine-only
worst-case bounds are [−.12, .64].
   Three stable recovery procedures vary successful completion among invited nonresponders.
The standard procedure has probabilities (.1, .9) in control and (.9, .2) in treatment; the
intensive procedure has (.8, .98) and (.98, .8) respectively; complete recovery has probability
one in both arms. These probabilities define the generated truth. The estimator is not given
the outcome-dependent selection law or the true arm means.

1.4     A recovered record can concern the wrong outcome

for half of the originally unsuccessful, routinely missing treated customers. It leaves control
outcomes unchanged. This deliberately violates historical-outcome stability. The apparent
identified point becomes .235 although the historical treatment effect remains .10. Inviting
more customers now estimates the wrong contrast more precisely.

1.5     The information purchase and its alternatives
The available implementation is a serious comparator for bounded randomized experiments
with the declared follow-up design. Its sample-ratio estimator, asymptotic variance and
poststratification should be compared under matched sampling rules and targets. The proposed
inverse-invitation calculation makes known design probabilities and a conservative finite-sample
guarantee explicit, but its crossed endpoints and wide intervals are costs of that choice. Neither
route validates the other merely because their population limits agree. The package declares
GPL-3; a proposed integration must retain its version, applicable terms and dependency record.
No enabled integration or measured runtime advantage is claimed. (Coppock et al., 2026)
   Other options change the information or assumptions. A reliable administrative linkage
may recover the fixed historical outcome more credibly than a later customer report. An
intensive protocol can reduce the unresolved mass, while more invitations to the same protocol
mainly improve precision. A model that assumes completion is ignorable conditional on
recorded covariates can support a point estimate, but it must justify that assumption and
diagnose overlap. The package’s trimming option instead targets customers whose outcomes
would be observed under either treatment, with selection assumptions explicitly chosen. That
always-observed target can be useful, but it is not the full experimental-population effect in this
section. Equal observed response rates alone do not establish individual response monotonicity
or justify changing the target without saying so. (Coppock et al., 2026)
   To connect identification with a financial decision, stipulate a benefit of USD 100 for each
additional historical repayment and an offer cost of USD 5 per customer. These invented values
define net benefit 100τ − 5, not observed bank returns. The standard-recovery population
set permits net values from USD −13.70 to USD 10.40. Intensive recovery narrows this to
USD 0.60 through USD 6.08; complete stable recovery gives USD 5.00. Those are population
implications. A finite-sample spending recommendation needs its own nonempty net-benefit
confidence set above zero, and follow-up costs must also be counted. A positive effect alone is
insufficient to clear the USD 5 threshold.
   At full invitation under intensive recovery, only 5 of 500 bounded sets and 22 normal bands
have a nonempty net-benefit range wholly above zero. The earlier counts of 401 and 469
concerned a positive effect, before deducting the offer cost. Under complete stable recovery,
the corresponding net-positive counts are 335 and 432. These are decision frequencies in
the same retained datasets. They show how a small population margin can remain hard to
distinguish from zero even after every missing customer is invited. The normal-band frequencies
retain their empirical qualification; more affirmative decisions alone do not establish a better
procedure.
   The resulting design choice is concrete. The bank should establish whether follow-up recovers
the fixed historical endpoint, record every invitation and completion, and compare the cost
of reducing the unresolved mass with the cost of estimating a fixed set more precisely. A
workflow that cannot defend stability should expose an appropriately justified sensitivity range
or withhold the historical-effect recommendation. The generated single-treatment example
shows why more invitations, more completed responses and more stable historical information
can have different decision values. Extending that conclusion to a bank requires evidence about
its actual recovery process. Unbounded longitudinal rewards require their own identification
and sampling arguments, and operating acceptance remains unestablished.
