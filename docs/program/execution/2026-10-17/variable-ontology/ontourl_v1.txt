                                                                                         Abstract
                                                     Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across
                                                     a range of natural language processing tasks, yet their ability to process structured
                                                     symbolic knowledge remains underexplored. To address this gap, we propose a
                                                     taxonomy of LLMs’ ontological capabilities and introduce O NTO URL, the first
                                                     comprehensive benchmark designed to systematically evaluate LLMs’ proficiency
                                                     in handling ontologies—formal, symbolic representations of domain knowledge
                                                     through concepts, relationships, and instances. Based on the proposed taxonomy,
                                                     O NTO URL systematically assesses three dimensions: understanding, reasoning,
                                                     and learning through 15 distinct tasks comprising 58,981 questions derived from
                                                     40 ontologies across 8 domains. Experiments with 20 open-source LLMs reveal
                                                     significant performance differences across models, tasks, and domains, with current
                                                     LLMs showing proficiency in understanding ontological knowledge but substantial
                                                     weaknesses in reasoning and learning tasks. These findings highlight fundamental
                                                     limitations in LLMs’ capability to process symbolic knowledge and establish
                                                     O NTO URL as a critical benchmark for advancing the integration of LLMs with
                                                     formal knowledge representations.


                                         1    Introduction
                                         Ontologies play a foundational role in encoding structured domain knowledge among the most
                                         prominent symbolic frameworks, particularly in fields such as finance, medicine, science and legal
                                         domain. They provide a formal structure through well-defined concepts (classes), relationships
                                         (e.g., hierarchies and semantic connections), and instances (individuals) [32, 79, 70]. In recent
                                         years, as Large language models (LLMs) have significantly revolutionized numerous fields with
                                         their remarkable capabilities in various tasks [80, 106], the interaction between ontologies and the
                                         knowledge-rich LLMs has sparked significant interest, raising research into ontology-related tasks
                                         such as leveraging LLMs for ontology creation.
                                         However, whether LLMs can truly comprehend and manipulate structured symbolic knowledge
                                         remains a subject of intense debate [103, 83, 110, 91]. This discussion centers on whether statistical
                                         pattern recognition can replicate the symbolic representations and logical structures traditionally
                                         managed by classical knowledge representation systems. Therefore, a critical yet underexplored

                                         Preprint.
question arises for ontology practitioners and researchers: to what extent can LLMs understand,
utilize, and construct ontologies? While several datasets have been developed for ontology-related
tasks [8, 84, 107, 15, 86, 100, 37, 102, 49, 47, 64, 63], these works typically focus on one or two
isolated ontology aspects and are rarely designed specifically for evaluating LLMs. Furthermore,
there is an absence of a comprehensive taxonomy that systematically categorizes the ontological
capabilities required across domains and tasks, thus hindering the reliable evaluation of LLMs
capabilities on ontology.
To fill in this gap, we investigate three key research questions spanning three dimensions: (1)
can LLMs accurately memorize the fine-grained details inherent in ontologies, including concepts,
hierarchical relationships, properties, and instances? (2) Can LLMs perform robust reasoning over
ontologies, such as transitive superclass inference or description logic reasoning? (3) Can LLMs
autonomously construct ontologies based on their rich knowledge, such as tasks like ontology
hierarchy construction?
We first propose a taxonomy of ontological capabilities for LLMs and then introduce O NTO URL, the
first comprehensive benchmark for evaluating LLMs’ abilities on ontologies. O NTO URL consists of
58,981 questions derived from 40 ontologies, encompassing 15 tasks across 8 domains. These tasks
are meticulously crafted to assess LLMs’ proficiency in three crucial dimensions: understanding,
reasoning, and learning. By evaluating 20 open-source LLMs, we gain critical insights into their
strengths and limitations on handling structured symbolic knowledge. Our primary contributions are
summarized as follows:

    • We present a taxonomy of LLM ontological capabilities in three dimensions: understanding,
      reasoning, and learning, providing a systematic framework for analyzing LLMs’ interactions with
      structured symbolic knowledge.
    • We introduce O NTO URL, a benchmark comprising 58,981 questions derived from 40 ontologies,
      covering 15 tasks across 8 domains. Our benchmark enables rigorous evaluation on LLMs’
      abilities across multiple dimensions, such as conceptual understanding, structural knowledge,
      logical reasoning, structure construction, and concept alignment.
    • We conduct in-depth pilot studies on 20 LLMs and provide comprehensive analyses across model
      scales, task levels, and specific domains. All code for benchmark construction and experiments,
      as well as the benchmark dataset, are publicly available (Appendix A).


2     Background

Ontology Ontologies provide formal, logic-based representations of domain knowledge, orga-
nizing concepts, properties, instances, and their interrelations in a structured, symbolic form [32]
(Appendix C.1). Unlike taxonomies or controlled vocabularies which focus primarily on hierarchical
groupings, or knowledge graphs which typically emphasize instance-level assertions, ontologies
encode domain semantics through explicit axioms and logical constraints (further comparison in
Appendix B.3).
As a formal knowledge representation system, ontologies are typically formalized in Description
Logic [60]—a family of formal languages designed for representing and reasoning about knowledge.
Ontologies comprise three fundamental components: Terminological Box (TBox), containing class
hierarchies and definitions; Assertional Box (ABox), capturing assertions about individuals; and
Role Box (RBox), defining relationships and properties among entities. Figure 1 illustrates these
components in a conference ontology excerpt, where “Chair” and its superclass “Committee Members”
represent TBox elements, “Mary” as an instance of “Chair” forms part of the ABox, and “has_authors”
is a relation between “Review” and “Review Expertise” belongs to the RBox.
By offering a standardized vocabulary with formal semantics, ontologies support semantic interop-
erability, knowledge integration, and automated reasoning [101]. Most ontological resources are
created by domain experts, such as Gene Ontology [4], Plant Ontology [21], and LKIF Core Legal On-
tology [39]. Appendix C.4 summarizes the 40 ontologies used in our benchmark, and Appendix C.3
introduces the language for describing these ontologies: RDF (a foundational data model) and OWL
(the core ontology language). As ontologies have been central to symbolic AI approaches for decades,
understanding and leveraging such structured symbolic knowledge are essential for LLMs.

Ontology-related Tasks Previous work has primarily focused on conceptual understanding, using
probing techniques to examine how LLMs memorize and retrieve class-level knowledge [8, 84, 92,
82, 107, 97, 44, 73, 48, 100], and structural knowledge [37, 74, 81, 43, 112].
Beyond basic understanding tasks, some works perform specialized forms of deductive logic reasoning
on ontologies. Rule-based ontology reasoners support a wide range of inference tasks, including
validating ontology coherence, deriving complete class hierarchies, assigning individuals to their
most specific types, inferring property relationships, and executing queries to retrieve relevant classes
or individuals [104, 71, 51, 96, 30, 18, 14, 27, 9]. Recently, a few studies have begun to explore using
language models for ontology reasoning, particularly within the framework of Description Logic
[37, 105].
Another active research direction involves learning and constructing ontological structures. Traditional
approaches employed statistical term extraction and pattern-based methods to identify candidate
concepts and taxonomic relations [38, 54, 66, 108, 3, 65, 53, 12, 5, 109, 58]. More recent efforts
leverage pretrained language models, enabling more sophisticated concept extraction and hierarchy
learning [7, 77, 64].

Ontology-related Benchmarks To evaluate these diverse ontology-related tasks, researchers have
developed various benchmarks focusing on specific capabilities. These include evaluations for
conceptual knowledge [8, 84, 107, 15, 86, 100], hierarchical knowledge [37, 102, 49, 47], ontology
reasoning [37, 105], ontology matching [98, 56, 57, 42, 41], and ontology learning [46, 7, 64, 63].
However, most existing datasets and benchmarks focus on only one or two specific aspects of
ontologies, and few are designed specifically for LLMs with appropriate question-answering or
generation formats. This limitation underscores the need for a comprehensive benchmark that covers
a wide range of ontologies, domains, and tasks.


3      OntoURL

3.1      Design Principle

OntoURL is designed as a comprehensive evaluation benchmark to systematically assess the multi-
dimensional capabilities of LLMs within domain-specific ontologies. It serves two primary purposes:
supporting ontology practitioners in selecting appropriate LLMs for ontology-related applications,
and providing LLM researchers with insights into models’ conceptual, hierarchical, reasoning and
generative capabilities in ontological contexts.
While considerable research has explored interactions between LLMs and ontologies, few studies have
provided a systematic classification of the underlying capabilities. Drawing inspiration from Bloom’s
Taxonomy of educational objectives [13], we introduce a three-level taxonomy for ontological
capabilities for LLMs—understanding, reasoning and learning (Figure 2).

Ontological Understanding This capability represents the most fundamental ontological level and
is thus placed at the base of the triangle in Figure 2. It encompasses the memorization, recall, and
comprehension of explicitly defined ontology knowledge. For example, retrieving the definition of
the concept “calyx”in the Plant Ontology, identifying its superclass and subsumption relationships,
and recognizing its associated properties and instances.

Ontological Reasoning This capability builds upon ontological understanding and is positioned
above it in the triangle of Figure 2. It involves inferring implicit knowledge that is not explicitly
defined within an ontology. Structured ontologies often encode rich hierarchical relationships
from which additional facts can be logically deduced. For example, the Plant Ontology states that
“testa” is a subclass of “seed coat” (testa ⊑ seed coat), “seed coat” is a subclass of “seed”
(seed coat ⊑ seed), and “seed” is a subclass of “plant embryo” (seed ⊑ plant embryo). From
these axioms, it follows that “testa” is also a subclass of “plant embryo” (testa ⊑ plant embryo).
We classify ontological reasoning as the ability to infer such implicit knowledge through reasoning
process such as logical deduction.

Ontological Learning This capability represents the highest level in our taxonomy and is placed
at the top of the triangle in Figure 2. It primarily concerns the process of constructing ontologies.
Traditional ontology learning tasks have largely focused on generating hierarchical structures, while
often neglecting other essential structural components like properties and instances. Therefore, we
propose that ontological learning should encompass multiple dimensions: the generation of class
definitions, the construction of class hierarchies, and the integration of properties and their constraints.
In addition, ontology alignment—ensuring consistency across multiple ontologies by identifying and
mapping equivalent concepts—is a critical aspect of ontology construction and is thus also considered
part of this capability.

3.2   Data Collection and Processing

Data Sources OntoURL draws on 40 expert-created, open-source ontologies spanning a broad
range of 8 different domains, including (1) sciences; (2) health and medicine; (3) business and
finance; (4) earth and environment; (5) arts, media and entertainment; (6) food and agriculture; (7)
human and society; and (8) the legal domain. All ontologies are provided in RDF [72] or OWL [70]
format. Detailed information of these ontologies can be found in Appendix C.4. While open-domain
ontologies such as DBpedia [6] are available, we focus on domain-specific ontologies due to their
greater depth, consistency, and formal structure (see Appendix B.4 for discussion). In cases where
multiple ontologies exist within the same domain, we address their heterogeneity by designing
prompts tailored to each ontology (more discussion in Appendix B.5).

Data Processing After collecting the ontologies, we apply a four-step pipeline to create multiple-
choice, true/false question and open-ended questions, as illustrated in Figure 3. We provide more
details and implementations of specific tasks in Appendix D.

Figure 3: The pipeline of O NTO URL construction: (1) elements are extracted from ontologies using
rule-based extraction (understanding and learning tasks) and an ontology reasoner HermiT (reasoning
tasks); (2) the extracted elements are transformed into natural language questions; (3) distractors are
added to form multiple-choice questions; and (4) the generated data is filtered and evaluated.



First, we extract task-relevant entities, including classes, instances, properties and their associated
semantic details (e.g., definitions, relationships, and range/domains) from each ontology. The sub-
ontology of “Basenji” in the first step of Figure 3 presents the most involved elements in this extraction
process. In particular, for reasoning tasks, an ontology reasoner is required to derive implicit relations
(e.g. the relation between “Basenji” and “Canid”).
Next, based on the extracted information, we construct natural language questions targeting different
capabilities. Examples of questions for understanding, reasoning, and learning are shown in the
second step of Figure 3.
For the multiple-choice questions, we generate answer options by selecting semantically plausible and
structurally relevant distractors (e.g., ancestors, siblings, or children, as shown in the understanding
example in the third step of Figure 3). For true/false questions, we incorporate the distractors directly
into the statements, as demonstrated in the reasoning example.
After question generation, we apply several filtering and balancing strategies to ensure quality and
diversity: (a) To avoid over-representing abstract concepts, we assign sampling probabilities based on
class depth (distance from the root) and sample data according to these probabilities. (b) Questions
for same classes are de-duplicated. For instance, as shown in Figure 3, a class like “Dog” has multiple
subclasses (e.g., “Basenji”, “Leonberger”), but only one question about the subsumptions of "Dog"
will be retained. (c) We limit the number of questions per class (they may from different ontologies)
to a maximum of five per task. (d) We set the total number of questions per ontology-task pair at 500
to prevent any single ontology from disproportionately influencing the evaluation results.
Finally, we perform an human verification to ensure the data quality, focusing on three key dimen-
sions: (1) Syntax, to verify fluentness and grammatical correctness; (2) Semantics, to confirm the
correctness of the answer; and (3) Quality (for multiple-choice questions), to assess whether the
distractors are well-designed and appropriately challenging. Each expert reviewed 20% of the data for
each task, with a 5% overlap between annotators to allow cross-verification of annotation consistency.
Expert review confirms the high quality of the benchmark, with over 95% of questions rated as
syntactically and semantically correct, and inter-annotator agreement exceeding 0.85 (Fleiss’ κ [62])
across evaluation dimensions (detailed guidelines and results are provided in Appendix E).

For Understanding capability, tasks evaluate a model’s ability to comprehend explicitly defined
ontological elements, including class definitions (U1), class relationships (U2), property domains
(U3), instance classifications (U4), and instance definitions (U5).
The Reasoning capability increases complexity by requiring inference over implicit knowledge not
explicitly presented in the ontology. Inferred relation reasoning (R1) extends task U2 by shifting
from explicit to inferred class relationships. Similarly, constraint reasoning (R2) and instance class
reasoning (R3) are inference-based counterparts of tasks U3 and U4, respectively. Tasks R4 and R5
introduce more advanced logical inference: SWRL-based logic reasoning (R4) involves reasoning
over rules defined in the Semantic Web Rule Language [40, SWRL], encompassing conjunctions,
property chains, and conditional implications. Description logic reasoning (R5) focuses on reasoning
with description logic, where models must interpret formal expressions and perform deductive
inference over constructs such as ∃, ∀, ⊓, and numerical restrictions (e.g., ≥ n, ≤ m).
The Learning capability tasks are generative and typically involve longer and more complex input
contexts, making them more challenging than multiple-choice tasks. Class definition generation (L1)
corresponds to task U1, requiring models to generate class definitions based on names and related
information. Class hierarchy construction (L2) and property relation construction (L3) align with
task U2. Constraint construction (L4) builds on task U3 by requiring models to generate constraints.
Ontology alignment (L5) evaluates whether models can align semantically equivalent classes and
instances across two ontologies.


3.4   Benchmark Statistics

Figure 4 presents the distribution of questions across tasks and domains in the O NTO URL benchmark.
As shown in Figure 4a, Tasks U2 (Class Relation Understanding, 15.6%), U1 (Class Definition
Understanding, 15.5%), and R1 (Inferred Relation Reasoning, 13.9%) are the most prevalent in
our benchmark. Conversely, ontology learning (L1-L5) and property-related tasks (U3, R2, L3,
L4) constitute a smaller portion of the dataset. This distribution stems from two primary factors.
First, most ontological classes contain superclasses and definitions, enabling the generation of more
questions for tasks U1, U2, and R1. In contrast, properties and their associated constraints are not
consistently provided across all ontologies, resulting in fewer questions for property-related tasks.
Additionally, the ontology learning tasks were significantly reduced during the filtering process, which
systematically eliminated overlapping sub-ontologies to ensure data quality and prevent redundancy.

ontologies. The Sciences domain represents the largest portion (28.4%), as it encompasses 8
ontologies, including extremely large resources like the Gene Ontology [4, 2], Cell Ontology [24],

4     Evaluation
4.1    Setup

We evaluate LLMs under both zero-shot and few-shot settings across all 15 tasks in O NTO URL.
For the zero-shot scenario, the input to the LLMs consists solely of task instructions, questions, and
answer options (where applicable). In the few-shot setting, we provide two or four carefully selected
examples for each task to demonstrate the expected reasoning pattern and output format. As shown in
Table 1, we use task-appropriate metrics: Accuracy for multiple-choice and true/false questions (tasks
U1-U5, R1-R5), ROUGE-L for text generation (task L1), and F1 score for structured outputs such as
triples or tuples (tasks L2-L5). We apply regular expressions to extract valid triples or tuples from
the model’s responses to mitigate the impact of irrelevant text. The hyperparameters and hardware
configurations are detailed in Appendix F. All instructions and prompts are provided in Appendix D.

4.2    Evaluated Models

We evaluate a diverse set of 20 language models, which can be categorized into three groups.
General-purpose LLMs include 14 widely used open-source models across various parameter scales:
Qwen2.5-(3B, 7B, 14B, 32B, 72B) [111], QWQ-32B, Phi4-4B [1], LLaMA3.1-8B, LLaMA3.3-
70B [31], Aya-Expanse-(8B, 32B), InternLM3-8B, Mistral-8B, and Mistral-small. Ontology-
trained LLMs comprise two task-specialized models—Ollm-wiki and Ollm-arxiv [64]—which
are fine-tuned from Mistral-7B [45] on Wikipedia category and arXiv taxonomy data, respectively.
Domain-specialized LLMs include SaulLM-7B (legal domain) [20], BioMistral-7B (sciences) [61],
OpenBioLLM-8B (biomedicine), and Finance-Chat-7B (finance) [19]. These models are included to
provide a complementary perspective by assessing how domain-specific pretraining affects perfor-
mance on ontological tasks. Links to all model repositories are provided in Appendix F.

Ollm, which is specifically trained for ontology construction, performs relatively poorly—likely due
to its specialization in hierarchical generation rather than general understanding or reasoning.
Model scale correlates strongly with performance, especially in understanding and reasoning. For
instance, Qwen2.5-72B achieves top scores of 92.6 on U4, 93.4 on R2, and 21.6 F1-score on L5.
Similarly, LLaMA3.3-70B scores 91.8 on U4, 92.9 on R2, and 20.2 on L5. This pattern is even more
pronounced within the same architecture: across Qwen, Mistral, Aya, and LLaMA, larger models
consistently outperform smaller models on most tasks.
Ontological Understanding Capability of LLMs Tasks U1 to U5 demonstrate that LLMs generally
perform well on ontological understanding, particularly in recognizing hierarchical structures. This is
reflected in the high accuracy on U2 (class relations) and U4 (instance classification), with scores
ranging from 80% to 94%. In contrast, performance is less consistent on definition- and property-
based tasks. U1 (class definition), U3 (property domain), and U5 (instance definition) show notable
gaps in certain models, for instance, Aya-8B scores only 77.1%, 62.4%, and 77.9% on these tasks,
respectively. As suggested in the examples (Appendix D), U1, U3 and U5 require the knowledge of
highly specialized domains and the distractors closely resemble the correct answer.
Ontological Reasoning Capability of LLMs Reasoning tasks present greater challenges for LLMs
than understanding tasks. Task R1 (inferred relation reasoning) is a difficult variant of task U2 (class
relation understanding), requiring inference to identify class relationships not explicitly defined. As
expected, models generally perform 3-4 percentage points worse on R1 than on U2, with the most
dramatic decrease observed in Ollm-arxiv-7B (from 84.4% to 64.2%). Similarly, R3 (instance class
reasoning) functions as the reasoning-intensive counterpart to U4. Performance on R3 (60-70%)
demonstrates a substantial decline compared to U4 (80-90%). These results indicate that when
reasoning across multiple relationships is required, LLM performance deteriorates significantly.
Tasks involving logical expressions, such as R4 (SWRL-based reasoning) and R5 (description logic
reasoning), are even more difficult. Compared to natural language-based reasoning (R1, R2 and R3),
model performance drops more dramatically when logical operators are involved, with scores ranging
between 60% and 75%. Even the best-performing model, Qwen2.5-72B, achieves only 75.6% and
68.8% on these two tasks, respectively. This highlights a significant limitation of current LLMs:
difficulty in executing precise symbolic reasoning over formally structured ontologies.

Ontological Learning Capabilities of LLMs Although direct comparisons is limited due to
different evaluation metrics, generation tasks are shown to be more challenging, comparing with
understanding and reasoning tasks. In L1 (class definition generation), models struggle to generate
definitions, as evidenced by low ROUGE-L (typically below 10). This poor performance likely stems
from the challenges of domain-specific definition generation, which requires not only describing the
target class but also distinguishing it from adjacent concepts (e.g. its superclasses). Unlike human
domain experts who possess comprehensive ontological perspectives, LLMs struggle to make such
fine-grained semantic distinctions within specialized domains.
Similar limitations appear in structural construction tasks. In L2 (class hierarchy construction), L3
(property relation construction), and L4 (constraint construction), models frequently fail to produce
syntactically valid triples in zero-shot settings. Performance improves modestly in two- and four-shot
settings (Appendix G), but output quality remains low, often featuring ill-defined or hallucinated
relations (Appendix H). For L5 (ontology alignment), models achieve slightly higher F1 scores in the
range of 10-20%. But for practical ontology applications, the performance of the evaluated LLMs on
all learning tasks remains substantially poor for reliable deployment.

5    Conclusion
We introduce O NTO URL, a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating symbolic ontological capa-
bilities in LLMs. We establish a taxonomy of ontological capabilities—understanding, reasoning,
and learning—and develops a systematic pipeline for creating and verifying questions based on this
framework. The benchmark comprises 58,981 questions derived from 40 ontologies, covering 15 dis-
tinct tasks across 8 domains. Evaluation results on O NTO URL reveal that while contemporary LLMs
demonstrate strong ontological understanding capabilities, they struggle with reasoning and learning
tasks. These findings indicate that despite rapid advancements in LLM technology, substantial room
for improvement remains in handling symbolic ontological knowledge. We believe that O NTO URL
can serve as a valuable resource for both ontology practitioners and AI researchers, facilitating the
evaluation, analysis and development of LLMs in ontology domain.

B     Discussion
B.1   Limitations

While O NTO URL provides a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating ontological capabilities of
LLMs, several limitations remain.

Limitations of Source Ontologies Although O NTO URL includes 40 ontologies spanning 8 do-
mains, this coverage remains limited given the vast and evolving landscape of ontological resources.
Many specialized ontologies are not yet included. As O NTO URL is designed to be a long-term
maintained resource, we plan to continuously expand the ontology set in future iterations.

Limitations of Task Coverage The benchmark covers 15 tasks across three capability dimensions—
understanding, reasoning, and learning. However, certain aspects of ontological knowledge remain
unexplored, such as in-depth property semantics, constraints over roles, and full ontology construction
from scratch. To improve task diversity and completeness, we will continue to incorporate additional
task types in future versions.

Limitations of Language Scope The current benchmark is designed primarily for English-language
ontologies and prompts. While some of the source ontologies provide multilingual labels or transla-
tions, we exclude these in this release. Future work will explore multilingual evaluation settings and
tasks to better support cross-lingual ontology understanding.

Limitations of Evaluation Protocols The evaluation of generative tasks primarily relies on au-
tomatic metrics such as ROUGE-L and F1, which may not fully reflect the semantic adequacy or
logical consistency of model outputs. Future work will incorporate human evaluation and formal
validation techniques for a more rigorous assessment.

Limitations of Experiment Settings Our study focuses on open-source LLMs under zero-shot and
few-shot settings; exploring more advanced prompting techniques (e.g., chain-of-thought prompting,
retrieval-augmented reasoning) or fine-tuning strategies remains an important direction for future
research.

B.2   Broader Impacts

O NTO URL offers a structured framework for evaluating the capabilities of language models in
handling symbolic ontological tasks, with potential relevance to both computational linguistics
and knowledge representation research. By systematically assessing understanding, reasoning, and
learning over formal ontologies, the benchmark contributes to a clearer characterization of how
language models interact with structured domain knowledge. Possible downstream uses include
assisting in ontology engineering workflows, supporting cross-domain ontology alignment, and
improving tools for semantic access to formalized information.
At the same time, the application of language models in domains such as law, medicine, and science
introduces risks when outputs are used without verification. Although O NTO URL enables more
rigorous and fine-grained evaluation of model behavior on structured symbolic inputs, current models
often struggle with logical consistency and precise concept differentiation. The benchmark is therefore
intended as an analytic resource for identifying model limitations, rather than as an endorsement of
model deployment in sensitive domains.


                                                  17
B.3   Taxonomy, Knowledge Graph v.s. Ontologies

While taxonomies, ontologies, and knowledge graphs are all used to represent structured knowledge,
they differ in complexity, expressiveness, and purpose. A taxonomy organizes concepts in a hierarchi-
cal tree structure, typically using “is-a” relationships, making it useful for classification but limited in
semantic richness. A knowledge graph extends beyond hierarchical structures by capturing diverse
entities and their relationships in graph form, often integrating factual assertions from large datasets.
However, it usually lacks formal semantics, tending to presenting instance-level information.
In contrast, an ontology offers a formally defined and logic-based representation, specifying not
only class hierarchies but also properties, constraints, domains, and reasoning rules. This formalism
enables logical inference, consistency checking, and rich semantic interoperability. Ontologies
are thus particularly well-suited for applications requiring symbolic reasoning, such as biomedical
informatics, semantic web technologies, and explainable AI.

B.4   Open-domain Ontology v.s. Domain-specific Ontology

Open-domain ontologies aim to cover broad, general-purpose knowledge across diverse topics, but
they often lack depth, consistency, and formal structure. In contrast, domain-specific ontologies are
carefully curated to capture rich, fine-grained semantics within a particular field, such as biology or
medicine. These ontologies typically include formal definitions, logical axioms, and well-defined
class hierarchies, making them ideal for evaluating symbolic understanding, reasoning and learning.

B.5   Heterogeneity of Ontology

Ontology development is often domain-specific, yet not necessarily standardized. As a result,
multiple ontologies may exist for the same domain, each reflecting different design decisions, levels
of granularity, or intended applications. This phenomenon can be referred to as the heterogeneity of
ontologies. In the medical domain, for instance, both the Human Disease Ontology (DO) and the
International Classification of Diseases (ICD) ontology aim to represent disease-related knowledge,
yet they differ significantly in focus and structure. The Disease Ontology emphasizes semantic clarity
and integration with biomedical ontologies, while ICD is designed primarily for clinical coding and
statistical reporting.


C     Ontology

C.1   Ontology Composition

Ontologies consist of formal components that define domain knowledge. These components can be
broadly categorized into terminological knowledge (TBox) and assertional knowledge (ABox). The
TBox describes the conceptual schema of a domain, while the ABox contains concrete assertions
about individual entities. Below, we summarize the key elements used in our benchmark construction.

Class Classes represent abstract concepts in a domain and are the primary building blocks of the
TBox. They form hierarchical structures, enabling generalization and specialization. For example, the
class Animal may include subclasses such as Mammal or Bird, each denoting more specific categories.

Property Properties describe attributes or relationships involving classes and instances. Ontologies
typically distinguish between datatype properties, which link individuals to literal values (e.g.,
hasColor), and object properties, which connect individuals to other individuals (e.g., hasPart).
Properties are central to expressing constraints, enabling inference, and defining the semantic structure
of a domain.

Instance Instances (individuals) are concrete members of a class and belong to the ABox. For
example, an individual such as JohnDoe may be an instance of the class Person. Instances enrich
the ontology with real-world data and are essential for tasks such as classification, validation, and
knowledge population.

C.3   Languages, Tools and Reasoner for Ontology

Ontologies provide a formal structure for representing domain knowledge, widely used in AI, the
semantic web, and data integration. Key languages include OWL (Web Ontology Language), a
W3C standard supporting rich semantic modeling and automated reasoning, and RDF (Resource
Description Framework), which represents data as subject–predicate–object triples. RDFS provides
lightweight schema capabilities, while SPARQL enables querying over RDF graphs.


                                                 19
Ontology development is supported by tools such as Protégé1 , a widely used open-source editor for
creating and managing OWL and RDF ontologies. For reasoning, systems such as HermiT, Pellet, and
FaCT++ are commonly used to perform tasks like consistency checking, classification, and inference.
These reasoners are based on Description Logic and integrate with editors like Protégé to support
ontology validation and enrichment.
In recent years, modern ontology engineering has increasingly incorporated machine learning and
natural language processing techniques. Tools like DeepOnto2 [36] provide a unified Python-based
framework for ontology learning, alignment, and embedding, supporting deep learning-based ap-
proaches to ontology tasks. Similarly, OntoGPT3 [17] leverages large language models to support
ontology-guided information extraction and schema population, making ontology-based workflows
more accessible and semi-automated.

Our task generation pipeline processes each ontology file by 1.loading it, extracting all classes with
explicit definitions, and then for each class: 2. Retrieves its human-readable label and definition. 3.
Selects three distractors, preferably from the same superclass, or randomly if not feasible. 4. Shuffles
the correct definition with the distractors to create a four-option set.

Task U2: Class Relation Understanding The task focuses on evaluating the ability of LLMs to
recognize relational knowledge in ontologies, such as subclass, disjoint, or equivalent class relations.
The question generation pipeline processes each ontology file by: 1. Loading the ontology and
extracting all classes along with their hierarchical and relational information. 2. Extracting relation
triples (e.g., subclassOf, disjointWith, unionOf) using explicit axioms, object properties, and RDF
list structures. 3. Selecting distractors for each relational triple, prioritizing disjoint classes and
non-ancestor classes to ensure semantic plausibility. 4. Generating multiple-choice questions, each
asking about the correct relational target and pairing it with three distractors (choose from subclass,
subsumptions or siblings).

Task U3: Property Domain Understanding The task targets LLMs’ understanding of data
property semantics in ontologies by testing their ability to identify valid domain or range classes.
Each ontology is processed by: 1. Loading the ontology and collecting all data properties and classes.
2. Extracting (property, domain, range) triples for properties with defined domains. 3. Selecting
distractors from unrelated classes to ensure plausible options. The distractors are selected by the
distance of from the correct class.

Task U4: Instance Class Understanding The task evaluates LLMs’ ability to classify individuals
by their correct ontology class.
Each ontology is processed by: 1. Loading the ontology and extracting all classes and instances. 2.
For each instance, selecting one asserted class as the correct answer. 3. Generating distractor classes
by excluding the target’s ancestors and descendants. 4. Creating multiple-choice questions asking
which class the instance belongs to.

Task U5: Instance Definition Understanding              The task assesses LLMs’ ability to match instances
to their correct textual definitions.
Each ontology is processed by: 1. Loading the ontology and identifying instances with explicit
definitions. 2. For each instance, retrieving its definition as the correct answer. 3. Selecting three
distractor definitions from other instances of the same classes (or sibling classes). 4. Generating
multiple-choice questions asking which definition best describes the given instance.

Task R1: Inferred Relation Reasoning The task evaluates LLMs’ ability to understand inferred
ontological relations, which is not explicitly defined in the ontologies.
Each ontology is processed by: 1. Loading the ontology and applying logical reasoning (where
feasible) to infer subclass, equivalence, and property-based relations. 2. Extracting explicit and
inferred triples using Ontology reasoner (HermiT). 3. Sampling inferred-only triples (i.e., not
explicitly stated in the ontology). 4. For each inferred triple, constructing a multiple-choice question
with three distractors from non-related classes.

Task R2: Constraint Reasoning The task assesses LLMs’ understanding of inferred semantic
constraints in ontologies, including property ranges, property chains, and existential restrictions.

isolate inferred-only instance-class links. 4. Generating multiple-choice questions that ask which
class an instance belongs to, with distractors drawn from unrelated classes.

Task R4: SWRL-Based Logic Reasoning The task evaluates LLMs’ ability to perform inference
over SWRL-style logical rules encoded in OWL ontologies.
Each ontology is processed by: 1. Parsing class-level logical definitions (e.g., existential restric-
tions, conjunctions) to extract SWRL-like rules. 2. Translating each rule into a natural language
question: given a set of conditions (rule body), which logical conclusion (rule head) follows. 3.
Generating plausible distractors by altering predicates, argument order, or combining irrelevant rules.
4. Supporting both single-rule and composite multi-rule inference.

Task R5: Description Logic Reasoning The task evaluates LLMs’ ability to assess the satisfiability
of complex Description Logic (DL) expressions.
Each ontology is processed by: 1. Sampling and synthesizing class expressions using DL constructors
(e.g., ∃, ∀, ⊓, ≥, ¬). 2. Injecting expressions into a temporary ontology as equivalence axioms
and reasoning to check satisfiability. 3. Translating OWL expressions to labeled Description Logic
(DL) notation. 4. Filtering expressions by a minimum complexity threshold and balancing between
satisfiable and unsatisfiable cases.

Task L1: Class Definition Generation The task evaluates LLMs’ ability to generate or recognize
class-level definitions in ontologies.
Each ontology is processed by: 1. Extracting named classes with explicit definitions. 2. Sampling
classes based on structural metrics (e.g., depth, siblings, subclass count) to ensure diversity.

Task L2: Class Hierarchy Construction This task assesses LLMs’ ability to reconstruct ontology
hierarchies and relations from a set of related classes and their textual definitions.
For each ontology: 1. we select multiple sets of semantically related classes (via subclass/superclass
relations). 2. For each set, it extracts a local ontology subgraph (typically 8–15 classes deep)
including: subclass axioms, object property and data property triples and class-level annotations (e.g.,
definitions, comments). 3. Each task presents only the class labels and optional definitions (without
explicit structure).

Task L3: Property and Relation Construction This task assesses LLMs’ ability to reconstruct
ontology property structures from a set of interrelated classes and a list of available property names.
For each ontology: 1. we select multiple sets of structurally connected classes (based on subclass
and property usage patterns). 2. For each set, we extract a local ontology subgraph (8–15 classes)
that includes object property and data property assertions among the classes, as well as relevant
annotations. 3. Each task presents only the class labels (with optional comments) and a list of used
object/data property names—without revealing any relational structure—requiring models to recover
all valid property triples and their characteristics (e.g., functional, symmetric, transitive).

Task L4: Constraint Construction This task assesses LLMs’ ability to recover property-level
constraints from an ontology.
For each ontology: 1. We sample multiple sets of ontology properties (object and data properties),
ensuring each set is sufficiently rich. 2. For each set, we extract a local subgraph of related classes
by traversing the domain and range of the selected properties, expanding outward (via subclass
relations) to reach a representative size. 3. Each task presents a list of class labels (with optional
definitions/comments) and a list of property names.

H    Case Study

Because the Understanding and Reasoning tasks are formatted as multiple-choice questions, we do
not conduct case studies for them. Instead, we present two case studies for the Learning tasks (L1
and L2), selecting the best- and worst-performing models for each task to facilitate analysis and
comparison.
For L1 (definition generation), we observe that the correct answers often require substantial external
knowledge not available in the input prompt or model-internal structure. While the models may
demonstrate adequate understanding, it remains challenging for them to produce definitions that are
precise and usable within an ontological context.
For L2 (structural construction), models frequently generate extraneous text beyond the expected
triplet format. Although these extra words do not directly affect evaluation—since only triplets are
extracted—the quality of the generated triplets is notably poor. Many include custom or ill-formed
relations, which significantly undermines overall performance.
