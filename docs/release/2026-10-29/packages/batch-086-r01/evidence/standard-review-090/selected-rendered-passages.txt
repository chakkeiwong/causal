What the bank should buy first

A bank considering a better deposit rate must pay for money it already holds as well as money
the offer attracts. A collections team must account for how today’s contact changes tomorrow’s
information. A discovery algorithm may return several explanations of the same observations,
with opposite implications for a pricing decision. An apparently clean customer table may still
conceal misrecorded treatment or incorrectly linked accounts. These are four different reasons
why a persuasive numerical answer can fail to improve a decision.
   An increase in one account’s recorded balance can be a transfer from another account at
the same bank. The funding outcome must aggregate the intended household and distinguish
new external money from internal movement. Competitor repricing can also change balances
during the same period. A lender’s threshold comparison concerns the applicants affected at
that margin, while a treasury shock analysis must separate the external shock from the bank’s
own response. These economic distinctions determine which comparison is useful before a
statistical model is chosen.
   The proposal is to make a small class of defensible studies easier to conduct and compare.
The first investment should be a comparative integration using established methods, a precise
study description and a report that preserves uncertainty. A capable economist using existing
tools is the reference alternative. The proposed service earns a place only if the completed
study is better or cheaper at equivalent quality. Broad replacement of the bank’s statistical
estate is not justified by the evidence presented here.
   That conclusion follows from the examples, rather than from an assumed advantage of
a common interface. In the constructed offer study, a population balance effect is carried
through sampling uncertainty into annual contribution. The servicing calculation shows why a
correctly fitted regression can still answer the wrong policy question. The graph example gives
identical observed laws with different intervention values. The observation example shows how
additional measurement knowledge can reverse a financial conclusion. Each case tells us what
a service must preserve and what it must decline to infer.
   The evidence has different roles. Established causal results justify inference under stated
assumptions. The project’s exact derivations and generated studies expose target errors,
weak support and unresolved models. Public data reveal realistic timing and measurement
difficulties. Local interface executions establish that particular requests retain their meaning
through particular clients. None of these substitutes for an independently reviewed study on
authentic bank information. Their combined value is to identify a useful and bounded next
experiment before undertaking that more expensive work.

9.4.1      A treatment bridge need not be a positive weight

Equation (9.8) constrains a conditional average of the bridge, not its pointwise sign. It is an
inverse equation, rather than the definition of an inverse propensity. This distinction follows
from the source’s treatment-bridge condition (Ghassami et al., 2022, Section 4, Assumption
7 and equation (9)). The finite banking construction above makes the consequence explicit.
Solving its two conditional equations for each action gives

For example, among untreated units the probabilities of Z = 1 given W = 0 and W = 1 are
23/74 and 15/26, respectively. Substituting them gives conditional bridge means 50/37 and
50/13. These are exactly the reciprocals of the untreated probabilities 37/50 and 13/50 given
those proxy values. Thus a bridge can take the negative value −25/16 while satisfying both
positive conditional targets. The treated equations give the symmetric construction. These
are project calculations under the already stated finite law, not an additional empirical finding
or a theorem that all bridges are signed.

incorrect contrast 265/64. The total clipped weight is 95/64 in each arm, so normalizing the
clipped weights does not repair the result: it gives arm means 21/19 and 74/19, and contrast
53/19. Positivity of the action mechanism has not failed; clipping has broken the bridge
equations. If the outcome bridge were also correct, augmentation would still have its separate
outcome-bridge robustness, but the clipped treatment bridge could no longer justify the other
branch.
   This example changes the implementation choice. A proxy-bridge solver must preserve the
signed solution and evaluate its conditional moments, second moments and regularization
sensitivity. Large signed values can signal an unstable inverse problem. Restricting or clipping
them is a new approximation whose bias must be analyzed; calling the operation ordinary
weight stabilization would conceal the changed estimating equation and potentially change a
cost-based decision.

12.2.1      An observed reward can carry additional state information

Mean rewards suffice for evaluating a policy that ignores reward as information and uses the
recovered signal process. They need not suffice for the full-information belief recursion in
Equation (10.16). If the bank observes the reward before its next decision, its conditional
distribution can change the posterior over states even when its mean is unchanged.
   To identify that richer recursion, strengthen the emission restriction: conditional on
(St , St+1 , At , Rt ) the two signals have independent laws E·St and E·St+1 . For every measurable
reward set D, the observed marked joint matrix then satisfies

            Ja,D (o, o′ ) = P (Ot = o, Rt ∈ D, Ot+1 = o′ | At = a) = E diag(µt )Ka,D E ′ ,

where Ka,D (s, s′ ) = Ta (s, s′ )P (Rt ∈ D | s, a, s′ ). The same multiplication yields

                                    Ka,D = diag(µt )−1 LJa,D L′ .

Thus the marked observed law identifies the transition–reward measure, and division by
Ta (s, s′ ) > 0 identifies its reward conditional law on supported state transitions. For finite
reward categories this is one matrix inversion per category. For general rewards it is an identi-
fication statement about all measurable D, not a finite-sample density-estimation guarantee.
Together with known invariant emissions, these joint kernels supply the information update as
well as expected reward. Merely recovering ra does not establish this stronger result.
   For a summary-preservation counterexample, let a fair binary state persist, with a binary
signal correct with probability 4/5. In one model the first reward is independently +1 or −1
in either state. In the other it is independently +1 or −1 in state zero and +2 or −2 in state
one. Both have the same zero reward mean, transition and signal kernel. At the next decision,
choosing the state correctly pays one. The optimal success probability is 4/5 in the first model
and one in the second, because absolute reward reveals the state. Their full reward observation
laws differ; the example proves insufficiency of the compressed mean-reward interface, not
nonidentification from the full marked law.
