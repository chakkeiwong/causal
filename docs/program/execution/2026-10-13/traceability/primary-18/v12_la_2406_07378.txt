                                                                                        Abstract
                                             Causality is essential for understanding complex systems, such as the economy, the brain, and
                                             the climate. Constructing causal graphs often relies on either data-driven or expert-driven ap-
                                             proaches, both fraught with challenges. The former methods, like the celebrated PC algorithm,
                                             face issues with data requirements and assumptions of causal sufficiency, while the latter de-
                                             mand substantial time and domain knowledge. This work explores the capabilities of Large Lan-
                                             guage Models (LLMs) as an alternative to domain experts for causal graph generation. We frame
                                             conditional independence queries as prompts to LLMs and employ the PC algorithm with the
                                             answers. The performance of the LLM-based conditional independence oracle on systems with
                                             known causal graphs shows a high degree of variability. We improve the performance through a
                                             proposed statistical-inspired voting schema that allows some control over false-positive and false-
                                             negative rates. Inspecting the chain-of-thought argumentation, we find causal reasoning to justify
                                             its answer to a probabilistic query. We show evidence that knowledge-based CIT could eventually
                                             become a complementary tool for data-driven causal discovery.
                                             Keywords: Causality; large language models; causal discovery; conditional independence testing;
                                             constrained-based causal discovery.


                                         1 Introduction
                                         Understanding causality is imperative across various disciplines, as it offers critical insights into the
                                         mechanisms of complex systems. For example, in the field of epidemiology, recognizing the causal
                                         link between smoking and lung cancer has led to effective public health campaigns and anti-smoking
                                         policies, significantly reducing the prevalence of smoking-related diseases (Cornfield et al., 1959;
                                         Wynder, 1997).
                                             Investigating causality enhances our understanding of the underlying processes and enables us to
                                         make informed decisions and take proactive measures to address pressing challenges. Investigating
                                         causality poses considerable challenges, with constructing causal graphs representing a formidable
                                         task. Data-driven causal discovery methods, including prominent techniques like PC (Spirtes et al.,
                                         2000) and GES (Chickering, 2002a), encounter a range of issues. These methodologies rely heavily
                                         on large amounts of data, necessitating complex conditional independence tests that can be par-
                                         ticularly challenging, especially when working with diverse and mixed data types. The assumption
                                         of causal sufficiency, which presumes that all relevant variables are observed, can lead to erroneous
                                         conclusions, especially when unobserved variables act as potential confounders between system vari-
                                         ables. Notably, there exist alternative methods such as LPCMCI (Gerhardus and Runge, 2020),
                                         FCI (Spirtes et al., 2000), SVAR-FCI (Malinsky and Spirtes, 2018), and GPS (Claassen and Bucur,
                                         2022) that do not assume causal sufficiency (Camps-Valls et al., 2023). Nonetheless, missing data



                                                                                             1
and selection bias continue to pose persistent challenges in real-world applications, prompting efforts
to develop more resilient causal discovery methods (Camps-Valls et al., 2023).
    In addition to data-driven causal discovery methods, another approach for creating causal graphs
involves leveraging domain knowledge. However, this process is inherently challenging and time-
consuming, demanding substantial expertise and labor (Long et al., 2023b). Experts tasked with
constructing causal graphs must possess a deep understanding of the relationships and mechanisms
within the system under investigation, which often requires knowledge across different disciplines.
This often entails extensive consultations, discussions, and reviews with domain specialists, adding
significant time and resource commitments. Furthermore, the complexity of many real-world systems
amplifies the difficulty of accurately capturing all relevant causal relationships, leading to potential
oversights and inaccuracies in the resulting causal graph. These challenges underscore the necessity
for more automated methodologies. In that respect, LLMs could play a key role if they prove to be
a reliable source of causal knowledge.
    LLMs present a promising knowledge-driven alternative to expert-based graph building or data-
driven causal discovery methods. They have shown good performance across a range of language
understanding and logical reasoning tasks (Brown et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2023). This could extend to
probabilistic and causal reasoning, including interventional and counterfactual scenarios. Whether
or not this is the case is the subject of heated debate (Hobbhahn et al., 2022; Willig et al., 2023;
Zečević et al., 2023). Despite the inherent complexity of directly asking LLMs to provide complete
causal graphs, given the various levels of reasoning involved and the rich context necessary, different
strategies exist to elicit causal graphs from LLMs effectively. These strategies include LLMs to
predict causal relations (Kıcıman et al., 2023; Long et al., 2023a; Willig et al., 2023), LLMs as priors
for data-driven causal discovery methods (Ban et al., 2023), and LLMs to aid in downstream causal
inference tasks by predicting the causal order of variables (Vashishtha et al., 2023).
    To marry the traditional and the LLM-based methods, our work proposes chatPC as a hybrid
approach. Specifically, this work:
   • Frames conditional independence queries as prompts to LLMs and employs the PC algorithm
     with this oracle for causal graph construction.
   • Evaluates the performance of LLMs on conditional independence tests across various problems,
     showing varied performance.
   • Introduces a statistical-based approach for aggregating multiple LLM answers, improving per-
     formance.
   • Examines the graphs predicted by PC with the LLM oracle, finding them to be reasonable.

   • Identifies a general tendency for conservative answers from LLMs compared to experts and
     finds traces of causal reasoning in the model’s answers.
   • Suggests that the approach could mitigate limitations of traditional methods, offering a promis-
     ing avenue for automated causal graph construction.

We argue that relying solely on LLM-based bivariate causal queries inherently overlooks the pres-
ence of mediators, consequently limiting the comprehensive understanding of the full causal graph.
Additionally, our research explores the extent to which LLMs’ queries can effectively substitute
data-driven conditional independence tests, considering that PC represents the current state of the
art in causal discovery, and under a perfect oracle, it can consistently retrieve the ideal graph up to
the Markov equivalence class (Spirtes et al., 2000).

2 Background
This section presents the causal discovery task and briefly reviews different methodologies. We start
by describing classical data-driven causal discovery methods, briefly review the integration of data
and prior knowledge, and present novel approaches to causal discovery using LLMs.

2.1 Data-Driven Causal Discovery
Nowadays, there exists solid and established literature on causal discovery, which is the task of gen-
erating and selecting appropriate causal hypotheses from data. Starting from the seminal work of
Wright (1934), using graphs to represent causal relationships has become widespread in causal anal-
ysis. In the prevalent structural equation model (SEM) formalism, a causal model is described by a
system of equations that implicitly define a directed acyclic graph (DAG) among the variables of the
system through the signature of each equation (Peters et al., 2017). Importantly, in this framework,
a causal model entails a statistical or data-generating process of the data while representing addi-
tional information such as interventional and counterfactual quantities (Peters et al., 2017). Causal
discovery methods in the statistical DAG framework are usually categorized as constrained-based or
score-based approaches (Glymour et al., 2019; Scutari et al., 2019). The former methods attempt the
search for graphs that most closely entail the set of conditional independence relations judged to hold
in the population, while the latter approaches search the spaces of graphs for the best scoring ones
for some score function (e.g., penalized likelihood, such as BIC or AIC scores). A famous example of
score-based methods is the GES algorithm (Meek, 1997; Chickering, 2002a,b), which performs sim-
ple moves (add/remove edges, invert directions) directly in the space of Markov equivalence classes.
The most well-known constrained-based method is the PC-algorithm (Spirtes et al., 2000), which
we briefly describe as it will be central in our successive examples of LLM’s integration. It follows
three basic steps: (i) in the first step conditional independence statements (X ⊥⊥ Y |Z), obtained
through hypothesis tests performed on the data, are used to prune the edges of the fully connected
undirected graph, thus building the so-called skeleton of the causal graph. (ii) In the second step,
colliders are oriented such that invalid conditional (in)dependencies are ruled out. (iii) The third
and last step applies a set of graphical rules (Meek, 1995) to orient as many edges as possible. Both
the GES and the celebrated Peter-Clark (PC) algorithm (Spirtes et al., 2000) have been proven
consistent in the large sample limit under specific assumptions. In particular, if we consider an
oracle for conditional independence statements, and we assume causal sufficiency and faithfulness,
the PC algorithm outputs a partially directed graph that represents the true Markov equivalence
class (without additional assumption, it is impossible, from statistical information alone, to recover
something more then the MEC). We refer the readers to Pearl et al. (2000), Peters et al. (2017) and
recent surveys and reviews Camps-Valls et al. (2023); Glymour et al. (2019); Scutari et al. (2019)
for a more exhaustive overview of causal discovery algorithms and the DAG/SEM formalism.




                                                  3
2.2 Causal Discovery with Prior Knowledge
The need for integrating prior expert knowledge and data-driven methods has been recognized
since initial works on causal discovery (Constantinou et al., 2023). The Bayesian methodology
straightforwardly incorporates prior knowledge with data-driven approaches (Heckerman and Geiger,
1995; Friedman and Koller, 2003; Xu et al., 2015; Suter et al., 2023). Methods have been developed to
build informative priors on graphs based on agreement with prior beliefs of different types (Mukherjee
and Speed, 2008) or with partial prior knowledge (Castelo and Siebes, 2000). Moreover, the Bayesian
paradigm has also been used to integrate data with multiple sources of prior knowledge (Amirkhani
et al., 2016; Werhli and Husmeier, 2007). Prior knowledge integration has also been proposed
and employed in non-Bayesian settings. Classical score-based and constrained-based algorithms
can be combined with different prior knowledge such as structural restrictions (de Campos and
Castellano, 2007; De Campos and Ji, 2011; Li and Beek, 2018) or information on directed causal
paths (Borboudakis and Tsamardinos, 2012, 2013). Similarly, prior knowledge given as ancestral or
topological order constraints can help guide exact methods (Chen et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2021).
Alternatively, human-in-the-loop approaches perform active interactions with the experts to guide
the data-driven methods in discovering the causal graph (Masegosa and Moral, 2013; da Silva et al.,
2023).

2.3 Causality with LLMs
LLMs present a promising knowledge-driven alternative to expert-based graph building or data-
driven causal discovery methods. Different strategies exist to elicit causal graphs from LLMs effec-
tively. These strategies include LLMs to predict causal relations (Kıcıman et al., 2023; Long et al.,
2023a; Willig et al., 2023), LLMs as priors for data-driven causal discovery methods (Ban et al.,
2023), and LLMs to aid in downstream causal inference tasks by predicting the causal order of
variables (Vashishtha et al., 2023). Kıcıman et al. (2023) showcase LLMs’ knowledge-based causal
discovery capabilities by eliciting DAGs for a series of bivariate and multivariate benchmark datasets
where the ground truth is known. Vashishtha et al. (2023) proposed an approach using the topo-
logical order over graph variables, showcasing the potential of LLMs in obtaining causal order and
enhancing causal inference in diverse fields. Long et al. (2023b) and Long et al. (2023a) examined
the role of LLMs in facilitating the construction of causal graphs, focusing on the automation of
edge scoring and the use of expert knowledge to improve data-driven causal graph identification, re-
spectively. Zečević et al. (2023) argued against the causal capabilities of LLMs, defining a subgroup
of Structural Causal Models (SCMs) and emphasizing the reliance of LLMs on embedded causal
knowledge. Cox Jr (2023) conducted a Socratic dialogue with an LLM, highlighting the necessity
of sustained questioning for refining human reasoning imitated by LLMs and the reliability of their
conclusions. Pawlowski et al. (2023) and Gat et al. (2023) explored the use of LLMs in enabling
precise causal reasoning and model-agnostic explanations. Chen et al. (2023) focused on Bayesian
Networks (BNs) and proposed strategies for addressing erroneous prior causal statements in LLM-
based causal structure learning. Zhang et al. (2023) discussed the potential of enhancing causal
machine learning and leveraging LLMs for improved performance and trustworthiness.


3 Conditional Independence Queries via LLM
We start by describing and evaluating conditional independence (CI) queries with LLMs. Specifically,
we are interested in estimating the validity of conditional independence statements of the type:
     Is X independent of Y given Z = (Z1 , Z2 , . . . , Zk )?,
without having access to observations of the involved variables. Instead, we would like to rely on
available or expert knowledge accessible through LLMs.

   For an LLM to be able to answer CI queries, it needs to be presented with some context and
additional information related to the variables of interest alongside their description. In particular,
we assume that for each problem, we have access to the following information:
variables names or acronyms plus a short description for each quantity of interest.
field the general subject area or expert field related to the problem.

context a description of the broad context of the variables under consideration, including relevant
     details that go beyond general knowledge

3.1 Prompting for conditional independence testing
While the approach we propose in this work could be implemented with any LLM trained to fol-
low instructions, we compare GPT-3.5-turbo1 and GPT-42 from OpenAI. The former is a less
computationally-expensive model with good performance over different benchmarks, while the latter
is among the current state-of-the-art LLMs (Chiang et al., 2024). For a CI statement (X ⊥⊥ Y |Z),
we consider a simple prompt that combines the field and context information and a description of
the involved variables (X, Y , and eventually Z) with a general instruction and response template as
follows (see appendix A for a detailed specification of the used prompt):
     Persona specification
     Instructions
     Context
     Variables description
     CI Statement question
     Response template
The persona is based on the field variable and primes the LLM to produce reasoning appropriate
to the area under study. To improve the answers, we apply chain-of-thought prompting following
Wei et al. (2023), enabling us to gain insights into the model’s reasoning and inspect if it is causally
inspired. Further, we ask the model to provide uncertainty about its best guess along the lines of
Tian et al. (2023).

3.2 Testing
Various strategies can be followed in order to perform a “Hypothesis test” for a conditional in-
dependence statement with LLMs. A naive option consists of asking the LLM a single question
with the prompt described in the previous section and decide that a statement is valid (i.e., the
variables are indeed independent) if the answer is YES and invalid, if it is NO. The main problem
with this approach is that since LLMs are probabilistic, a single answer from an LLM does not need
to correspond to its mode (the most likely answer) or could fail to respect the required response
template: its answer could be, for instance, UNCERTAIN instead of YES/NO. Instead, we ask for
an independent batch (size n) of answers, parse the obtained answers (YES or NO) together with
the reported uncertainties, and finally output either an answer based on simple voting or weighted
voting where the weights are the reported probabilities.
    Alternatively, we implement a “statistical approach” where we actually produce p-values for the
null hypothesis pno ≥ pyes (or alternatively pno ≤ pyes ) where pno , pyes are the proportion of NO
and YES answers over the total requested batch n. The constructed test is based on the idea that
we want to test whether the probability of obtaining the answer NO is significantly higher than that
of obtaining the answer YES and vice versa. We opt for the null hypothesis if this difference is not

level α and rejecting the chosen null hypothesis if the p-value is less or equal to α (we will employ
α = 0.05 in the experiments). This last strategy has the advantage of considering the random
variability of the answers and could offer a principled way of controlling the false positive rate. The
user could then specify, for a particular problem, which of the two null hypotheses they would like
to employ (either pno ≥ pyes or pyes ≥ pno ), which in turn implies a different false-positive control
(considering either NO or YES as positive).

3.3 Evaluation
We evaluate the performance for CI testing on various problems defined in the BNLearn reposi-
tory (Scutari and Denis, 2014), the spurious correlation website (Vigen, 2023), a classical problem
on reconstructing protein-signaling networks (Sachs et al., 2005) and a set of graphs on climatic
teleconnections (Kretschmer et al., 2021):
   • cancer Simple causal graph involving four factors influencing the probability of cancer (Korb
     and Nicholson, 2010).
   • burglary The classical earthquake example in (Korb and Nicholson, 2010; Scutari and Denis,
     2014)
   • asia Causal graph of eight factors linked to respiratory problems (Lauritzen and Spiegelhalter,
     1988).
   • sachs: Causal graph among 11 phosphorylated proteins and phospholipids in single-cell data (Sachs
     et al., 2005).
   • spurious: Famous examples of spuriously correlated pairs of variables obtained from the
     spurious-correlation website (Vigen, 2023).

   • bk-spv: Hypothesized pathway from autumn sea ice in the Barents and Kara Seas (BK) to
     the stratospheric polar vortex (SPV) in winter, including various confounding and mediating
     factors (Kretschmer et al., 2021).
   • nao-dk-med: Hypothesized causal relation between summertime North Atlantic Oscillation
     (NAO) and precipitation patterns in Denmark (DK) and the Mediterranean (MED) (Kretschmer
     et al., 2021).
For all graphs except sachs, we evaluate all the possible CI statements over all variables with both
permutations of X and Y . For sachs, we evaluate all CI statements (with X-Y permutations) up to
conditioning sets of size 1. Lastly, for spurious, we evaluate the marginal independence statements
X ⊥⊥ Y for all pairs of variables. For all the experiments, we obtain the answer to the CI queries
by aggregating, as described previously, n = 10 independent batched responses from the LLM.
The code implementing our approach and the problem descriptions are available in the repository
https://github.com/IPL-UV/causal_GPT.

context, consistency means that the result of a conditional independence test should not depend on
the order of variables, i.e., it should be commutative in X and Y (given Z). As a first sanity check,
we checked the consistency of the responses across all CITs, with respect to the change of order of

knowledge or weigh sources against each other (old vs. new; unsystematic vs. scientific). As data-
driven methods also reveal major failure modes, using knowledge-driven evidence to complement
data can be a promising future research avenue.

4 Application to Causal Discovery
We propose to couple the CI oracle or testing, introduced in the previous section, with the PC-
algorithm (Colombo and Maathuis, 2014) for recovery of the Markov equivalence class of a causal
graph. The PC algorithm starts from a fully connected skeleton (undirected graph) among the con-
sidered variables and iteratively removes edges between variables X and Y when it finds a condition-
ing set Z such that X is independent of Y given Z. After the so-called skeleton phase, v-structures
are identified through specific CI testing, and finally, a set of orientation rules are applied (Meek,
1995). Hence, to implement chatPC, we plug in the LLM-based conditional independence testing to
an existing PC implementation (Atienza et al., 2022).
Causal graphs from the examples The PC algorithm should not be run only with LLMs for
arbitrarily large graphs. First, CITs with growing conditioning set size turn even for an expert in-
creasingly into mental gymnastics as you technically verify d-separation. Second, with the increasing
number of variables, the number of required CITs grows exponentially. This renders the approach
under the current energy and financial costs for querying LLMs infeasible unless the graph is small
or one couples it with data-driven CITs. We applied chatPC to the burglary, cancer, asia and
nao-dk-med.
    First, for burglary, chatPC can retrieve the correct assumed causal graph (see fig. 3). This result
stands even though the method made some mistakes in the CITs for burglary. The graph of the
cancer variables (see fig. 4) contains the right sub-graph, which means that the LLM introduced no
incorrect independence. The links between pollution and dyspnoea and smoking and dyspnoea are
absent in the ground truth, but research suggests that such pathways might be assumed (Berliner
et al., 2016). An expert may choose to introduce sparsity into a graph by including only the strongest
links. With LLM-based CITs, this is possible through the significance level of the statistical tests,
which we could tune up to the point where some of these links disappear. It is also noteworthy
that chatPC could not direct any edge in the retrieved graph. In principle, directing edges is a
simpler task that could, in a second step, also be carried out with LLMs (Kıcıman et al., 2023).
For asia (see fig. 6), we find an overall reasonable graph that almost matches the skeleton of the
ground truth. Notable differences are that the asia node is disconnected, smoke is directly connected
to dysp, which aligns with the cancer graph, and xray only has a direct link to bronc. Finally, in
NAO-DK-MED, the hypothesized graph suggests that the summertime North-Atlantic Oscillation is the
single common cause of precipitation patterns in Denmark and the Mediterranean. The LLM would
hence need to identify that DK ⊥  ⊥ MED| NAO, but it fails to do so (see fig. 5). Kretschmer et al.
(2021) assume that the data is detrended, and even then, it is a debatable assumption that there are
no connections left. Hence, the graph retrieved by chatPC is arguably not false. Moreover, one can

to shed light on where we stand in this endeavour.
    We found that LLM conjectures about hidden confounders, showing that they use causal rea-
soning to tackle this primarily statistical task. This, however, is neither consistently nor always
successfully done. The varying performance over different tasks showed that more effort is needed
to steer the models to more efficient causal reasoning. We proved that employing an aggregating
mechanism framed as a statistical test leads to improved performance and effective control over the
retrieved graph’s density in the context of the PC algorithm. The causal graphs predicted by the PC
algorithm with LLM-based CITs appear reasonable. While not infallible, the method demonstrates
potential in capturing meaningful causal relationships, offering a promising avenue for automated
causal graph construction in combination with data-driven methods. Finally, we found that LLMs
generally tend toward conservative answers, contrasting with the often bolder responses from human
experts. Understanding and addressing the cautious nature of LLM reasoning is crucial for refining
the accuracy and reliability of the generated causal graphs. Future work will explore the combina-
tion of data-driven and language-driven causality, where relying on CIT estimates in PC schemes
constitutes a sound framework to seamlessly integrate evidence based on data and knowledge. Here,
RAG-based systems (Lewis et al., 2021) could play a key role by ensuring that retrieved knowledge
stems from scientific sources. The proposed approach could be useful in alignment and evaluating
LLMs. Probing CITs and their implication for a causal graph construction with LLMs is useful to
understand the level of causal reasoning and understanding of such systems. It does not escape our
notice that, despite the many challenges in our pathway to causal reasoning with language models,
the approach can bring many opportunities for scientific discovery.
