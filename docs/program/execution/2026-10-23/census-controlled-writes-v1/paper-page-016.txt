A.5   LLM- ERA HYBRIDS

The closest systems translate model output into a symbolic problem. Logic-LM Pan et al. (2023),
LINC Olausson et al. (2023), SatLM Ye et al. (2023), LeanReasoner Jiang et al. (2024), and
CLOVER Ryu et al. (2025) all make the model translate natural language into a formal object and
then rely on a symbolic executor or verifier. Their target formalisms include logic programming,
FOL, SAT/SMT, and Lean. They also differ in the repair and decomposition performed before
execution. SymbCoT Xu et al. (2024) and Aristotle Xu et al. (2025) keep more of the symbolic rea-
soning trajectory inside LLM prompting, while CaRing Yang et al. (2025) extracts human-readable
proofs from Prolog search logs. These systems motivate the distinction between validating an infer-
ence and checking its premises. CPUNeSy admits source units through a certificate gate and records
the dependencies of conclusions produced by the symbolic core.
Structured generation addresses errors in output syntax. PICARD Scholak et al. (2021) and XGram-
mar Dong et al. (2025) constrain decoding so generated strings remain syntactically valid. This is
useful for reducing parser failures, but a well-formed formula may still assert an unsupported fact.
Datalog engines such as Soufflé Scholz et al. (2016) and provenance semantics for Datalog Bour-
gaux et al. (2022) provide execution and dependency tracking. CPUNeSy combines these operations
with source admission and removal of redundant stored units. Proposition 3 specifies when to cache
a derived result under the stated cost model, and Section 4.4 reports the measured costs.

A.6   N EURAL THEOREM PROVING AND PREMISE SELECTION

GPT-f Polu & Sutskever (2020) introduced transformer step-proving on Metamath; HTPS Lample
et al. (2022) added search-guided expert iteration; DSP Jiang et al. (2023) pipelines informal drafts
to formal sketches; LeanDojo/ReProver Yang et al. (2023) and MagnusHammer Mikuła et al. (2024)
made learned premise selection the standard interface to large libraries; AlphaGeometry Trinh et al.
(2024) demonstrated a neuro-symbolic loop at olympiad level; Li et al. (2024b) surveys the area.
Premise-selection indexes and our kernel store both supply candidates for proof construction. We
compare candidate coverage and selection accuracy against ReProver in Appendix F.2.

A.7   FAILURE MODES : REASONING SHORTCUTS

Marconato et al. Marconato et al. (2023) showed that NeSy predictors can reach high label accuracy
while violating the intended semantics; mitigation requires extra supervision or architectural con-
straints. These findings motivate checking which premises support a prediction. Our experiments
measure the effects of restricted writes, agreement, and source rechecking (Section 4.2).

A.8   L OGICAL - REASONING BENCHMARKS AND PRESSURE TESTS

Recent benchmarks sharpen the empirical boundary for LLM reasoning. LogicBench Parmar et al.
(2024) isolates individual inference rules across propositional, first-order, and non-monotonic pat-
terns; ProverGen/ProverQA Qi et al. (2025) combines LLM generation with symbolic provers to cre-
ate FOL reasoning problems with verified intermediate chains; RuleArena Zhou et al. (2025) moves
rule following into realistic airline, sports, and tax-policy settings. These benchmarks test whether
models follow contextual rules through multiple inference steps. Our L1-HARD, M-HARD, Con-
tractNLI, MedCalc, and LeanDojo evaluations examine how deterministic execution and checks on
model-proposed premises affect errors, abstentions, and cost.
