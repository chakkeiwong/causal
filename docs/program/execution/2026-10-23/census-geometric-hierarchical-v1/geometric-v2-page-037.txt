B.5    Proof of Proposition 5
Proof. We first show that the IV model is linear-Gaussian.
    To do this, we first show the distribution over each noise variable p(ϵv ) is Gaussian with mean
zero. By construction of the GCM, p(ϵv ) is invariant to O. It is ergodic by Assumption 2, since we
can choose a series of transformations ϕn ∈ O that swap the first n elements with the second n (the
same series as we chose for S in Section 6.1). This implies p(ϵv ) must be Gaussian with mean zero,
        iid
i.e. ϵvω ∼ N (0, σ v ), by Freedman’s theorem [Orbanz and Roy, 2015, Kallenberg, 2005, Theorem
1.31].
    We now show the causal mechanisms are linear. We first examine univariate mechanisms y =
f(x). Since the permutation group is a subset of the orthogonal group, S ⊂ O, the mechanisms
must be permutation equivariant. Finite interference (Assumption 3) implies that any mechanism
y = f(x) can be written
                                            yω = g(xω )                                    (74)
for ω ∈ N. The reason is if modifying any one xω′ ̸=ω affected yω , then yω would depend on an
infinite number of other xω′ ̸=ω , by applying permutations that move ω ′ while leaving ω in place.
    Now let 0 = (0)ω∈N and note ϕ(0) = 0 for any rotation ϕ ∈ O. From equivariance, we then have

                                         f(0) = f(ϕ(0)) = ϕ(f(0))                                       (75)

for any ϕ ∈ O. Choose ϕ to be a rotation such that ϕ(x)1 = −x1 . Then we have g(0) = f(0)1 =
ϕ(f(0))1 = −g(0). Hence, g(0) = 0.
    Next, let x = (a, 0, 0, . . .) and note f(x) = (g(a), 0, 0, . . .). Choose ϕθ ∈ O(2) ⊂ O to be a
rotation of the first two elements,
                                                                  
                                            cos θ − sin θ 0 . . .
                                           sin θ cos θ 0 . . .
                                      ϕ= 0                           .                         (76)
                                                                  
                                                     0      1 . . .
                                              ..     ..      ..
                                                                  
                                               .      .         .

Equivariance, f(ϕθ (x)) = ϕθ (f(x)), then implies

                                          g(a cos θ) = g(a) cos θ,                                      (77)

for all a and θ. So, g is linear. We thus have, for some m ∈ R,

                                                yω = mxω                                                (78)

for all ω ∈ N.
    Next consider multivariate mechanisms, y = f(x, z), with equivariance f(ϕ(x), ϕ(z)) = ϕ(f(x, z)).
From permutation equivariance and finite interference, yω = g(xω , zω ), by the argument above.
Setting x = z = 0, we find g(0, 0) = 0, by the argument above. Setting z = 0 and x = (a, 0, 0, . . .),
we obtain g(x, 0) = mx x by the argument above, and likewise g(0, z) = mz z. Now, let x =
(a, 0, 0, . . .) and z = (0, b, 0, 0, . . .). Equivariance then implies,

  f(ϕθ (x), ϕθ (z))2 = g(a sin θ, b cos θ) = sin θ g(a, 0) + cos θ g(0, b) = (sin θ)a mx + (cos θ)b mz , (79)

for all θ and all a, b ∈ R. Thus g is linear in (x, z), and we have, g(x, z) = mx x + mz z.
    The argument extends to more than two inputs, giving g(x1 , . . . , xk ) = m1 x1 + . . . + mk xk , by
applying rotation matrices in higher dimension, ϕ ∈ O(k).
