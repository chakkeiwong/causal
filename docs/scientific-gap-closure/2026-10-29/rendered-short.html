Public data permits that exercise before a bank commits staff time, customer records and money to a pilot. Its value depends on which question it answers. A public experiment can test whether an analysis preserves the population, intervention and uncertainty. It cannot establish that the same intervention works for a bank’s customers. The National Supported Work demonstration provides a concrete example. In the retained experimental subset, the estimated increase in 1978 earnings is about $1,794, with an ap- proximate 95% interval from $479 to $3,109. These are the archive’s earnings units, without conversion to today’s purchasing power. The result supports a positive average contrast under the stated design assumptions. It does not establish that the gain exceeds an illustrative $1,000 cost. That distinction is exactly the distinction a banking proposal must preserve when discussing a profitable offer or servicing intervention. 1.1 The population is part of the question The author archive supplies separate files for 185 treated and 260 control observations with earnings in 1974, 1975 and 1978. Chapter 2 Repeated decisions and customers who disappear A servicing policy changes what happens next. A reminder, payment holiday or collection action affects both the customer’s state and the information the bank subsequently observes. Estimating a policy from customers who remain in the records can therefore answer a different question from the one the bank faces. The relevant value averages over the initial eligible population, including customers whose later outcomes are missing. An explicitly generated study makes this distinction measurable. With a correct observation model, weighting recovers the chosen policy’s value on average, but repeated actions and dropout leave little effective information. When dropout depends on an unobserved future shock, the same calculation becomes biased and its nominal 95% interval covers the numerical reference in only 80% of replications. Analysing complete observed trajectories also overstates value. These are findings under the stated constructed law, not estimates of a bank’s servicing policy. 2.1 A policy changes a path, not just a row Each generated customer has a fixed characteristic X ∼ N (0, 1) and initial state L 0 = 0.4X +ε 0 . There are three decisions, t = 0, 1, 2. The historical action is binary, with probability p t = clip{expit(0.3X − 0.5L t + 0.2A t−1 ), 0.2, 0.8}, A −1 = 0. Clipping is a feature of this generated assignment law, not a silent alteration of weights estimated from observed bank data. Chapter 4 When a bank in the data ceases to be the same bank A funding study follows institutions through time. Its economic interpretation requires the deposit outcome and the exposure to refer to the same institution. A fixed effect removes a persistent level difference; it cannot repair an outcome assigned to the wrong institution. This distinction matters before a bank spends money on a more elaborate covariance estimator. 4.1 A controlled comparison The generated study follows 60 institutions for 20 dates. Exposure follows x i,t = 0.5x i,t−1 + v i,t , starting from zero, and the outcome is y i,t = α i + 0.2 sin(t/3) + 0.4x i,t + u i,t . The institution effects, exposure innovations and outcome disturbances are independent standard normal draws. The target slope is therefore 0.4 in this specified economy. It is an illustrative response, not an estimated bank elasticity. One alternative adds a common innovation with an institution-specific loading. Another circularly reassigns the outcomes of five institutions after the tenth date while leaving their exposures unchanged. The latter is an artificial record error, not an authentic merger history. There are 300 independent panels in each of the three cases. We compare a pooled regression with a regression that removes institution and date means. For the latter, the uncertainty calculations use observation-level HC2, institution-clustered covariance, or the sum of institution and date cluster covariances less the observation intersection. Reported intervals use the normal critical value 1.96. These are finite experiments with 20 dates, not a general justification for that reference distribution. 4.2 What changed the conclusion In clean panels, the pooled HC2 interval covers the target in 82.7% of requests. Within- institution and date adjustment gives 94.7% with HC2 and 94.3% with institution clustering. Monte Carlo standard errors for these coverage fractions are about 1.3–2.2 percentage points. Removing a nuisance level and repairing a sampling calculation are different operations. With reassigned outcomes, the within estimate has bias −0.0170, with Monte Carlo standard error 0.00169. The evaluator’s correct mapping gives bias 0.00102 and institution-cluster coverage 95.0%. The point is the remaining bias: a wider interval can sometimes cover a target without restoring the meaning of the observation. A supplied warning identifies all 300 deliberately corrupted panels and prompts refusal; this says nothing about a system’s ability to discover an unknown break. The next useful evidence would join exposures, outcomes and institution histories using externally checked identifiers, then test whether the estimated response survives the corrected economic unit. Chapter 5 An uncertain customer identity changes the economic question A customer table and an outcome table do not become one economic sample merely because a matching algorithm returns a name. A wrong match assigns somebody else’s outcome to the intervention. An unmatched record removes an outcome from the comparison. These are different errors, and either can change the case for spending. The generated study has 1,000 independent people. A random intervention A i ∼ Bernoulli(1/2) is independent of a characteristic X i ∼ U [−1, 1] and disturbance ϵ i ∼ U [−1, 1]. Outcomes satisfy Y i = 100 + 30A i + 10X i + 20ϵ i . The population effect is 30 illustrative units and all outcomes lie in [70, 160]. Names and birth- day categories create deliberately artificial collisions. Independently assigned row numbers and shuffled records carry no cross-table identity. The analyst sees intervention and characteristics in one table, and identifiers and outcome in the other. True links remain in a separate evaluator table. We corrupt exactly zero, 10% or 30% of outcome records. Corruption alternates between a missing name, a one-character edit, another person’s identifiers and a conflicting duplicate outcome. Records are chosen either independently, in proportion to 1 + 0.8X, or with greater probability for larger outcomes. There are 200 independent replications of each of the nine cases. This construction tests consequences of specified errors; it does not estimate the prevalence or form of errors in a bank. 5.1 What matching can and cannot establish The simplest analysis accepts a unique exact name-and-date match. A fuzzy alternative permits a one-character name difference at the same date and uses date candidates when the name is missing. Both abstain when a candidate is ambiguous or the same outcome is proposed for several people. Neither uses the true identity or the intervention outcome to choose a match. With outcome-dependent corruption at 30%, exact matching retains 65.6% of people and assigns a wrong outcome to 1.56% of the original population. Its effect estimate has bias −0.560 (Monte Carlo standard error 0.071); its normal interval covers the population effect in 93.5% of replications. The fuzzy rule retains 62.8%, with wrong matches for 0.477% of the initial population. Its bias is −0.071 (Monte Carlo standard error 0.079) and coverage is 94.5%. 16 5.2. Chapter 7 What a causal reasoning test measures A banking analyst asks several different questions of an assistant. Can it translate a request for higher deposits into an intervention and a measurable target? Can it distinguish information known before an offer from information created by the offer? Can it explain which arrows in a diagram are assumptions, and recognise when several diagrams fit the same observations? A correct answer to a narrative puzzle is useful evidence about some of these abilities, but it does not establish all of them. 7.1 Explanations deserve their own test Wang and Shen examine three constructed scenarios: coin operations, interrupted file downloads and a hypothetical vaccination calculation. Four variants of each scenario and three prompting approaches give 36 prompt conditions. Their scoring distinguishes a correct result and explanation, a flawed explanation, and an incorrect result. This is valuable because a correct number can accompany a mistaken economic argument. The study reports mixed changes from examples and guided reasoning, rather than a uniformly helpful prompting intervention. (Wang and Shen, 2024) The terms used in a benchmark also need scrutiny. The paper calls reasoning from an observed effect to a cause actual causality. That task description does not, by itself, specify a formal criterion for an individual’s actual cause. Its smallpox case asks which source produces more deaths in a stipulated population. With the given numbers, vaccination reactions produce 990,000(0.01)(0.01) = 99 deaths, while disease among unvaccinated people produces 10,000(0.02)(0.20) = 40. Those population counts differ from the per-person risks, 0.0001 and 0.004. Confusing a total with a risk is an estimand error; the arithmetic alone does not establish a general Simpson-reversal mechanism. These are deductions from the paper’s stated example, not new medical evidence. The banking analogue is direct. A large customer group can contribute more defaults while offering a smaller default risk per account. A deposit offer can increase gross balances without increasing net contribution. An assistant should state the denominator and economic target before presenting the answer. The paper’s prompts provide useful stress cases for that distinction. The small scenario set and model configurations do not support a current ranking of assistants for banking work. A useful banking comparison 7.4 23 A useful banking comparison The proposal should use such benchmarks to diagnose formulation and reasoning failures, then add tasks whose consequences can be checked independently. A compact questionnaire and an executable calculation are necessary comparators. For a deposit question, all methods should receive the same decision date, eligibility rule, alternative offer and contribution definition. The public experimental, servicing and linkage studies in this volume provide numerical cases of that kind, but no live assistant has yet been evaluated on them. The inspected papers therefore sharpen the proposed experiment rather than establishing a benefit from the proposed service. The useful next comparison measures consequential mistakes and time on held-out tasks, with human adjudication and a fixed cost allowance.
