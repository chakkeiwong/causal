   To connect identification with a financial decision, stipulate a benefit of USD 100 for each
additional historical repayment and an offer cost of USD 5 per customer. These invented values
define net benefit 100τ − 5, not observed bank returns. The standard-recovery population
set permits net values from USD −13.70 to USD 10.40. Intensive recovery narrows this to
USD 0.60 through USD 6.08; complete stable recovery gives USD 5.00. Those are population
implications. A finite-sample spending recommendation needs its own nonempty net-benefit
confidence set above zero, and follow-up costs must also be counted. A positive effect alone is
insufficient to clear the USD 5 threshold.
   At full invitation under intensive recovery, only 5 of 500 bounded sets and 22 normal bands
have a nonempty net-benefit range wholly above zero. The earlier counts of 401 and 469
concerned a positive effect, before deducting the offer cost. Under complete stable recovery, the
corresponding net-positive counts are 335 and 432. These are decision frequencies in the same
retained datasets. They show how a small population margin can remain hard to distinguish
from zero even after follow-up reaches every missing customer. The normal-band frequencies
retain their empirical qualification; more affirmative decisions alone do not establish a better
procedure.
   The resulting design choice is concrete. The bank should establish whether follow-up recovers
the fixed historical endpoint, record every invitation and completion, and compare the cost
of reducing the unresolved mass with the cost of estimating a fixed set more precisely. A
workflow that cannot defend stability should expose an appropriately justified sensitivity range
or withhold the historical-effect recommendation. These generated results close the missing
selective-completion argument for a bounded single-treatment example. They do not establish
feasible bank recovery, a general solution for unbounded longitudinal rewards, or independent
operating acceptance.

15.1      Repeated measurements do not specify the identification
          strategy
A panel of branch deposits can support several different questions. One can model a homoge-
neous response to price after removing stable branch differences, identify an adoption-cohort
effect from untreated trends, fit an interactive-factor counterfactual, or estimate a dynamic
equation using lagged instruments. All use a rectangular data set; their counterfactual
assumptions are different.
  Let i = 1, . . . , N index branches and t = 1, . . . , T calendar periods. Write Yit (āt ) for the
outcome under an action history. The shorter Yit (at ) assumes away carryover. Let Gi be the first
adoption date only when an absorbing intervention makes that description meaningful. A price
that rises and falls, a repeated collections action and a permanent branch rollout do not share
the same treatment history merely because each is coded as a binary column. Arkhangelsky
and Imbens (2024, Sections 2–4) emphasizes assignment pattern, potential-outcome dynamics
and the relative sizes of N, T as distinct organizing dimensions.
  Banking measurements also change composition. Branch closure can remove a low-performing
unit, household transfers can move balances between branches, and a merger can change the
entity represented by an identifier. An unbalanced panel is not automatically missing at
random. The outcome and unit definitions of Chapter 6 remain part of the panel estimand.
