6.1     Begin with the intervention, unit and observation process
The annual repricing example distinguished assignment from receipt. This chapter uses a
separate ninety-day activation example to show what changes when customers choose whether
to take up an invitation. Its outcome and action symbols are local to that example.
   Consider a household eligible for a preferential deposit product. Let Z denote the bank’s
invitation, A actual activation, M a subsequent change in balances and Y the ninety-day
funding contribution. An intervention on Z is operationally different from forcing A = 1.
An increase in the database field recording A is different again. The bank can randomize
an invitation; it cannot generally compel activation or create liquidity merely by changing a
recorded balance. A causal question must identify the mechanism that the proposed decision
actually changes.
   The unit is a household rather than an account when money can move between accounts
owned by the same people. The outcome includes an agreed transfer price, interest paid and
servicing cost, with movements within the household consolidated. This is part of defining
Y , rather than a correction performed after estimating an account-level effect. For a fixed
population and horizon, write Y (a) for the outcome under a specified activation intervention,
and Y Z=z for the outcome under invitation assignment z. Two initial targets are
                     θA = E{Y (1) − Y (0)},         θZ = E{Y Z=1 − Y Z=0 }.                (6.1)
They differ even in an ideal randomized invitation experiment. An invitation may have costs or
affect trust without activation. Such effects belong to θZ and threaten an exclusion restriction
used to infer θA .
  Consistency means that a household exposed to the specified action has its corresponding
potential outcome. It requires clarity about price, duration, channel, eligibility, execution and
outcome measurement. It is not ensured by giving all those interventions the same binary
code. Interference would require Yi (a), or a justified exposure mapping, instead of Yi (ai ).
The present single-period derivations assume a population of noninterfering units and a fixed
version of each action. Cluster or network designs must change those assumptions explicitly
(Hernán and Robins, 2020, Chapters 1–3).
  For clarity most graph calculations below use discrete variables and sums. Continuous
versions use conditional densities or probability kernels, with absolute continuity and inte-
grability stated at the estimation stage. This distinction matters: an intervention at one

6.2     Structural equations define what an intervention changes
Definition 6.1 (Acyclic structural model). An acyclic structural causal model M consists of
endogenous variables V = (V1 , . . . , Vd ), exogenous variables U , a joint law PU , and assignments

                              Vj := fj (Vpa(j) , Uj ),   j = 1, . . . , d,

whose endogenous parent graph is a DAG. A hard intervention do(A = a) replaces the
assignment for A by the constant a and retains the remaining assignments and exogenous law.

   The colon distinguishes an assignment from an algebraic identity that may be inverted
freely. A regression residual is constructed to be orthogonal to fitted regressors; a structural
disturbance represents omitted causes and need not be orthogonal to the action. Autonomous
mechanisms are what make intervention replacement meaningful (Pearl, 2009, Sections 3.1–3.2).
   If all Uj are mutually independent, the model is Markovian relative to its displayed endoge-
nous DAG. Hidden common causes can instead be displayed as latent parents, producing a
larger DAG, or left as dependence in PU . One must not draw an observed DAG and silently
assume independent disturbances after omitting a common cause. Both the functions and the
disturbance law are components of the causal model.
   Potential outcomes can be generated by recursively evaluating the modified assignments
at the same exogenous realization: Y (a) = fY (. . . ; a, U ). This constructs consistency and
expresses P {Y (a)} as the distribution of Y in the intervened model. Conversely, a potential-
outcome analysis need not posit mutually independent disturbances for every structural
equation. In particular, assumptions sufficient to identify each single-world intervention
distribution need not determine joint quantities involving incompatible interventions.
   The SWIG construction makes this distinction explicit. Split an action node into its random
part A, retaining incoming arrows, and a fixed part a, retaining outgoing arrows. Relabel its
descendants as counterfactual variables. For X → A, X → Y , A → Y this yields a graph for
(X, A, Y (a)) in which A ⊥ Y (a) | X can be read by separation. It does not simultaneously
supply a graph for the unobserved pair (Y (0), Y (1)). The SWIG framework covers the less
restrictive FFRCISTG model as well as the independent-error structural submodel (Richardson
and Robins, 2013, Section 2, node splitting and modularity). Consequently, identifying a
mean effect does not identify individual gains or their variance: Var{Y (1) − Y (0)} additionally
involves the unobserved Cov{Y (1), Y (0)}.

stored aggregate changes the underlying transactions. Deterministic accounting identities also
create conditional dependencies that require care in discovery algorithms relying on generic
faithfulness assumptions.


6.4     Paths, colliders, latent variables and learned graphs
A path ignores the direction of its successive edges. An internal node is a collider when
both adjacent edges have arrowheads into it. A DAG path is active conditional on S when
every noncollider is outside S and every collider has itself or a descendant in S. Two sets
are d-separated when no such path connects them. In a distribution Markov to the DAG,
d-separation implies conditional independence (Pearl, 2009, Definition 1). Faithfulness is the
additional converse assumption used by many discovery procedures; it is not needed to deduce
20                                                                         Causal analysis for a universal bank


independences from a stipulated causal graph.
  In the bank graph, conditioning on X blocks A ← X → Y but leaves A ← U → Y open.
Adding M can block a causal path and therefore change the target. Conditioning on A opens
the collider Z → A ← U → Y : invitation and latent need need not remain independent among
activators. This is why analyzing compliance strata defined by observed receipt is not the
same as randomizing receipt. Conditioning on C = 1 can also open A → C ← V → Y .
  For a numerical collider example, let A, V be independent fair binary variables, Y = V ,
and let C = 1 exactly when A = V . Action has zero causal effect on Y . Among records with
C = 1, however, Y = A, so the observed contrast is one. Positive marginal action probabilities
and perfect outcome prediction within the selected sample do not repair selection bias.
   Marginalizing latent nodes can yield an acyclic directed mixed graph (ADMG). In the
simple common-cause motif, A ← U → Y becomes A ↔ Y ; a directed latent path can induce
a directed observed edge. Directed and bidirected edges can coexist. The corresponding
separation rule, m-separation, uses arrowheads to define colliders in the same way. A bidirected
edge records a permitted hidden common-cause route; it is not an instruction to regress in two
directions. With selection and more general latent structures, maximal ancestral graphs and
PAGs require their own semantics; an ADMG is not interchangeable with those objects. The
latent-time-series setting of Gerhardus and Runge (2020, Section 2), for example, explicitly
excludes selection and targets a time-window-dependent ancestral equivalence object.
   A DAG supplied by an analyst, a CPDAG representing a DAG Markov-equivalence class,
and a PAG representing latent/selection-compatible ancestral graphs are different inputs.
For DAGs the same skeleton and unshielded colliders give the same implied conditional
independences (Peters et al., 2017, Definition 6.24 and Lemma 6.25). Two variables alone do
not observationally distinguish A → Y from Y → A. Known timing may exclude the latter but
still leave hidden confounding. A discovery score cannot turn the remaining graph ambiguity
into a unique causal effect by choosing whichever orientation permits an estimator to run.

6.5.3    District decomposition and an obstruction certificate

In a semi-Markovian ADMG a district is a maximal set joined by bidirected paths. A singleton
without a bidirected neighbor is also a district. For S ⊆ V , define the interventional kernel
Q[S] = p(s | do(v \ s)). It is a function of s and the fixed outside values; in general it is not
the ordinary conditional p(s | v \ s). Shared hidden disturbances couple variables within a
district. Integrating over distinct independent latent groups factorizes an intervention kernel
over districts in its remaining graph.
   The ID algorithm exploits this decomposition recursively. With no intervention it marginal-
izes the supplied law. It first restricts to ancestors of the requested outcome, summing
irrelevant variables. It can then add interventions on remaining nonancestors in the graph
with the original intervention nodes removed, because they cannot change the target in that
graph. When the remaining graph has several districts, it identifies their kernels separately,
multiplies them, and marginalizes nonoutcome variables. When one district remains, it either
extracts topologically ordered conditional factors for a district already available in the current
kernel, recurses inside a larger containing district, or returns a failure witness. These steps
are made precise in Shpitser and Pearl (2006, Section 4, Figure 2); they apply to its specified
semi-Markovian model class.
   The obstruction is a hedge. An R-rooted C-forest is a bidirected-connected subgraph whose
observed nodes have at most one directed child, with childless root set R. Two such forests
F ′ ⊂ F form a hedge for the query when F meets the intervention set, F ′ does not, and their
common roots are ancestors of the outcome in the intervened graph. The source proves that
such a pair witnesses nonidentification and establishes completeness of ID for the specified
queries. These are inherited theorems; the following small case makes their practical meaning
concrete.
   For the bow graph A → Y , A ↔ Y , take F = {A, Y } and F ′ = {Y }, with common root Y .
The larger forest contains A and the smaller does not. Thus failure to identify p(y | do(a)) is
certified, rather than merely caused by a search routine finding no adjustment set. Section 6.6
constructs two positive observed-law examples witnessing that same failure. The front-door
graph avoids this obstruction for the total effect through an identifiable composition of district
kernels.
  Conditional interventional queries require another distinction. Dividing an identified p(y, z |
do(a)) by its z margin works when that joint law is identified and the denominator is positive,
6.5. Identification is a property of a model class                                              23


but failure to identify the joint law need not settle the conditional query. The IDC algorithm
first exchanges eligible conditioning variables for interventions using do-calculus, then invokes
ID and normalizes the remaining joint kernel (Shpitser and Pearl, 2006, Section 5, Figure 3).
Its input is a conditional interventional distribution, not a generic cross-world mediation or
personalized counterfactual request.

6.7.1    Edge assignments and a single-world alternative

For the simple independent-error system M = fM (A, X, UM ) and Y = fY (A, M, X, UY ), with
conditional independence of the relevant disturbances given X, an edge intervention can send
a′ along A → M and a along A → Y . The resulting response is fY (a, fM (a′ , X, UM ), X, UY ).
Integrating the independent disturbances yields exactly the mediation functional above. This
derivation makes its stronger model explicit: splitting a node into different edge values is not
the same intervention as setting the single node once. A manipulation of a composite product
that changes only a pricing channel also requires the claimed separation of those mechanisms
to have operational meaning.
   Now introduce a post-action covariate L that affects both M and Y . The natural response
Y (a, M (a′ )) involves L(a) in the outcome path and L(a′ ) inside the mediator path. The
observed conditional kernels do not generally determine the joint law of these two counterfactual
values. The same node would have to transmit different versions of its history along its outgoing
paths; ordinary single-world g-computation does not supply that joint law.
   A feasible alternative can be defined by a new randomized mediator draw Ga′ from p(m |
A = a′ , X), conditionally independent of the customer’s other innovations given X. Under
action exchangeability, mediator–outcome exchangeability given (A, L, X), consistency and
the requisite cross-regime support, direct mechanism replacement identifies the project-defined
target

define yet another target. A product must preserve the chosen mechanism and conditioning
information instead of returning all of these as one “indirect effect.”

6.8    What the evidence allows us to conclude
The mathematical input is an intervention, target population, observed information and
model class. Its identification output is either a functional with an assumption-dependent
derivation, a set of possible values, or a demonstrated failure of identification. A missing
software implementation is a fourth state: it says nothing about identification.
  For the graph in Figure 6.1, a result cannot be labeled “adjusted ATE” after simply fitting Y
on (A, X). A valid result might instead report the randomized invitation effect, the consistency
bounds, or an IV analysis with extra assumptions. If multiple candidate graphs remain,
compute the effect for each supported graph or carry their union of identified sets. Reporting
the spread across two handpicked graphs is only a sensitivity exercise for those graphs, not a
confidence interval covering all plausible causal structures.
   The initial product can implement explicit DAG back-door checks and registered functional
templates with human-supplied assumptions. It should record variable meanings, graph version,
intervention transformation, required kernels, support restrictions and target population
weights. Discovery and automated data preparation can propose inputs to this process; they
cannot certify the absence of unmeasured causes. The next chapter shows why an instrumental-
variable route changes the returned object even when it uses the same customers, actions and
outcomes.

7.1     Why an invitation is not an adjustment covariate
Suppress X, M, C temporarily and consider Z → A → Y with A ← U → Y . In a randomized
invitation study, Z changes receipt while U confounds receipt and outcome. The graph
expresses that Z is independent of the common causes and that all directed paths from Z to Y
pass through A. Neither condition is equivalent to Z ⊥ Y | A: conditioning on receipt opens
the collider path through U . An empirical association between Z and Y among activators is
therefore not by itself evidence against exclusion.
   The graph alone does not generally identify E[Y (1) − Y (0)] (Pearl, 2009, Section 3.5). Linear
homogeneous effects, monotone binary compliance, and a nonparametric structural response
each add different restrictions. This chapter develops the first two and connects them to the
inverse problem that arises in the third. Calling all three outputs an “IV effect” would lose
the scientific distinction.

7.3.1       A constructive failure to identify the population ATE

7.8     Additional assumptions change the answer
The invitation experiment can return an intention-to-treat effect under randomization. A
homogeneous linear model can return a structural coefficient and moment-based uncertainty
under rank conditions. Monotone binary compliance can return a complier effect and its
population weights. An unrestricted IV graph can instead return bounds or an explicit
nonidentification result. A regularized inverse problem returns an estimate conditional on a
function class, operator restrictions and regularization.
   Each result must carry the original intervention, population, assumptions, first-stage evidence,
score or likelihood, dependence unit and uncertainty method. A weak first stage should
affect the inference state; a disputed exclusion should affect the causal assumption state.
Combining them into one opaque confidence score would conceal which remedy is required.
Additional randomization can improve support; a different encouragement may improve
relevance; measuring an omitted mechanism may alter the graph. None of these changes is
equivalent to running a more accurate predictor.
