2 Background
This section presents the causal discovery task and briefly reviews different methodologies. We start
by describing classical data-driven causal discovery methods, briefly review the integration of data
and prior knowledge, and present novel approaches to causal discovery using LLMs.

2.1 Data-Driven Causal Discovery
Nowadays, there exists solid and established literature on causal discovery, which is the task of gen-
erating and selecting appropriate causal hypotheses from data. Starting from the seminal work of
Wright (1934), using graphs to represent causal relationships has become widespread in causal anal-
ysis. In the prevalent structural equation model (SEM) formalism, a causal model is described by a
system of equations that implicitly define a directed acyclic graph (DAG) among the variables of the
system through the signature of each equation (Peters et al., 2017). Importantly, in this framework,
a causal model entails a statistical or data-generating process of the data while representing addi-
tional information such as interventional and counterfactual quantities (Peters et al., 2017). Causal
discovery methods in the statistical DAG framework are usually categorized as constrained-based or
score-based approaches (Glymour et al., 2019; Scutari et al., 2019). The former methods attempt the
search for graphs that most closely entail the set of conditional independence relations judged to hold
in the population, while the latter approaches search the spaces of graphs for the best scoring ones
for some score function (e.g., penalized likelihood, such as BIC or AIC scores). A famous example of
score-based methods is the GES algorithm (Meek, 1997; Chickering, 2002a,b), which performs sim-
ple moves (add/remove edges, invert directions) directly in the space of Markov equivalence classes.
The most well-known constrained-based method is the PC-algorithm (Spirtes et al., 2000), which
we briefly describe as it will be central in our successive examples of LLM’s integration. It follows
three basic steps: (i) in the first step conditional independence statements (X ⊥⊥ Y |Z), obtained
through hypothesis tests performed on the data, are used to prune the edges of the fully connected
undirected graph, thus building the so-called skeleton of the causal graph. (ii) In the second step,
colliders are oriented such that invalid conditional (in)dependencies are ruled out. (iii) The third
and last step applies a set of graphical rules (Meek, 1995) to orient as many edges as possible. Both
the GES and the celebrated Peter-Clark (PC) algorithm (Spirtes et al., 2000) have been proven
consistent in the large sample limit under specific assumptions. In particular, if we consider an
oracle for conditional independence statements, and we assume causal sufficiency and faithfulness,
the PC algorithm outputs a partially directed graph that represents the true Markov equivalence
class (without additional assumption, it is impossible, from statistical information alone, to recover
something more then the MEC). We refer the readers to Pearl et al. (2000), Peters et al. (2017) and
recent surveys and reviews Camps-Valls et al. (2023); Glymour et al. (2019); Scutari et al. (2019)
for a more exhaustive overview of causal discovery algorithms and the DAG/SEM formalism.
