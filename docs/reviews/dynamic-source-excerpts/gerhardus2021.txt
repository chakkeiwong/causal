                                                                                               Abstract
                                                 Generalizing directed maximal ancestral graphs, we introduce a class of graphical models for rep-
                                                 resenting time lag specific causal relationships and independencies among finitely many regularly
                                                 sampled and regularly subsampled time steps of multivariate time series with unobserved variables.
                                                 We completely characterize these graphs and show that they entail constraints beyond those that
                                                 have previously been considered in the literature. This allows for stronger causal inferences with-
                                                 out having imposed additional assumptions. In generalization of directed partial ancestral graphs
                                                 we further introduce a graphical representation of Markov equivalence classes of the novel type of
                                                 graphs and show that these are more informative than what current state-of-the-art causal discovery
                                                 algorithms learn. We also analyze the additional information gained by increasing the number of
                                                 observed time steps.

1    Introduction
In recent decades causal graphical models have become a standard tool for reasoning about causal relationships,
see e.g. [18, 27, 9]. The most basic and popular class of models are directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), which in their
interpretation as causal Bayesian networks allow to derive interventional distributions and causal effects from
the observational distribution, see e.g. [26, 16, 17]. This model class is restricted to acyclic causal relationships
among variables that are not subject to latent confounding, i.e., such that there are no unobserved common
causes of observed variables. The latter assumption is known as causal sufficiency and intuitively entails that
all variables relevant for describing the system’s causal relationships are modeled explicitly. If this cannot be
asserted, as will often be the case in practice, one approach is to work with maximal ancestral graphs (MAGs)
[22, 33]. This larger class of graphs retains a well-defined causal interpretation in presence of latent confounding
and in addition allows for selection variables, i.e., for unobserved variables that determine which units belong to
the observed population. In this paper we rule out selection variables by assumption. It is then sufficient to work
with a subclass of MAGs that in [10] were termed directed maximal ancestral graphs (DMAGs).
The use of any of these model classes for causal reasoning requires to already know the system’s causal structure
in form of the respective graph. This will often be not the case. If experiments are infeasible one must then rely
on observational causal discovery, see e.g. [27, 21], which refers to the task of learning causal relationships from
observational data under suitable enabling assumptions. So-called independence-based methods, also referred to
as constraint-based methods, attempt to learn the causal graphical model from independencies in the observed
probability distribution. In general this is an under-determined problem because distinct graphs may describe
the same set of independencies, a phenomenon referred to as Markov equivalence. Without further assumptions
it is then only possible to learn those features of the causal graphical model that it shares with all its Markov
equivalent graphs. These shared features can in turn be represented by certain types of graphs, which for the case
of MAGs are known as partial ancestral graphs (PAGs) [1, 34]. There are sound and complete causal discovery
algorithms for learning PAGs from observational data, for example the FCI algorithm [28, 27, 34], where the
attribute sound refers to correctness of the method and complete to it learning all features that are shared across
the Markov equivalence class. The refinement of PAGs obtained when restricting from MAGs to DMAGs are
called directed partial ancestral graphs (DPAGs) [10].
The causal graphical model framework outlined above does not inherently rely on temporal information, and the
non-temporal setting up to this date appears to be its major mode of application. However, dynamical systems
and time series data are ubiquitous and of great interest to science and beyond. In this setting Granger causality
[8] is a widely-used framework for causal analyses. It is based on a predictive notion of causality, where a causal
influence of time series X on time series Y is inferred if the past of X helps in predicting the present of Y given
that the pasts of Y and all other time series except X are already known. As central limitations, Granger causality
requires the absence of latent confounders, i.e., unobserved time series that are a common cause of two observed
time series, and it cannot in general deal with contemporaneous causal influences, i.e., causal influences on a
time scale shorter than the sampling interval. See e.g. chapter 10 of [21] for an in-depth discussion. Since the
causal graphical model framework is not subject to these limitations, in recent years there has been a growing
interest in adapting it to the time series setting. At a basic level this can be achieved straightforwardly by
using graphs that include one vertex per variable and time step—the general theory then simply carries over.
However, in addition to statistical complications related to autocorrelation, see e.g. [25, 23, 7] for discussions in
the context of causal discovery, the temporal information imposes constraints on the allowed graphs: In causal
graphical models directed edges signify the direction of causal influences and hence must not point backwards
in time. Moreover, if the causal relationships are assumed to be persistent in time, a property referred to as
causal stationarity, the graph must reflect this by having edges that are repetitive in time. Regarding DAGs that
graphically represent time series without latent confounders, there are no constraints beyond these unless imposed
by additional assumptions. While there are works on independence-based time series causal discovery that allow
for latent confounding [4, 13, 7], no characterization of the associated class of graphical models has been given.


                                                         3
This is the conceptual gap that we close in this work, i.e., we completely characterize which DMAGs can serve
as causal graphical model for causally stationary time series with latent confounders. The novel model class
defined by this characterization entails constraints that are strictly stronger than those currently considered in the
literature and, thus, without having imposed additional assumptions allows to draw stronger causal inferences
than the previously considered model classes. We also introduce graphical representations of Markov equivalence
classes of the novel type of graphs and show that these are more informative than the graphs learned by current
algorithms for causal discovery in time series with latent confounders, i.e., these algorithms are not complete.
It should be noted that, since contemporaneous causal interactions are allowed without restrictions other than
acyclicity, the time series case considered here formally subsumes and is thus strictly more general than the
non-temporal case.
The structure of this paper is as follows: In Sec. 2 we begin by specifying the considered type of causally station-
ary time series processes. We then introduce ts-DMAGs, a class of causal graphical models for representing the
causal relationships and independencies among only the observed variables of such processes at finitely many
regularly spaced observed time steps. This construction recognizes that, in addition to some component time
series potentially being unobserved altogether, in practice one is always restricted to finitely many observed time
steps. In Sec. 3 we analyze the newly defined class of causal graphical models in detail and derive several con-
straints that ts-DMAGs necessarily obey. The analysis culminates in the complete characterization of ts-DMAGs
by a single necessary and sufficient condition in Theorems 1 and 2. We further show that this characterization
imposes constraints stronger than those that have previously been considered in the literature. For this reason ts-
DMAGs allow for stronger causal inferences than the previously considered model classes, as we also concretely
demonstrate with examples. Along the way we further introduce the concept of stationarifications in order to
illuminate various discussions. In Sec. 4 we put these developments to use in the context of causal discovery
by defining ts-DPAGs as representations of the Markov equivalence classes of ts-DMAGs. We show that these
graphs are more informative than the output of current causal discovery algorithms and point out a conceptual
misunderstanding that, as we argue, has misguided recent developments in this regard. Lastly, in Sec. 5 we
compare ts-DMAGs and ts-DPAGs with different numbers of observed time steps and define the limiting objects
obtained by sending this number to infinity. In Sec. A we summarize basic graphical concepts and introduce our
related notation. The proofs of all theoretical results are given in Sec. B.



2     A class of causal graphical models for time series with latent con-
      founders
In this section we specify the considered type of time series processes and explain how, in case there are no
unobserved variables, certain DAGs with an infinite number of vertices can model these as causal Bayesian
networks. By marginalizing these infinite DAGs we then introduce ts-DMAGs, a generalization of DMAGs for
representing the causal relationships and independencies among only the observed variables of such processes at
a finite number of regularly samples or regularly subsampled observed time steps. These graphs constitute the
novel class of causal graphical models that is the central topic in this paper.

2.2    Time series DAGs

The information about causal parentships as specified by a structural causal model can be represented by its
causal Bayesian network / causal graph, see e.g. [27, 18, 21]. This is a directed graph with one vertex per
variable, typically excluding the noise variables, and a directed edge from each variable to all variables of which
it is a causal parent. In other words, causal parentship agrees with graphical parentship. This construction also
applies to the structural processes as in eq. (1) and we here introduce several notions for characterizing the
structure of the corresponding causal graphs.
First, to capture that a vertex represents a random variable Vti that corresponds to a particular time step t of a
particular component time series V i we employ the following notion:

Definition 2.1 (Time series structure). A graph G = (V, E) has a time series structure if V = I × T, where
I = {1, 2, . . . , n} with n ≥ 1 is the variable index set and T = {t ∈ Z | ts ≤ t ≤ te } with ts ∈ Z ∪ {−∞},
te ∈ Z ∪ {+∞} and ts ≤ te is the time index set.

A vertex (i, t) ∈ V is at time t and, if ta ≤ t ≤ tb , to be within the time window [ta , tb ]. We say (i, ti ) ∈ V is
before (j, tj ) ∈ V and (j, tj ) ∈ V is after (i, ti ) ∈ V if ti < tj . An edge ((i, ti ), (j, tj )) ∈ E has length or lag
|ti − tj |. Edges of length zero are called contemporaneous, else they are lagged. Second, the directed edges do
not point backwards in time:

Definition 2.2 (Time order). A directed partial mixed graph G = (V, E) with time series structure is time
ordered if ((i, ti ), (j, tj )) ∈ E→ implies ti ≤ tj .

As a consequence (i, ti ) ∈ an((j, tj ), G) requires ti ≤ tj , thus showing that also indirect causal influences are
correctly restricted to not go backwards in time. Third, causal stationarity implies that the edges are repetitive in
the following sense:

Definition 2.3 (Repeating edges). A directed partial mixed graph G = (V, E) with time series struc-
ture has repeating edges if the following holds: If ((i, ti ), (j, tj )) ∈ E• with • ∈ {→, ↔, ◦→, ◦−◦} and
{(i, ti + ∆t), (j, tj + ∆t)} ⊂ V then ((i, ti + ∆t), (j, tj + ∆t)) ∈ E• .

By combining these notions we define the following class of graphical models:

Definition 2.4 (Time series DAG). A time series DAG (ts-DAG) is a DAG D = (V, E) with time series structure
with T = Z that is time ordered and has repeating edges.

Due to the repeating edges property a ts-DAG D is fully specified by its variable index set together with its edges
that involve a vertex at time t. If its longest edge is of finite length pD < ∞, this allows for an unambiguous
specification of D by drawing all vertices within the time window [t − pD , t] and the edges between these. See
Fig. 1 for an example. In slight abuse of notation we sometimes denote vertices by the random variable that they
represent.

The assumption of the structural process in eq. (1) being acyclic says that its causal graph is acyclic. In combi-

This observation has been made before, see e.g. [24] and [20] where ts-DAGs have alternatively been termed time

edges only.
The causal graph of an acyclic structural causal model is a Bayesian network for the entailed observational
distribution, see Theorem 1.4.1 in [18]. This property is often referred to as causal Markov condition [27]
and implies that the structure of the causal graph as captured by d-separation [15] (denoted by the symbol ./)
imprints the corresponding independencies in the distribution [30, 5]. The causal faithfulness condition further
assumes the opposite implication, i.e., that all independencies in the distribution correspond to d-separations in
the causal graph. This results in a one-to-one correspondence between d-separations and independencies. As
far as independencies and qualitative causal relationships are concerned we may thus work with ts-DAGs D as
representatives for structural processes as in (1).


2.4     Time series DMAGs

In most realistic scenarios unobserved common causes cannot be excluded. As discussed in Sec. 1 for the non-
time series setting, DMAGs are a common tool for modeling causal relationships and independencies in this case.
This builds on [22], where a marginalization procedure is defined that to a given DAG D over vertices V asso-
ciates a DMAG MO (D) over the subset of observed vertices O ⊂ V such that, first, ancestral relationships in
D carry over to MO (D) and, second, d-separations in D among vertices in O are in one-to-one correspondence
to the similar notion of m-separation (also denoted by ./) in MO (D). Provided D carries causal meaning as a
causal graph, this ensures that MO (D) carries causal meaning itself and can be used for causal reasoning [33].
We here generalize this construction to the considered time series setting. To this end, we first note that in our
setting there are two types of unobserved variables:

      • Unobservable variables: Some component time series L1 , . . . , LnL with 0 ≤ nL < nV may be unob-
        served altogether by the experimental setup. These are called unobservable, whereas the other component
        time series O1 , . . . , On0 with n0 = nV − nl ≤ ∞ are observable. The variable index set I of the ts-DAG
        D accordingly splits into I = IO ∪ IL . This resembles the case of unobserved variables in the non-time
        series setting.
      • Temporally unobserved variables: In addition, observations are in practice always restricted to a finite
        number of time steps TO ⊂ T = Z. This is specific to the time series setting and means that at times
        T \ TO also the observable time series are unobserved.

The set of observed vertices thus is O = IO × TO . Regarding the set of observed time steps TO , we restrict to
the following two cases:

Here, both τmax ≥ pts and τmax < pts are possible and the repeating edges property allows to fix the latest
time step of TO at an arbitrary reference time t. The reason for restricting to the above two cases is that else the
information about causal stationarity would be lost entirely. In summary, we are led to the following definition:
Definition 2.6 (Time series DMAG). Let D be a ts-DAG with variable index set I, let IO ⊂ I, and let TO ⊂ Z
be regularly sampled or regularly subsampled. The time series DMAG implied by D over variable index set IO
and time index set TO , denoted as MIO ×TO (D) and also referred to as a ts-DMAG, is the DMAG obtained by
marginalizing D over the subset of vertices L = V \ (IO × TO ).

The process of marginalization works in the same way as defined in [22] for the non-time series setting, such that
ts-DMAGs indeed are DMAGs as presupposed by the definition. Figure 2 illustrates the concept of ts-DMAGs
for both sampling schemes.
Time series DMAGs are the central objects of interest in this paper, with an essential part of the work spent on
deriving their properties. We will see that the repeating edges property of ts-DAGs D plays an essential role in
this regard. As a first step towards this objective, we here note which of the defining properties of ts-DAGs carry
over to ts-DMAGs:
Proposition 2.7. Let MIO ×TO (D) be the time series DMAG implied by a ts-DAG D over IO × TO . Then:
   1. MIO ×TO (D) has a time series structure.
   2. MIO ×TO (D) is time ordered.
   3. There are cases in which MIO ×TO (D) does not have repeating edges.

While according to part 1. of this statement every ts-DMAG is a DMAG with time series structure, part 2. shows
that the reverse is not true. In other words: ts-DMAGs are a proper subclass of DMAGs with time series structure.

2.5   ts-DMAGs are a generalization of DMAGs

Consider an arbitrary DMAG Mnt with vertex set Ont . As proven in [22] there is DAG Dnt over some vertex
set Vnt ⊃ Ont such that Mnt = MOnt (Dnt ). Now define D as the ts-DAG that consists of disconnected copies
of Dnt at every time step s ∈ Z, i.e., there are no lagged edges in D and for all s ∈ Z its induced subgraph on
Vnt × {s} is Dnt . Then the ts-DMAG MVnt ×{t} equals Mnt .
This identifies ts-DMAGs as a proper generalization of DMAGs and thereby shows that all statements about
ts-DMAGs also to apply to DMAGs as a special case.


2.6   Future vertices are not relevant for determining ts-DMAGs

In order to determine MIO ×TO (D) all time steps after t are irrelevant:
Lemma 2.9. Let D be a ts-DAG and denote with D(−∞,t] its induced subgraph on its set of vertices before or at
t. Then MIO ×TO (D) = MIO ×TO (D(−∞,t] ).

This result, which follows from time order and d-separation, has the intuitive interpretation that the future need
not be known in order to reason about the past and present.

3     Characterization of ts-DMAGs
As stated in Sec. 2.5, every DMAG can be obtained by marginalizing some DAG. The same is not true in the time
series setting, i.e., not every DMAG with time series structure is a ts-DMAG MIO ×TO (D). While this already
follows because ts-DMAGs are time ordered, we here derive several additional constraints which ts-DMAGs
necessarily obey. To the best of our knowledge not all of these have been considered in the literature. What
is more, the question of characterizing the space of ts-DMAGs has not yet been addressed. Namely: Which
additional constraints on a DMAG M with time series structure are sufficient for there to be a ts-DAG D with
M = MIO ×TO (D)? We answer this question by providing a single constraint that is both necessary and
sufficient.

different at first sight, these two cases are in fact equivalent from a graphical point of view:

3.2   Properties of ts-DMAGs

We here discuss several constraints that ts-DMAGs Mτmax (D) necessarily obey. These are such that a certain
graphical property persists when the involved vertices are collectively shifted in time. The according definitions
are:
Definition 3.2 (Time shift persistent graphical properties). A graph G = (V, E) with time series structure (in
part 2. a directed partial mixed graph, in parts 3.-4. a DMAG) has
   1. repeating adjacencies if the following holds: If ((i, ti ), (j, tj )) ∈ E and {(i, ti + ∆t), (j, tj + ∆t)} ⊂ V
      then ((i, ti + ∆t), (j, tj + ∆t)) ∈ E.
   2. repeating orientations if the following holds: If ((i, ti ), (j, tj )) ∈ E• with • ∈ {→, ↔, ◦→, ◦−◦} and
      ((i, ti + ∆t), (j, tj + ∆t)) ∈ E then ((i, ti + ∆t), (j, tj + ∆t)) ∈ E• .
   3. repeating ancestral relationships if the following holds: If (i, ti ) ∈ an((j, tj ), G) and {(i, ti + ∆t), (j, tj +
      ∆t)} ⊂ V then (i, ti + ∆t) ∈ an((j, tj + ∆t), G).
   4. repeating separating sets if the following holds: If (i, ti ) ./ (j, tj ) | S and {(i, ti + ∆t), (j, tj + ∆t)} ∪
      S∆t ⊂ V, where S∆t is obtained by shifting every vertex in S by ∆t time steps, then (i, ti + ∆t) ./
      (j, tj + ∆t) | S∆t .
   5. past-repeating adjacencies if the following holds: If ((i, ti ), (j, tj )) ∈ E and {(i, ti + ∆t), (j, tj + ∆t)} ⊂
      V with ∆t < 0 then ((i, ti + ∆t), (j, tj + ∆t)) ∈ E.

3.5    Canonical ts-DAGs

Returning to the goal of characterizing ts-DMAGs, let us recall some related facts from the non-time series
setting:

Definition 3.13 (Canonical ts-DAG. From Sec. 6.1 of [22], specialized to the case of directed ancestral graphs).
Let G = (V, E) be a directed ancestral graph. The canonical DAG Dc (G) of G is the graph defined as follows:
   1. Its vertex set is Vc = V ∪ L with L = {lij | (i, j) ∈ E↔ }.
   2. Its edges are
          • i→j for all (i, j) ∈ E→ ,
          • lij →i for all lij ∈ L,
          • lij →j for all lij ∈ L.

Proposition 3.14 (Theorem 6.4 in [22]). If M is a DMAG over vertex set O then M = MO (Dc (M)).

Intuitively, Dc (G) is obtained from G by replacing each bidirected edge i↔j with i←lij →j where lij is an addi-
tionally inserted vertex. Since Dc (M) is a DAG, this shows that every DMAG can be obtained by marginalizing
some DAG.
Does this generalize to the time series setting such that for every DMAG M with time series structure there
is some “canonical” ts-DAG Dc (M) which upon appropriate marginalizing equals M? As mentioned above,
the answer is No. Already in the generalization of canonical DAGs a complication arises: Consider an edge
(i, ti )∗−∗(j, tj ) in M that is not in stat(M). If, depending on the orientation of this edge, (i, ti )→(j, tj ) or
(i, ti )←(j, tj ) or (i, ti )←(lij , tij )→(j, tj ), where (lij , tij ) is an additionally inserted vertex, is included in a
ts-DAG Dc (M), the repeating edges property of ts-DAGs requires the same structure to be present at all other
time steps too. Upon marginalizing such Dc (M) there thus will be an edge (i, ti + ∆t)∗−∗(j, tj + ∆t) of the
same type for all ∆t with t − τmax ≤ ti + ∆t, tj + ∆t ≤ t. This graph cannot agree with M precisely because
(i, ti )∗−∗(j, tj ) is not in stat(M).
Given these considerations, the following appears to be a natural generalization of canonical DAGs to the time
series setting:

   1. Its set of vertices is Vc = (I ∪ J) × Z, where I ∪ J is the variable index set and Z is the time index set,

3.6   A necessary and sufficient condition that characterizes ts-DMAGs

4    Markov equivalence classes of ts-DMAGs and causal discovery
In this section we introduce ts-DPAGs as graphs that represent Markov equivalence classes of ts-DMAGs. These
can be understood as refinements of DPAGs obtained by incorporating the background knowledge of the data


                                                        15
generating process being as in eq. (1) and the observed time steps being regularly sampled or regularly subsam-
pled as defined in Sec. 2.4. We further introduce several alternative refinements of DPAGs, concretely DPAGs
that represent Markov equivalence classes of stationarified ts-DMAGs as well as DPAGs that incorporate only
some of the necessary properties of ts-DMAGs as background knowledge. As we show, all of these in general
carry less information about the underlying ts-DAG than ts-DPAGs do. Using this terminology we discuss and
compare three algorithms for independence-based causal discovery in time series with latent confounders and
show that none of them learns ts-DPAGs, i.e., none of them is complete in the considered time series setting.
This discussion also clarifies a related conceptual misunderstanding in the literature.


4.1     Background knowledge and DPAGs

Markov equivalent DMAGs by definition have the same m-separations and hence cannot be told apart by in-
dependencies in their associated probability distributions. They might, however, be discerned if additional as-
sumptions are made. One type of such assumptions is background knowledge, i.e., the assertion that DMAGs
with certain properties can be excluded because these are in conflict with a priori knowledge about the system of
study. This allows to refine the notion of DPAGs as follows:
Definition 4.1 (DPAGs with background knowledge). Let M be a DMAG, let [M] its Markov equivalence class,
and let [M]A ⊂ [M] the set of all DMAGs in [M] that are consistent with background knowledge A. Then:
  1. A directed partial mixed graph P is a DPAG for M if
         • P has the same skeleton (i.e., the same set of adjacencies) as M and
         • every non-circle mark in P is also in M.
  2. A DPAG for M is called maximally informative (m.i.) with respect to [M]0 ⊂ [M] if
         • every non-circle mark in P is in every element of [M]0 and
         • for every circle mark in P there are M1 , M2 ∈ [M]0 such that in M1 there is a tail mark and in
            M2 ∈ [M]0 there is a head mark instead of the circle mark.
  3. The maximally informative (m.i.) DPAG with respect to A, denoted as P(M, A), is the m.i. DPAG of M
      with respect to [M]A .
   4. The conventional m.i. DPAG for M is the m.i. DPAG P(M) = P(M, ∅) with respect to the empty
      background knowledge.

This slightly deviates from usual definitions of DPAGs, see the below remark, and combines Definition 2 in
[10] (regarding the general definition of DPAGs) with the definition of PAGs in [2] (regarding the refinement by
background knowledge). There is no need of modification for DMAGs with time series structure, this just carries
over to the DPAG.
Definition 4.2 (Stronger/weaker background knowledge, more/less informative DPAG). Let A1 and A2 be back-
ground knowleges, and let P1 and P2 be DPAGs for M.
      • We say A1 is stronger than A2 and A2 is weaker than A1 if every DMAG consistent with A1 is also
        consistent with A2 .
      • We say P1 is more informative than P2 and P2 is less informative than P1 if every circle mark in P1 is
        also in P2

This implies that P(M, A1 ) is more informative than P(M, A2 ) if A1 is stronger than A2 . It is therefore
desirable to use as much background knowledge as available because this may lead to more unambiguous edge
orientations and hence more causal inferences about the underlying data generating process. By construction
P(M, A) is the most informative DPAG for M that can be learned from independencies in the observational
distribution in combination with the background knowledge A.
Remark (on Def. 4.1). In the literature there are two common definitions of DPAGs that slightly deviate from
each other and our above definition: First, some works call P a DPAG for M if and only if it is, in our terminol-
ogy, a DPAG for M that is at most as informative as the conventional m.i. DPAG for M. Second, other works
call P a DPAG for M if and only if it is, in our terminology, the conventional m.i. DPAG for M. Our definition
has the advantage that P(M, A) is a DPAG for M even if A distinguishes Markov equivalent DMAGs.

6     Discussion
In this paper we developed and analyzed a class of causal graphical models for causally stationary multivariate
time series that may involve unobserved component time series. Concretely, we introduced ts-DMAGs as graph-
ical representation of the time-lag specific causal relationships and independencies among regularly sampled
or regularly subsampled observed time steps of the observed component time series. These graphs constitute
a strictly smaller class of graphical models than standard DMAGs with one vertex per observed time steps and
component time series, the latter being formalized by the notion of time series structure. As a central result of this
work, Theorems 1 and 2 completely characterize the space of ts-DMAGs. Examples demonstrated that the con-
straint imposed by this characterization is strictly stronger than all other constraints in the literature that we are
aware of and that, without having imposed additional assumptions, it allows for stronger causal inference about
the underlying data-generating process. Further, we defined ts-DPAGs as representations of Markov equivalence
classes of ts-DMAGs. These graphs contain as much information about the causal ancestral relationships as can
in principle be learned from observational data under the standard assumptions of independence-based causal
discovery. Based on the characterization of ts-DMAGs together with Theorem 3 we then showed that current
time series causal discovery algorithms do not learn ts-DPAGs, i.e., they are not complete in the considered time
series setting. With Algorithm 1 we explained how ts-DPAGs can in principle be learned. Lastly, we compared
ts-DMAGs and ts-DPAGs on different time windows and analyzed the information gained by observing more
time steps.
The results presented here point to various directions of future research. First, it would be valuable to consider the
causal discovery problem in more detail. In particular, it is desirable to develop orientation rules that impose the
background knowledge of an underlying ts-DAG without the need for listing consistent DMAGs (as Algorithm 1

does). Second, it remains open to characterize the causal inferences that can be drawn from ts-DMAGs and

A      Graphical concepts and notation
We here summarize common graphical concepts that are used without explanation in the main text. Our notation
and terminology is mixture of those used in [11, 19, 10].
A graph G = (V, E) consists of a set of vertices V together with a set of edges E ⊂ V × V. The vertices
i, j ∈ V are adjacent if (i, j) ∈ E or (j, i) ∈ E. We then say that there is an edge between i and j (j and i), that
i and j (j and i) are connected by an edge, and that i (j) is an adjacency of j (i).
Throughout this paper we only consider directed partial mixed graphs. These are graphs in which, first, there
is at most one edge between any unordered pair of vertices and, second, no vertex is adjacent to itself and, third,
there are at most four types of edges: directed edges (→), bidirected edges (↔), partially directed edges (◦→),
and non-directed edges (◦−◦). The last point is formalized by a decomposition of E as E = E→ ∪ E↔ ∪ E◦→ ∪
E◦−◦ that specifies the edge types (also called edge orientations) and which is considered part of the specification
of a concrete graph. A directed mixed graph is a partial mixed graph without partially directed and non-directed
edges, and a directed graph is a directed mixed graph without bidirected edges. The skeleton of a graph is the
object obtained when disregarding the information about the decomposition of E into E→ ∪ E↔ ∪ E◦→ ∪ E◦−◦ .
For directed partial mixed graphs G = (V, E) and G 0 = (V0 , E0 ) we say that G 0 is a subgraph of G and that G
is a supergraph of G, denoted as G 0 ⊂ G or G ⊃ G 0 , if V0 ⊂ V and (i, j) ∈ E0• with • ∈ {→, ↔, ◦→, ◦−◦}
implies (i, j) ∈ E• . By convention we let a set be its own subset and superset, hence a graph is its own subgraph
and supergraph. For a directed partial mixed graphs G = (V, E) its induced subgraph on V0 ⊂ V is the graph
(V0 , E0 ) such that (i, j) ∈ E0• with • ∈ {→, ↔, ◦→, ◦−◦} if and only if i, j ∈ V0 and (i, j) ∈ E• .
A directed edge (i, j) ∈ E→ is denoted as i→j or j←i and we say i→j (j←i) is in G if (i, j) ∈ E→ . Similarly
for the other edge types. Edges are interpreted as composite objects of the symbols at their ends, which are
referred to as edge marks and can be tails, heads, or circles. For example, i◦→j has a circle-mark at i and a head
mark at j, and i→j has a tail mark at i. Tails and heads are non-circle marks, these are unambiguous orientations.
Circle marks are ambiguous orientations. The symbol ‘∗’ is a wildcard for all three marks. For example, ∗→ may
be →, ↔, or ◦→.
                                                               .
A walk in a graph G is an ordered sequence π = (i1 , i2 , . . , in ) of vertices such that ik and ik+1 are adjacent
in G for all k = 1, . . . , n − 1. The integer n ≥ 1 is the length of π and a vertex in this sequence is said to be
on π. A path is a walk on which every vertex occurs at most once. For a path π = (i1 , i2 , . . . , in ) the vertices
i1 and in are the end-point vertices of π, all other vertices on π are the non end-point vertices of π. We refer to
π as a path between i1 and in (in and i1 ) and graphically represent it by i1 ∗−∗i2 ∗−∗ . . . ∗−∗in where ik ∗−∗ik+1
is the unique edge between ik and ik+1 . Such a graphical representation can also specify a path. We say that
π is out of i1 if i1 →i2 in G and that π is into i1 if i1 ←∗i2 in G. Similarly for the other end-point vertex. For
1 ≤ a < b ≤ n we write π(ia , ib ) for the path (ia , ia+1 , . . . ib ) and π(ib , ia ) for the path (ib , ib−1 , . . . ia ). Both
of these are referred to as subpaths of π. Given paths π1 = (i1 , i2 , . . . , in ) and π2 = (j1 , j2 , . . . , jm ) that have


                                                               24
only the vertex in = j1 in common we write π1 ⊕ π2 for the path (i1 , i2 , . . . , in , j2 , . . . , jm ). A vertex ik on path
π is a collider on π if it is a non end-point vertex of π and π(ik−1 , ik+1 ) is ik−1 ∗→ik ←∗ik+1 , otherwise it is a
non-collider on π. If the vertices i and k are non-adjacent we refer to a path i∗−∗j∗−∗k as unshielded triple and
to a path i∗→j←∗k as unshielded collider. A path of length n = 1 is called trivial. The path π = (i1 , i2 , . . . , in )
is a directed path if ik →ik+1 in G for all 1 ≤ k ≤ n − 1 or ik ←ik+1 in G for all 1 ≤ k ≤ n − 1. In the former
case we speak of a directed path from i1 to in , in the latter case of a directed path from in to i1 .
If i→j in G, i is a parent of j and j is a child of i. The vertex i is an ancestor of j and j is a descendant of i if
i = j or if there is a directed path from i to j. The set of parents and ancestors of a vertex i in G are respectively
denoted as pa(i, G) and an(i, G). We say vertex i is an ancestor of a set S of vertices and S is a descendant of
i if one element of S is a descendant of i. Similary, vertex i is a descendant of a set S of vertices and S is an
ancestor of i if one element of S is an ancestor of i.
A partial mixed graph G has a directed cycle if there are distinct vertices i and j such that i ∈ an(j, G) and
j ∈ an(i, G). A directed acyclic graph (DAG) D is a directed graph without directed cycles. A partial mixed
graph G has an almost directed cycle if i↔j in G and i ∈ an(j, G). A directed ancestral graph is a directed
mixed graph without directed cycles and almost directed cycles. An inducing path between i and j is a path π
between i and j such that all non end-point vertices of π are collider on π and ancestors of i or j. A directed
maximal ancestral graph (DMAG) M is a directed ancestral graph that has no inducing paths of length n ≥ 3.
Every DAG is a DMAG.

Definition B.29 (Canonical paths). Let π be a path in D between distinct observable vertices (i, ti ) and (j, tj )
with t − τmax ≤ ti , tj ≤ τmax . A path πc between (i, ti ) and (j, tj ) in Dc (Mτmax (D)) is canonical with respect
to π if all of the following holds:

   1. All observable vertices on πc are also on π.

   2. If (k1 , t1 ), (k2 , t2 ) and (k3 , t3 ) are distinct observable vertices on πc and (k2 , t2 ) is on
      πc ((k1 , t1 ), (k3 , t3 )), then (k2 , t2 ) is on π((k1 , t1 ), (k3 , t3 )).

   3. πc is collider-free.

   4. If (k1 , t1 ) and (k2 , t2 ) are distinct observable vertices on πc , then π((k1 , t1 ), (k2 , t2 )) is an inducing path
      relative to O(max(t1 , t2 ) − τmax , t)[πc ((k1 , t1 ), (k2 , t2 ))].

Remark (on Def. B.29). First, according to Prop. B.23 the canonically induced path πci of a collider-free path
π between distinct observable vertices (i, ti ) and (j, tj ) with t − τmax ≤ ti , tj ≤ τmax is canonical with respect
to π. Second, while the first property is implied by the second and would thus not be needed, we have included it
in the definition for clarity.

Lemma B.30. Given the assumptions and notation of Def. B.27, let 0 ≤ a1 < a2 < a3 ≤ nc + 1. Assume
that π̃ca1 ,a2 is canonical with respect to π̃ a1 ,a2 and active given S \ {da1 , da2 }, and that π̃ca2 ,a3 is canonical with
respect to π̃ a2 ,a3 and active given S \ {da2 , da3 }. Then: If at least one of π̃ca1 ,a2 and π̃ca2 ,a3 is out of da2 , then
there is a path π̃ca1 ,a3 that is canonical with respect to π̃ a1 ,a3 and active given S \ {da1 , da3 }.

Proof of Lemma B.30. We here assume that π̃ca2 ,a3 is out of da2 , the case in which π̃ca2 ,a3 is into da2 and π̃ca1 ,a2
out of da2 can be proven equivalently. To simplify notation we write t(v) for the time step of a vertex v, i.e.,
v = (·, t(v)). The proof is divided into 14 steps.


                                                             40
Step 1: No vertex on π̃ a1 ,a2 or π̃ a2 ,a3 is after t
If there would be a vertex on π̃ a1 ,a2 (on π̃ a2 ,a3 ) that is after t, then by time order of D and because both da1
and da2 (both da2 and da3 ) are not after t this path would have a collider after t. Again using time order this
collider could not be unblocked by S \ {da1 , da2 } (by S \ {da2 , da3 }), because by definition of S all vertices in
S are not after t. This contradicts the assumption that π̃ a1 ,a2 (on π̃ a2 ,a3 ) is active given S \ {da1 , da2 } (given
S \ {da2 , da3 }).
Step 2: ca2 and da2 are in D ancestors of da3
Because π̃ca2 ,a3 is out of da2 and, by means of being canonical with respect to π̃ a2 ,a3 , collider-free, it is directed
from da2 to da3 . Hence, all its vertices are ancestors of da3 and descendants of da2 in Dc (Mτmax (D)) and thus,
by part 2. of Prop. B.16, also in D. Because ca2 is an ancestor of da2 in D by means of ρa2 , this implies that ca2
is in D an ancestor of every vertex on π̃ca2 ,a3 .
Step 3: Definition and properties of ga2 ,a3 and properties of π̃ a2 ,a3 (ca2 , ga2 ,a3 )
Let ga2 ,a3 be the vertex next to da2 on π̃ca2 ,a3 (this may be da3 ). Since π̃ca2 ,a3 is directed from da2 to da3 , π̃ca2 ,a3
is into ga2 ,a3 . Because in Dc (Mτmax (D)) there are no edges into unobservable vertices, this shows that ga2 ,a3 is
observable. Moreover, using time order of Dc (Mτmax (D)) and that π̃ca2 ,a3 is directed from da2 to da3 , ga2 ,a3 is
not before da2 and not after da3 . Hence, ga2 ,a3 is within the observed time window [t − τmax , t].
Since π̃ca2 ,a3 is canonical with respect to π̃ a2 ,a3 , ga2 ,a3 is on π̃ a2 ,a3 = ρa2 (da2 , ca2 )⊕π a2 ,a3 ⊕ρa3 . If ga2 ,a3 were
on ρa2 , then ga2 ,a3 would in D be an ancestor of da2 by means of ρa2 (ga2 ,a3 , da2 ). This would violate acyclicity
of D because ga2 ,a3 is a descendant of da2 according to step 2. Hence, ga2 ,a3 is on π̃ a2 ,a3 (ca2 , da3 ) = π a2 ,a3 ⊕
ρa3 excluding ca2 and π̃ a2 ,a3 (ca2 , ga2 ,a3 ) is a non-trivial subpath of π̃ a2 ,a3 (ca2 , da3 ) and of π̃ a2 ,a3 (da2 , ga2 ,a3 ).
Lastly, π̃ a2 ,a3 (ca2 , ga2 ,a3 ) is into ca2 because π is into ca2 .
Since π̃ca2 ,a3 is canonical with respect to π̃ a2 ,a3 and t(da2 ) ≤ t(ga1 ,a2 ) by time order, π̃ a2 ,a3 (da2 , ga2 ,a3 ) is
an inducing path relative to O(t(ga2 ,a3 ) − τmax , t)[π̃ca2 ,a3 (da2 , ga2 ,a3 )]. Here, the simplification O(t(ga2 ,a3 ) −
τmax , t)[π̃ca2 ,a3 (da2 , ga2 ,a3 )] = O(t(ga2 ,a3 ) − τmax , t) applies because π̃ca2 ,a3 (da2 , ga2 ,a3 ) by definition of ga2 ,a3
consists of its end point vertices da2 and ga2 ,a3 only. The path π̃ a2 ,a3 (da2 , ga2 ,a3 ) thus has two properties:


   1. First, if v is an observable non end-point non-collider on π̃ a2 ,a3 (da2 , ga2 ,a3 ) then t(v) < t(ga2 ,a3 ) − τmax
      or t(v) > t. Because t(v) > t is excluded by step 1, in fact t(v) < t(ga2 ,a3 ) − τmax .


   2. Second, if v is a collider on π̃ a2 ,a3 (da2 , ga2 ,a3 ) then in D it is an ancestor of da2 or ga2 ,a3 . Because da2 is
      in D an ancestor of ga2 ,a3 , v is, in fact, an ancestor of ga2 ,a3 .


Both of these statements are also true for π̃ a2 ,a3 (ca2 , ga2 ,a3 ) because it is a subpath of π̃ a2 ,a3 (da2 , ga2 ,a3 ).
Step 4: All observable vertices on ρa2 other than da2 are before t(ga2 ,a3 ) − τmax
Let v 6= da2 be an observable vertex on ρa2 . Since ga2 ,a3 is not on ρa2 , see step 2, v is then a non end-
point vertex on π̃ a2 ,a3 (da2 , ga2 ,a3 ). Moreover, since ρa2 is directed from ca2 to da2 , v is a non-collider on
π̃ a2 ,a3 (da2 , ga2 ,a3 ). Since π̃ a2 ,a3 (da2 , ga2 ,a3 ) is an inducing path relative to O(t(ga2 ,a3 ) − τmax , t), see step 3,
this shows that t(v) < t(ga2 ,a3 ) − τmax or after t(v) > t. The case t(v) > t is excluded by step 1.
Step 5: Definition and properties of ha1 ,a2 and properties of π̃ a1 ,a2 (ha1 ,a2 , ca2 )
Let ha1 ,a2 be the observable vertex on π̃ca1 ,a2 closest to da2 other than da2 itself that is not more than τmax time
steps before ga2 ,a3 , i.e., for which t(ga1 ,a2 ) − τmax ≤ t(ha1 ,a2 ) (this may be da1 ).
Since π̃ca1 ,a2 is canonical with respect to π̃ a1 ,a2 , ha1 ,a2 is on π̃ a1 ,a2 = ρa1 (da1 , ca1 ) ⊕ π a1 ,a2 ⊕ ρa2 . Due to
step 4 and t(ga1 ,a2 ) − τmax ≤ t(ha1 ,a2 ) it cannot be on ρa2 unless it is da2 , which is, however, excluded by
definition. Hence, ha1 ,a2 is on π̃ a1 ,a2 (da1 , ca2 ) = ρa1 (da1 , ca1 ) ⊕ π a1 ,a2 excluding ca2 and π̃ a1 ,a2 (ha1 ,a2 , ca2 )
is a non-trivial subpath of π̃ a1 ,a2 (da1 , ca2 ) and of π̃ a2 ,a3 (ha1 ,a2 , da2 ). Lastly, π̃ a1 ,a2 (ha1 ,a2 , ca2 ) is into ca2
because π a1 ,a2 is into ca2 .
Write thd = max(t(ha1 ,a2 ), t(da2 )) and thg = max(t(ha1 ,a2 ), t(ga2 ,a3 )). Since π̃ca1 ,a2 is canonical with respect
to π̃ a1 ,a2 , π̃ a1 ,a2 (ha1 ,a2 , da2 ) is an inducing path relative to O(thd − τmax , t)[π̃ca1 ,a2 (ha1 ,a2 , da2 )]. In particular,
it has two properties:

Step 6: ga2 ,a3 and ha1 ,a2 are adjacent or almost adjacent in Dc (Mτmax (D))

π̃ a2 ,a3 (ca2 , ga2 ,a3 ) are into ca2 , see steps 5 and 3. We now show that ψ is an inducing path relative to O(thg −

Collider: According to steps 3 and 5 every collider on π̃ a1 ,a2 (ha1 ,a2 , ca2 ) and on π̃ a2 ,a3 (ca2 , ga2 ,a3 ) is in D an
ancestor of ha1 ,a2 or ga2 ,a3 . The junction point ca2 is in D an ancestor of ga2 ,a3 according to step 1.
Non end-point non-collider: Let v be a non end-point non-collider on ψ. Since ca2 is a collider on ψ, v is then
