10.1      One decision clock for the causal and statistical models
A household’s liquidity changes after an offer, its updated balance influences the next offer,
and both affect later funding contribution. Collections has the same feedback: assistance
changes arrears, arrears affect subsequent assistance, and both determine repayment. The
model must preserve which information was available before each action.

updated measurements and eligibility, with terminal outcome Y contained in LT . Behavior
chooses an action with probability et (a | Ht ). A target policy gt (a | Ht ) can be stochastic,
or deterministic with all mass at dt (Ht ). It must be measurable with respect to the stated
decision information and assign only feasible actions. Define

variants replace ignorability with other restrictions; their identified blip parameters need not
identify arbitrary marginal regime means. An MSM, an SNMM and a predictive state-space
model are different models of different objects, even when all use linear functions.

10.7.1      Recovering the observed-history g-formula

If qη (a | s, h) = et (a | h) and the structural innovations justify sequential exchangeability,
actions supply no extra information about St conditional on Ht . Summing Equation (10.14)
over states defines observable history kernels for (Rt , Ot+1 ). Under common supported histories,
their conditional state mixtures are invariant to replacing the observed-history policy, because
the action factors cancel from the latent posterior. Hence replacing et by gt yields the history
g-formula.
   This establishes the bridge: a controlled state-space model is a restricted parameterization
of longitudinal observation kernels when its action and observation assumptions permit that
reduction. It can be computationally valuable when histories are long. It is not a weaker
replacement for the causal assumptions underlying those kernels. When St is observed and
Markov sufficient, Qt (Ht , a) reduces to Qt (St , a); under partial observation it can reduce to a
belief state under further restrictions.

bounded fixed point. The belief-state sufficiency construction is developed in Kaelbling et al.
(1998, Sections 3.2–3.4); it assumes a supplied model.
   Three distinct identification questions precede causal use of the value. First, can the
observed law identify the likelihood parameters within the proposed state-space class? For
an uncontrolled stationary K-state HMM, full-rank ergodic transition and distinct emission
distributions identify parameters up to labels from a sufficiently long observation block under
the cited conditions (Alexandrovich et al., 2016, Theorem 1). This theorem is not automatically
a controlled, privately logged HMM theorem.
   Second, does the historical model identify the intervention law after changing q? Causal
autonomy, action support and restrictions on hidden assignment and measurement are ad-
ditional. Third, is the requested policy value uniquely determined even if some parameters
are not? Formally, for every two admissible models with the same observed law, one needs
VM1 (g) = VM2 (g), which is weaker than equality of every latent parameter but stronger than
good fit.
  A permutation of hidden-state labels leaves both observation likelihood and coherently
computed policy values unchanged; it is a representational ambiguity. The two worlds in

Chapter 6 instead have the same likelihood and different action values; that is causal ambiguity.
A bootstrap conditional on one selected model measures sampling variation within it and does

10.10      What a state-space adapter must preserve
A numerical filter consumes transition and observation kernels, initial state, parameter values
and masks. A causal adapter additionally needs the action clock, logger information, inter-
vention mechanism, reward definition and source of identification. It must specify whether
a missing measurement is exogenous, conditionally ignorable, or modeled through a state-
dependent observation process. Omitting an endogenous mask changes the likelihood and may
change policy identification.
   For continuous states, sums become integrals and Kalman, nonlinear Gaussian, particle or
variational methods approximate different objects. The state-space adapter should report the
actual likelihood approximation, not only the latent-state dimension. Label fixing, deterministic
lag coordinates, parameter priors and reward integration must be coherent across fitting and
intervention evaluation. This is how a numerical filtering library can be reused without
attributing causal guarantees to its numerical accuracy.
  The customer and the bank may also be different decision makers. The bank’s POMDP
maximizes a supplied bank reward. A model of customer activation, refinancing or default
requires utility, beliefs, information and transition restrictions of its own. Estimating a
customer’s dynamic choice likelihood is not equivalent to optimizing the bank’s reward;
equilibrium response adds another layer. Chapter 12 develops that inverse decision problem;
Chapters 17 and 18 connect structural shocks and feedback to it.

The longitudinal gradient becomes a practical alternative to a POMDP fit when the recorded
state is a sufficient observed history summary. Consider the three-state servicing process of
Section 88.3, with independent customer trajectories and eight decisions per customer. The
state records repayment, arrears or severe distress; repayment is absorbing and has only the

difference is the target, so the covariance between their estimated values must be retained.

tables by conditional counts and estimate the behavior probabilities in each eligible state. The
half-count smoothing of sparse cells is fixed in advance. Compute initial Qt , Vt by backward
recursion. On the evaluation fold form the cumulative policy ratios and the discounted-
reward analogue of Equation (10.7). Subtract the two policies’ returns within each customer,
concatenate the folds and use the sample variance of these contrasts divided by the total
number of customers. Treating each customer-date row as independent would discard precisely
the covariance that this score represents.

overlap on eligible states, table estimates have root-N rates. The second-order longitudinal
remainder is then Op (N −1 ). Cross-fold evaluation and finite-dimensional consistency make the
fitted score difference negligible at root-N scale. The iid trajectory CLT therefore supplies the
stated variance for fixed policies. Deterministic actions in the absorbing state are compatible
with this argument because both evaluation policies take that same action there. The argument
does not cover growing histories or unobserved manager information.

11.6.2     Selection, censoring and the fitted comparison

An independent randomized pilot of 2,500 synthetic customers learns a four-coefficient
immediate-reward contrast. The resulting stochastic policy uses action probability .7 where
that fitted contrast exceeds .5 and .2 elsewhere; the reference policy always uses .2. This is
a fixed learned candidate after the pilot, not a proof of dynamic optimality. Conditional on
the pilot, the target is its actual policy value. An unconditional claim about a policy-learning
algorithm would require a different sampling experiment that repeats the pilot as well. Regret
relative to an optimal policy is another estimand.
   Evaluation uses 2,400 fresh customers, four decisions and discount .97. Histories include
a baseline covariate, continuous current and lagged risk, previous action and cumulative
action count. These summaries are sufficient by the declared generator; no generic learned-
state sufficiency is assumed. The action and observation probabilities are fitted with logistic
regressions; arm-specific histogram boosting estimates continuation rewards backward. Two
whole-trajectory folds keep each evaluated customer’s nuisance fits outside that fold. A separate
million-trajectory intervention simulation gives contrast 3.5813 with Monte Carlo standard
error .0040, much smaller than the evaluation interval standard error of about .295.
   Across 120 repetitions under coarsening at random, the full score has bias −.0012, RMSE
.2873 and 95.8% coverage at nominal 95%. Ignoring censoring gives bias −.0199 and 83.3%
coverage: the continuation model is useful, yet its score variance is no longer an adequate
inferential construction. Direct recursion has mean bias −.0376; it is a point challenger without
a claimed interval. In the adverse generator, observation also depends on the otherwise unseen
reward innovation. The full fitted score now has bias −.1434 and 89.2% coverage; ignoring
censoring covers 57.5%. This is an observed failure of the identifying censoring condition, not
proof that a different learner will repair MNAR data. At 120 repetitions the Monte Carlo
standard error of coverage near 95% is about .020.
   Irregular opportunities require a further distinction. One may model the timestamps and
elapsed time as part of each observed history and retain the natural visit clock; changing
when visits occur is a different intervention. The event-intensity likelihood and weights in
Chapter 18 provide that second route. Interpolating missing months and treating them as
observed decisions supplies neither exchangeability nor the visit likelihood. Thus the proposed
servicing workflow first selects among observed-history adjustment, an explicit controlled
measurement model, and a proxy bridge. Their identifying information differs even when all
three output a policy value in the same currency.
