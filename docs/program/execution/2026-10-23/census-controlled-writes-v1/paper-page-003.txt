LLM-to-symbolic solver pipelines. The natural way to combine a language model with a symbolic
executor is to let the model translate and the solver execute. Logic-LM (Pan et al., 2023), LINC
(Olausson et al., 2023), and SatLM (Ye et al., 2023) established this pattern for first-order logic,
SAT, and constraint solving, and LeanReasoner (Jiang et al., 2024) and CLOVER (Ryu et al., 2025)
carry it into proof assistants and verification-backed domains. Once translation worked often enough
to be useful, the bottleneck moved to its failures. One-shot formalization is brittle, so DetermLR
(Sun et al., 2024), LINA (Li et al., 2024a), and HBLR (Li et al., 2025) wrap it in validate-and-repair
loops, and SymbCoT (Xu et al., 2024), Aristotle (Xu et al., 2025), and CaRing (Yang et al., 2025)
keep the reasoning trajectory itself in symbolic form so that errors can be caught mid-chain. Across
these refinements, one thing did not change. Once a formal problem is written down, execution is
trusted, and whether the written premises are supported by the source is never asked. CPUN E S Y
asks exactly that question, checking source support before a proposed predicate may become a
premise for execution.
Neuro-symbolic engines, provenance, and structured generation. A second body of work brings
symbolic structure into learning rather than serving. DeepProbLog (Manhaeve et al., 2018), Logic
Tensor Networks (Badreddine et al., 2022), NeurASP (Yang et al., 2020), Scallop (Li et al., 2023),
LNN (Riegel et al., 2020), and SATNet (Wang et al., 2019) each make a different fragment of
logic differentiable or probabilistic, so that gradients can flow through constraints during training.
Alongside them, constrained decoding made formal output syntactically reliable, first for SQL with
PICARD (Scholak et al., 2021) and then as general infrastructure with XGrammar (Dong et al.,
2025), and Datalog provenance (Bourgaux et al., 2022) tracks which base facts support each derived
fact.
Neural theorem proving. Theorem proving followed a similar arc. GPT-f (Polu & Sutskever, 2020)
showed that a language model can predict useful proof steps, retrieval made premise selection prac-
tical at library scale through Magnushammer (Mikuła et al., 2024) and LeanDojo’s ReProver (Yang
et al., 2023), and COPRA (Thakur et al., 2024) turned proving into an agentic search over tactic
applications. Recent work also verifies intermediate natural-language claims (Liu et al., 2025). Our
premise pools borrow the premise-retrieval setting but change the object being retrieved, since the
pool is built from the source kernel and its dependency closure rather than a raw library. Section 4.1
compares them with ReProver and BM25 directly.
Selective prediction, verification, and benchmarking. Knowing when not to answer has its own
history. Selective prediction lets a model abstain below a confidence threshold (Geifman & El-Yaniv,
2017), and post-hoc verification scores or repairs answers after generation (Cobbe et al., 2021; Liu
et al., 2025). A growing set of benchmarks, LogicBench (Parmar et al., 2024), ProverGen/ProverQA
(Qi et al., 2025), RuleArena (Zhou et al., 2025), SLR (Helff et al., 2026), LogicGraph (Wu et al.,
2026), MetaPCR (Galitsky et al., 2026), and Reasoners (Kordjamshidi et al., 2026), exists because
the community kept discovering that fluent systems reason incorrectly. CPUN E S Y shares the ab-
stention instinct but moves the check earlier, before execution rather than after generation, and the
fixed-trace comparison in §4.2 separates what source checking adds from what voting already pro-
vides. Appendix A gives the full comparison.

3.1   A RCHITECTURE

A predicate unit is either a ground fact or a Horn rule. CPUNeSy uses these units to represent both
source premises and derived conclusions. A model proposes units and inference steps, and a checker
decides which proposals may enter the state. This addresses two common failure modes. A model
that produces an answer and rationale together may skip a required inference. A model that writes
a formal problem for a solver may introduce facts or rules unsupported by the source. Figure 1
illustrates the two failure modes, while Figure 2 shows how CPUNeSy separates model proposals
from checked state updates.
