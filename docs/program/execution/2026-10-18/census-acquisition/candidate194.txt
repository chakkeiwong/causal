         Bruna Bazaluk Machado Videira. Grandes Modelos de Linguagem como Ferramen-
         tas para Descoberta Causal. Dissertação (Mestrado). Instituto de Matemática e Esta-
         tística, Universidade de São Paulo, São Paulo, 2026.


     Descoberta Causal (CD) é a tarefa de inferir estruturas causais automaticamente, usualmente a partir
de dados observacionais. Recentemente, muitos trabalhos têm demonstrado interesse em utilizar o conhe-
cimento contido nos Grandes Modelos de Linguagem (LLMs) para realizar tarefas de CD. Porém, os algorit-
mos de CD existentes atualmente que utilizam LLMs, geram apenas um único Grafo Direcionado Acíclico
(DAG) sem nenhuma forma de incerteza, o que diminui a confiança no resultado. Logo, esta dissertação
explora métodos de Aprendizado Estrutural Bayesiano (BSL) — os quais produzem distribuições de grafos,
representações naturais de incerteza — e utiliza LLMs para aprimorar sua capacidade de inferência. Este
trabalho tem três objetivos principais. Primeiramente, discutimos e revisamos algoritmos básicos de CD e
abordagens baseadas em LLMs. Há também uma curta seção que explora diferentes modos de calcular a
incerteza da saída de um LLM. Em segundo lugar, investigamos o uso de LLMs em conjunto com métodos
de BSL para CD. Especificamente, propomos utilizar o conhecimento do LLM como uma distribuição a
priori do modelo BSL sobre possíveis grafos. Nossos experimentos mostram que, tais distribuições a priori
retiradas da LLM podem aperfeiçoar o desempenho dos métodos Bayesianos. Finalmente, o texto também
traz um trabalho onde focamos em Grafos Ancestrais (AGs), o que nos permite lidar com modelos que
contenham confundidores latentes. Além disso, nossa abordagem utiliza uma LLM como um “expert-in-the-
loop”, misturando a distribuição aprendida pelos dados com a informação recebida do especialista, no caso a
LLM, durante o processamento do modelo. Nossos experimentos mostram que nosso método é competitivo
em relação a outros que abordam confundidores latentes, tanto em conjuntos de dados sintéticos quanto
reais; além disso, nosso design para incorporar o feedback de um especialista humano (simulado) ou de um
LLM melhora a qualidade da inferência.

         Bruna Bazaluk Machado Videira. Large Language Models as Tools for Bayesian
         Causal Discovery. Thesis (Master’s). Institute of Mathematics and Statistics, Univer-
         sity of São Paulo, São Paulo, 2026.


     Causal Discovery (CD) is the task of automatically inferring causal structures, typically from obser-
vational data. Recently, there has been much interest in utilizing domain knowledge from large language
models (LLM) in causal discovery. However, existing LLM-based approaches only output a single directed
acyclic graph (DAG) without uncertainty, which can be unreliable. Thus, the focus of the present disserta-
tion is exploring Bayesian Structure Learning algorithms — which produce distributions of graphs, natural
representations of uncertainty — and using LLMs to enhance their inference power. The present disser-
tation has three main goals. The first is discussing and reviewing the basic algorithms of CD, and also
focusing on LLM-based approaches. There is also a short section that explores different methods of defin-
ing the LLM’s output reliability. Secondly, we intend to investigate the usage of LLMs alongside Bayesian
structure learning (BSL) methods for causal discovery. In particular, we propose to harness the domain
knowledge from the LLMs as the prior distribution over graphs. Our experiments show that LLM-informed
priors can improve the performance of BSL methods. Lastly, this text describes another approach, which
focuses on Ancestral Graphs (AGs), where we do not need the sufficiency assumption. Furthermore, our
model describes a method that uses the LLM as an expert-in-the-loop, mixing the data information with
the outside knowledge during the model processing. Our experiments show that our method is competitive
against others that address latent confounding on both synthetic and real-world datasets; and our design
for incorporating feedback from a (simulated) human expert or a Large Language Model (LLM) improves
inference quality.


Keywords: Causality. Causal Discovery. Bayesian Causal Discovery. Large Language Models.

Introduction

    Understanding the correlation between random variables in a system is very im-
portant for many practical sciences. However, finding probabilistic relationships is not
enough to describe all the knowledge contained in a system. Many problems are unsolv-
able if we look at them only through a probabilistic correlation lens. Moreover, ever since
the dawn of science, when it was simply called Philosophy, humans have been looking
for the “causes” of many natural phenomena.
    This is where Causality stands out as a singular point of view. Considering that a
model admits not only a simple correlation among variables but also cause-and-effect
relationships enables the use of a wider set of convenient tools (Pearl, 2009; Rubin, 1974).
   There are two main focuses in Causality: Causal Inference and Causal Discovery. The
former consists on studying the causal effect (see Definition 6) some random variable
may have on another, and the latter focuses on finding a direct acyclic graph (DAG) 1 that
represents the causal relationships between the variables. This Master’s thesis is focused
on Causal Discovery (CD).
     Observational data is limited, e.g. different graph structures can explain the same sta-
tistical patterns equally well — which we discuss in the following Related Work Section.
Thus, it is impossible to distinguish between certain causal directions without further
intervention or prior knowledge. That is why a reliable CD algorithm should output the
uncertainty of its result, either by giving confidence scores for specific edges or a posterior
distribution over possible graphs. This is crucial for high-stakes decisions, as it helps dif-
ferentiate between relationships that are supported by the data and those that may be
simply noise.
    Neither of the classic CD methods outputs any uncertainty over the final answer. Al-
ternatively, Bayesian Structure Learning (BSL) methods do not learn one graph, but a
distribution of graphs. These algorithms use a prior distribution — representing any previ-
ous knowledge available over the distribution — and a likelihood function to learn the
posterior.
    Considering the practical difficulty of the CD task, many of the classic algorithms were
adapted to accept the input of prior knowledge of a system. Some accept information on
the existence of specific edges, others only a correlation between some variables. BSL al-
gorithms already rely on a prior distribution, an ideal place to insert previous knowledge.

        Usually, this prior information is provided by a human expert of that specific research
    area. However, depending on a human process may be slow and expensive. Consequently,
    many CD works have been using Large Language Models (LLMs). Some use the LLM
    as the main piece of the CD framework (Jiralerspong et al., 2024), others, only as a
    helper (Li et al., 2025a).
        LLMs are very good at eliciting knowledge from the database they were trained
    on (Zhu et al., 2024). In this work, we leverage this idea to develop methods to use LLMs
    as tools to help in BSL models.


    Related Work
        From an applied scientist’s perspective, CD appears to be the ideal solution. Neverthe-
    less, in many cases, e.g. Linear Gaussian (LG) causal models, it is proven to be unindenti-
    fiable without some background knowledge of the problem (Eberhardt, 2017). Without
    further information, one can only discover that some variables might be correlated, but
    not whether they represent the causes or effects. For this reason, many algorithms do
    not focus on finding the causal DAG but its Markov Equivalence Class (MEC, see Def-
    inition 14), or other structures that contain information about independence relations
    between the variables. However, in other cases, it is possible to explore the asymmetry
    of the model in order to find which variable represents the cause and which represents
    the effect (Genin and Mayo-Wilson, 2020).
        The two classical CD algorithms are PC (Spirtes et al., 1993), which outputs a MEC,
    and FCI (J. Zhang, 2008) — a faster version of PC —, that outputs a PAG. Both of these
    algorithms are constraint-based and use conditional independence tests in order to find
    the edges of the graph. Besides this strategy, there are also score-based algorithms, like
    Greedy Equivalence Search (GES) (Chickering, 2003), which starts with an empty graph
    and iteratively adds an edge based on a likelihood score.
        The mentioned methods rely on assumptions that may not be true in real prob-
    lems, like faithfulness, which states that the conditional independences from the joint
    distribution represented by the causal graph imply d-separations (Definition 1) in the
    DAG. Meanwhile, there are some methods that rely on different assumptions, such as
    LiNGAM (Hoyer et al., 2006), which is based on Independent Component Analysis (ICA)
    theory.


    Objectives
       Given all these considerations, the goals of this Master’s project are:
       1. Studying Causal Discovery and describing the main algorithms, highlighting the
          different approaches and their advantages and disadvantages.
       2. Studying how LLMs are being used in Causal Discovery.
       3. Focusing on BSL methods, developing a framework to use LLMs as tools to help these
          methods on CD tasks as well as doing experiments with a specific BSL method and
          different LLMs.

   4. Investigating incorporating LLMs as experts in-the-loop in causal discovery al-
      gorithms, while considering uncertainty quantification, in collaboration with re-

The third goal was presented as a poster during the Causal Abstractions and Representa-

Thesis Outline
    We start this dissertation with a review of the field of Causality, focusing on Causal
Discovery (CD). Then, in Chapter 2, we discuss the state-of-the-art research regarding
the usage of LLMs in such algorithms. Chapters 3 and 4 describe my main contribu-
tions.Finally, Chapter 5 brings a conclusion to this Master thesis.

Contributions
    • A review on Causal Discovery:
      We present a consistent review on Causal Discovery algorithms, covering a subset
      of them, considering the main algorithms of each paradigm. We also discuss the use
      of LLMs for Causal Discovery and the most recent advances in this matter.
    • A framework with LLMs as tools for BSL:
      We describe a new way of eliciting a prior distribution of possible DAGs from an
      LLM to use in BSL methods. In the present work, we show results using a specific
      BSL method, DiBS (Lorch et al., 2021).
    • Using an LLM as an expert-in-the-loop for Ancestral GFlowNets(AGFN):
      We describe our model, Ancestral GFlowNets (AGFN), which generates ancestral
      graphs (AGs) based on a score that reflects our uncertainty about the causal rela-
      tionships. Building on this, we propose a system for getting expert feedback. This
      includes a strategy to decide exactly which questions to ask the expert, along with
      a method to update the AGFN with their answers.

Chapter 1

Causality

    This chapter contains the necessary background regarding Causality. We start with
a description of the field, along with motivations. Then, we present a concise review of
Causal Inference. Finally, we explore Causal Discovery and its main methods.


1.1     What is Causality?
   Causality is an area that studies the causal relationships between variables in a system.
One example would be querying about how much a variable 𝑌 would change if we provoke
a change on its cause 𝑋 .
    The present work focuses on the framework developed by Pearl (2009), in which a
causal model defines the analyzed system alongside a causal graph. A causal probabilis-
tic graphical model may be defined as a pair (𝒢 , 𝑓 ), where 𝒢 is a causal graph, a DAG
with nodes V = (𝑉0 , 𝑉1 , … , 𝑉𝑛 ) representing the causal relationships between the variables
V, and 𝑓 is a probability density function over V (Stern, 2025).
    Markovianity is one of the most common assumptions made when performing in-
ference over the causal model, or discovering the causal graph given some data. However,
we need one important definition in order to define it: d-separation.
Definition 1 (d-separation, Pearl, Glymour, et al. (2016)). Given a graph 𝒢 , a path 𝑐 is
blocked by a set of nodes 𝑍 if and only if
    • 𝑐 contains a chain of nodes 𝐴 → 𝐵 → 𝐶 or a fork 𝐴 ← 𝐵 → 𝐶 such that the middle
      node 𝐵 is in 𝑍 (i.e., B is conditioned on), or

1.1.1         Causal Models as Bayesian Networks
    Bayesian Networks are probabilistic graphical models that encode conditional inde-
pendences among variables using DAGs. They emerged side by side with Pearl’s notion
of causality (Pearl, 2009), and were firstly designed to define only causal relationships.
Nowadays, they are used in broader scopes.
Definition 5 (Bayesian Networks). A Bayesian Network is a pair (𝒢 , 𝑓 ), where 𝒢 is a
DAG with vertices V = {𝑉0 , 𝑉1 , … , 𝑉𝑛 }, and 𝑓 is a probability density funcion over the random
variables V, such that 𝑓 is Markovian w.r.t. 𝒢 .
   As the reader may notice, causal models derived from SCMs are causal Bayesian Net-
works (BNs).


1.2          Causal Inference
    Causal Inference is the study of causal queries and their estimands. In other words, it
studies quantities related to causal relationships or their approximation.
    When discussing the possible causal queries one can ask about a Causal Model, it is
essential to mention the causal hierarchy. It is composed of a three-rung ladder (Pearl
and Mackenzie, 2018), usually called “The Ladder of Causation”:
       1. Association: Queries related to observation. Example: “Does smoking cause lung
          cancer?”;
       2. Intervention: Queries related to actions (interventions) on the system. Example:
          “If we make every smoker stop smoking, would lung cancer incidence decrease?”

       3. Counterfactual: Queries about imaginary scenarios. Example: “If John hadn’t

    1.2.1    Intervention
        A common saying in Statistics is “correlation does not imply causation”. Let us give an
    example. Suppose we observe the rate of ice-cream consumption raises at the same time
    that the rate of sunburn cases does. We may say these variables are correlated, but is one
    causing the other? In order to answer this question we can make a mental experiment:
    suppose we intervene on the system by stopping the production of ice-cream, causing
    the consumption to decrease. Would the sunburn cases also decrease? Considering our
    knowledge of the world, we know the answer is no. Why? Because sunburn cases are
    caused by a sunny weather, as well as the ice-cream consumption raise.
        Nevertheless, as the example given above, some real-life experiments involve variables
    that cannot be intervened upon. Therefore, to study the effect of possible interventions
    in causal models, we need specific mathematical concepts.
    Definition 6 (Causal Effect, Pearl (2009)). Given two disjoint sets of variables, 𝑋 and 𝑌 ,
    in a SCM, the causal effect of 𝑋 on 𝑌 , denoted as 𝑃(𝑌 |𝑑𝑜(𝑋 )), is a function from 𝑋 to the

    probability of 𝑌 = 𝑦 induced by deleting from the Structural Causal Model all equations
    corresponding to variables in 𝑋 and substituting 𝑋 = 𝑥 in the remaining equations.
        It is only possible to calculate 𝑃(𝑌 |𝑑𝑜(𝑋 )) if it is identifiable, that is, if it is unique
    given the assumptions and data.

    spective constant. We observe that the updated causal model does not have edges pointing
    to 𝑋 , because it is now a constant. This process of removing graph edges given an inter-
    vention is called graph surgery.

1.3      Causal Discovery (CD)
    In this section, we describe what is Causal Discovery. Here, we discuss some applica-
tions, algorithms and their assumptions and more.


1.3.1      What is Causal Discovery?
    Causal Discovery (CD) is the task of finding relationships of cause and effect among
variables in a system. Given a dataset of observed data, causal discovery algorithms output
a graph — not always a DAG — that represents the model. In many cases, it is impossible
to find the real DAG given the data alone. This is the reason most of the CD algorithms
do not output a DAG but other graph structures that give different types of information
about the system, i.e. the ancestral relationships. Ancestral relationships are all causal
relationships not only between parent-child variables (direct), but also ancestral-child (in-
direct) — where ancestor means a variable that is either a parent of the given child or a
parent of an ancestor of the child.


1.3.2      Algorithms
    In this section, we discuss classic and state-of-the-art CD algorithms. As this area
has been getting more notoriety in the last years, many frameworks have been emerging
recently and thus are not mentioned here. We choose the standard classic algorithms
— PC, FCI, GES, LiNGAM — and Bayesian structure learners that are main references
for our work.
   Usually, CD algorithms are divided in three groups: constraint-based, score-based and
functional (based on SCMs). However, we add here another category: Bayesian Structure
Learning (BSL), which is discussed in detail in Section 1.3.3.

   DAGs that encode the same set of d-separation statements are part of an observable
equivalence class. This class is defined in terms of unshielded colliders, the structures that
represent the conditional independencies from the distribution associated to the graph.
Definition 12 (Unshielded collider (v-structure)). An unshielded collider is a collider
which parents are not adjacent. That is, given the triple of nodes 𝑋 → 𝑌 ← 𝑍 , 𝑌 is an
unshielded collider iff, 𝑋 and 𝑍 are not adjacent. The triple (𝑋 , 𝑌 , 𝑍 ) is called a v-structure.
Definition 13 (Graph skeleton). The skeleton of a directed, or partially directed, graph 𝒢
is a graph 𝒢 ∗ that has the same vertices and edges, except the edges are all undirected.
   With these two definitions, we are able to define the observable equivalence class,
more commonly described as Markov Equivalence Class (MEC).
Definition 14 (Markov Equivalence Class (MEC)). We say two DAGs 𝒢 and ℱ are ob-
servationally Markov equivalent iff they have the exact same v-structures and skeleton. The
Markov Equivalence Class (MEC) of a DAG 𝒢 is is the set of all DAGs that are observationally
Markov equivalent to 𝒢 .
    We invite the reader to observe that DAGs from the same MEC encode the same
CI statements. Thus, in order to represent this class, we usually use Completed PDAGs
(CPDAGs).
Definition 15 (Completed Partially DAGs (CPDAGs)). A Completed Partially DAG
(CPDAG) 𝒢 is a PDAG which directed edges are all compelled and undirected edges are all
reversible.
   Hence, constraint-based CD algorithms consider compelled edges and the skeleton
they suggest as constraints and use them to find the MEC that includes the true DAG.
Thus, they usually output a CPDAG.
  Peter-Clark (PC) (Spirtes et al., 1993) This is one of the first, and most well-known,
CD algorithms. It consists of 5 steps:
    1. Define a complete and undirected graph over the variables’ set;
    2. Remove edges according to a series of (heuristically chosen) applied CI tests;
    3. With the skeleton and CI tests in hands, identify the v-structures;
    4. Orient the edges according to the found v-structures and the acyclicity constraint;
    5. The result is a CPDAG encoding the MEC.
    Fast Causal Inference (FCI) (J. Zhang, 2008) This algorithm is an extention of PC,
which, differently from its predecessor, accepts causal insufficient cases. Thus, it does not
output a CPDAG, but a graphical structure that represents the MEC of a special type of
graph that considers the existence of possible latent confounders. The entire algorithm
is very similar to PC, however, there are a few new constraints when orienting edges in
order to consider selection bias and latent confounders.

    We highlight that these algorithms are agnostic to the CI test, meaning that they may
be applied in many different scenarios, like discrete, continuous or even mixed data. More-

     distributions of graphs.
         Greedy Equivalent Search (GES) (Alonso-Barba et al., 2013) The algorithm be-
     gins by initializing an empty graph, and has two main phases: a forward search and a
     backward search. During the forward phase, the graph is modified by iteratively adding
     the specific edge that yields the highest score improvement. Then, when adding edges no
     longer increases the score, it moves to the next step. In the backward phase, it removes
     the edge that generates the highest scoring graph, continuing until the algorithm reaches
     a local maximum. It is important to say that this algorithm assumes causal sufficiency.

         As it is assumed that DAGs in the same MEC have the same score, these algorithms
     also output MECs. One advantage over constraint-based methods is that it avoids the use
     of CI tests, which can be unreliable when dealing with limited sample sizes, particularly
     due to challenges in controlling Type I errors (Farcomeni, 2008).
         However, as these methods rely on searching for the graph that maximizes a score, the
     task becomes an NP-hard problem, similar to other structure learning challenges (Zhou
     and Q. Chen, 2022). Consequently, since it is computationally infeasible to score every
     possible graph, most algorithms employ greedy strategies that may converge to a local
     optimum rather than the global one.

    The advantages of BSL algorithms is that they acknowledge the reliability of the re-
sults by design. They are usually very flexible, you can choose the likelihood and prior
distribution that fit your specific causal model.

technique employed, the process can be computationally expensive, especially when the
causal graph contains a large number of variables.

    Our work uses LLMs as tools in CD tasks. Thus, we are not explaining the details of
these models. We start with a short explanation of LLMs. Then, the chapter is divided in
two, first, we discuss the state-of-the-art of using LLMs in CD tasks. Lastly, we explore

    Large Language Models are Generative Language Models, deep learning models that
generate text based on predicting the most probable token. Some better-known models
(e.g. DeepSeek, GPT) are autoregressive and use the transformer architecture (Vaswani
et al., 2017) in parts or entirely.
    As these models work with texts and language, their basic elements are strings. A
string is a sequence of characters and the vocabulary of an LLM is a set of strings. Each
element from this set is a token. Usually, tokens are words or sub-words (parts of a word).
The process of dividing a text into tokens and creating an LLM’s vocabulary is called
tokenization (Manning and Schutze, 1999).

                                            𝑖=0

where ‘prompt’ is the input text (token sequence) the LLM receives from the user.
   Then, an LLM is a model that receives a sequence of tokens as input (the prompt) and
outputs the next token probability distribution according to the input.
    These models are trained with many texts to perform various tasks, including text
classification and translation. The only difference between a Language Model (LM) and

     2.1     Using LLMs in CD
        Recently, many works have been using LLMs to improve CD algorithms. Mostly, the
     frameworks focus on using the LLM alongside a data-driven model. The most common
     methodologies for querying the LLM, according to Wan et al., 2024, are:
        1. Pair-wise discovery;
        2. Conditional independence tests;
        3. Full graph discovery.
     Most methodologies focus on extracting pairwise relationships from the LLM (Ban,
     Lyuzhou Chen, et al., 2025; Jiralerspong et al., 2024); however, alternative approaches
     attempt to infer the global graph structure at once (Ban, Lyvzhou Chen, et al., 2023). On
     top of that, recent works have also used LLMs for providing indirect information that
     improves the CD process. For example, Li et al., 2025b uses the LLM for intervention
     targeting, while Vashishtha et al. (2023) solely focus on finding a causal order, instead of
     the causal graph. Surprisingly, none of the mentioned works considers any uncertainty
     metric for the answers given by the LLM.
         A recent paper by Ankan and Textor, 2025 considered an expert-in-the-loop of the
     CD taks, which could be either a human or an LLM. The approach is based on using CI
     statements or an iteration of the classical PC algorithm (Spirtes et al., 1993) alongside an
     expert-in-the-loop. However, this approach outputs a single DAG and does not consider
     any uncertainty over the final result. Importantly, the used dataset does not have a defined
     true graph to be compared to and the code is not available. Thus, we were unable to test
     their framework and use it as comparison to our work, which is described in detail in
     Chapters 3 and 4.


     2.2     LLM’s Output Uncertainty
         Analyzing the uncertainty of LLMs’ outputs is a critical step in our work, since we
     transform the LLM’s response in a prior distribution that represents our prior knowledge
     of the causal relationships in the model.
         Currently, there is an ongoing discussion in the literature about how to calculate the
     uncertainty over an LLM’s response. The various approaches for analyzing this uncer-
     tainty can be categorized based on the level of model access required and the specific
     methodology used to elicit or compute uncertainty estimates Shorinwa et al., 2025.
        Token-Level Uncertainty Quantification.
     Token-level methods leverage the model’s underlying probability distribution for each
     generated token to estimate confidence (Shorinwa et al., 2025). These are generally called
     white-box or grey-box approaches because they require access to the model’s internal
                                                                                              19

2.2 | LLM’S OUTPUT UNCERTAINTY




logits (Shorinwa et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025; H. Ma et al., 2025). Common measures
include average token log-probability, perplexity, and maximum entropy of the probabil-
ity distribution for each token (Shorinwa et al., 2025).
    However, there is a major drawback in these methods: high token likelihood is not
always correlated with factual accuracy. A false statement may be built by tokens that fit
naturally in context, giving them a high probability, despite the sentence being factually
incorrect (Xiong et al., 2023; H. Ma et al., 2025).
   Self-Verbalized Uncertainty.
These methods prompt the LLM to directly express its confidence in natural language.
This is a black-box approach, mostly used in closed-source models like GPT-4o. Here,
prompt engineering becomes important. Many strategies are used to improve the accu-
racy of these verbalized estimates, e.g. :
    • Chain-of-Thought (CoT), where the model reasons step-by-step;
    • Self-Probing, where the model evaluates its own previously generated answer in a
      new session;
    • Top-K Guesses, where the model provides multiple possible answers and a proba-
      bility for each (Xiong et al., 2023).
   The main problem with these approaches is that LLMs tend to be overconfident when
verbalizing uncertainty (Shorinwa et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2023).
    Semantic-Similarity.
Because an LLM might use different words to mean the same thing, these methods focus
on semantic meaning rather than exact token matches. They may use Natural Language
Inference (NLI) models to group semantically equivalent responses into clusters and com-
pute semantic entropy over those clusters (Shorinwa et al., 2025).
    Consistency-Based Approaches.
These methods quantify uncertainty by analyzing the consistency between multiple re-
sponses generated for the same query. The method we use in our contributions may be
classified as consistency-based. Some examples of sampling strategies are:
    • Sampling with a temperature parameter > 0;
    • Prompt paraphrasing (varying the input prompt);
    • Providing misleading hints to check if the model changes its answer (Xiong et al.,
      2023; H. Ma et al., 2025).
    Hidden State Probing.
This emerging field investigates the inner processes of the model to find information
on the output’s uncertainty. The work of L. Liu et al., 2024, for example, suggests that
hidden neurons and activations in the model’s internal layers encode information about
its own confidence. Probing methods involve training a simple linear classifier on these
intermediate activations to predict whether a response is accurate (Shorinwa et al., 2025).

3.1     Problem Definition
    The goal of this work is to use LLMs as a source of expert domain knowledge to
improve existing Bayesian CD algorithms. For this purpose, we elicit from GPT-4o (Ope-
nAI et al., 2024) and DeepSeek-V3 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025) a prior distribution, which
corresponds to the probabilities of each edge of the causal DAG. The obtained prior is
then pluged within a BSL framework, DiBS (Lorch et al., 2021), a differentiable Bayesian
structure learning algorithm based on variational inference.
   Our process follows three simple steps:
Given a dataset,
   1. Elicit the probability of each possible edge from the LLM (prior distribution);
   2. Plug in this prior to a BSL framework;
   3. Run the BSL framework and get the posterior distribution.
    Firstly, we need to choose how to define the reliability of the LLM’s response. In the
present work, we decide to use a Monte Carlo estimate in order to obtain a probability dis-
tribution over the outputs. We refer the reader to Section 2.2 for a discussion on the topic.
   It is important to notice that, although our experiments and results are based on DiBS,
our framework is not dependent on it. The method may be used jointly with any other

4.1     Goal
    This work, similarly to the work on the last section, focuses on developing a CD frame-
work that considers uncertainty quantification and incorporating information beyond the
data on the process. One difference is that this work presents two different manners of
including this knowledge: i) including human expert knowledge and ii) including the
knowledge extracted from an LLM.
    Differently from most CD algorithms that consider expert knowledge, e.g. T. Wang
et al., 2022, ours considers the expert to be imperfect, meaning the information added to
the CD method has a level of reliability. This work proposes a two-stage probabilistic CD
framework called Ancestral GFlowNets (AGFN):

1. First, an AGFN approximates an energy-based distribution over AGs based on a given
   score function (Schwarz, 1978);
2. Then, it probes an uncertain expert (possibly an LLM) on the relationship between
   selected pairs of variables and uses their feedback for self-refinement.

Thus, AGFN can provide accurate responses even in the presence of unfaithful data, in
contrast with standard CD algorithms.
   As described above, differently from the work in Chapter 3 — where the knowledge
was used as a prior distribution only —, the knowledge here is used in-the-loop, that is,

     4.2      Ancestral Graphs (AGs)
         An ancestral graph (AG) 𝒢 over variables V is a directed graph which contains both
     directed and bidirected edges, assuming no selection bias(T. Richardson and Spirtes,
     2002; J. Zhang, 2007).
     Definition 17 (Ancestral Graph (AG)). Let us define the set 𝐴𝑛(𝑉𝑖 ) as the set of ancestors
     of 𝑉𝑖 . An Ancestral Graph is a graph with directed and bidirected edges, such that:
        1. there are no directed cycles, so, if 𝑉𝑖 → 𝑉𝑗 is in 𝒢 , then 𝑉𝑗 ∉ 𝐴𝑛(𝑉𝑖 );

     causal relations that exist through latent confounding (T. Richardson and Spirtes, 2002).

4.6.2              LLM as the Expert
    In this section we describe our experiments using GPT4o as an expert- in-the-loop. In
previous, unreported, experiments, DeepSeek was also tested; the results however, were
noticeably worse. Importing the conclusions from Bazaluk et al., 2025, we presume the
high uncertainty — derived from the sparsity of the distribution generated by DeepSeek
— affects negatively the results.


   Here we also use the Sachs dataset. However, as we are interested in considering cases
with latent confounders, we consider three different versions: Sachs-5 with 5 variables,
Sachs-7 with 7 variables, and the original Sachs with 11 variables.

   To generate the reduced datasets, we apply the Fisher-z transformation to test the inde-
pendence of all pairs of variables. The ones with the most false observed independencies
were treated as unobserved, ensuring the resulting graphs remain sufficiently connected
and not overly sparse.

    Figure 4.3 shows that incorporating the LLM’s knowledge as feedback in-the-loop is
inversily proportional to the 𝐸-SHD of the posterior distribution. Importantly, GPT4o is
not a perfect expert, an oracle. In fact, its accuracy is around 70% in these experiments,
that is, some responses are incorrect. However, the E-SHD curve is decrescent, showing
that considering the reliability of the feedback guarantees robustness.

     4.7     Conclusion
         This work introduces AGFN, a probabilistic CD method that not only considers la-
     tent confounding, but also combines a (potentially noisy) feedback from an expert, either
     human or LLM, with the data from the model.
        Especially, our model is not limited to the BIC score or LG causal models. For example,
     we could replace the BIC with other scoring functions better suited for different variable
     types, such as discrete data (Drton and T. S. Richardson, 2008). Moreover, our frame-
     work allows for efficient updates because we do not need to retrain the AGFN every time
     we incorporate new feedback.
        Our results also show that the framework successfully converges to the true AG even
     when relying on imperfect experts, such as an LLM.


     4.8     Discussion and Comparison
        Both works, from Chapters 3 and 4 have similar conclusions: using the knowledge
     from the LLM yields better results than the Bayesian CD method alone.
         Unfortunately, in the two works we work with different types of graph. Then, it is
     not possible to compare the results, as in Chapter 3 we work in the space of DAGs and
     in Chapter 4 we work in the space of AGs. Moreover, the second work focuses on graphs
     with latent confounders, but the first considers normal DAGs.

5.1     Causal Discovery and LLMs
    The present dissertation starts discussing about the field of Causality and, more specif-
ically, Causal Discovery. We explore the definitions and algorithms that solve the impor-
tant task of finding the relationships among given variables in a dataset.
    Additionally, we talk about eliciting the knowledge base from an LLM to improve
CD algorithms. We observe that most of the people have been using the LLMs alongside
data-based algorithms. This happens because usually LLMs are not reliable enough to
guarantee the prediction of a graph. Then, we enter in an interesting discussion on the
reliability of LLMs’ output. We see in Section 2.2 that there are many ways of estimating
this uncertainty.


5.2     Contributions
    The main contributions of the present work are two experimental works I published.
Bazaluk et al., 2025 was presented at CAR Workshop at UAI 2025 and Silva et al., 2025
is under review for a journal.
    An LLM-Guided Prior with DiBS
This work uses LLMs as a source of expert domain knowledge to improve existing
Bayesian CD algorithms. For this purpose, we elicit from GPT-4o (OpenAI et al., 2024)
and DeepSeek-V3 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025) a prior distribution, which corresponds to
the probabilities of each edge of the causal DAG. The obtained prior is then plugged
within a BSL framework, DiBS (Lorch et al., 2021), a differentiable Bayesian structure
learning algorithm based on variational inference.
42

         Although adding the LLM prior yielded better results than using DiBS’ original prior,
     the best SHD results were from the prior distributions alone — not using DiBS, considering
     only the distribution elicited from the LLM; for both the MAGIC-7 and Summer datasets,
     using DeepSeek alone yields the best overall performance in terms of both E-SHD and
     AUROC. This indicates that modern LLMs are highly effective at solving simpler CD tasks,
     particularly discovering small graphs in well-known domains.
         Expert-Aided Causal Discovery of Ancestral Graphs using GFlowNets
     As the work described above, this one focuses on developing a CD framework that con-
     siders uncertainty quantification and incorporating information beyond the data on the
     process. One difference is that this work presents two different manners of including
     this knowledge: i) including human expert knowledge and ii) including the knowledge
     extracted from an LLM.
        Differently from most CD algorithms that consider expert knowledge, T. Wang et al.,
     2022 for example, ours considers the expert to be imperfect, meaning the information
     added to the CD method has a level of reliability.
         Our results show that the framework successfully converges to the true AG even when
     relying on imperfect experts, such as an LLM. Moreover, adding the LLM knowledge im-
     proved the results.
        Possible Future Work
     A possible future direction could be to build a BSL framework from scratch that lever-
     ages the knowledge-retrieving powers of the LLMs. In this case, we could use Retrieval-
     Augmented Generation (RAG) to improve the LLM’s knowledge base with specific infor-
     mation about the dataset’s context.
