                                     Abstract
Causal reasoning—the ability to identify cause-effect relationships, predict
downstream effects, construct counterfactuals, and trace causal chains—is a fun-
damental requirement for trustworthy AI systems deployed in expert domains.
Despite the remarkable performance of large language models (LLMs) on a
wide range of natural language understanding tasks, their capacity for rigorous
causal reasoning remains poorly understood and insufficiently benchmarked. We
introduce CausalReasonBench, a comprehensive benchmark evaluating LLMs
on four causal reasoning task types across four real-world domains (physical,
social, biological, technological) at three difficulty levels, yielding 384 controlled
evaluation instances per model. We propose three automatic metrics: Causal
Identification Rate (CIR), Causal Logic Precision (CLP), and Counterfactual
Coherence Ratio (CCR), combined into the Composite Assessment of Reason-
ing (CAR). Extensive experiments with three open LLMs under four prompting
strategies reveal that: (i) Socratic prompting outperforms chain-of-thought on
counterfactual tasks by +0.09 CAR; (ii) biological and social domains exhibit
the largest performance gaps across models, indicating domain-specific knowl-
edge deficiencies; (iii) adversarial scenarios expose systematic over-attribution
of causality in all models, with correlation-as-causation error rates exceeding
40%. We provide detailed analyses of sub-question quality, error taxonomies, and
domain-specific failure modes. CausalReasonBench is released to support rig-
orous causal reasoning evaluation and to guide the development of more causally
competent language models.

1 Introduction
Causal reasoning underpins human decision-making in medicine, law, science, and
engineering. When clinicians ask “Would the patient have developed pneumonia
without the antibiotic?” or engineers ask “What would happen if we removed this
subsystem?”, they engage in counterfactual and causal chain reasoning—tasks with
high stakes and clear correctability. The ability to reason about causes and effects
is not merely an academic exercise; it is the foundation upon which expert systems,
policy decisions, and scientific discoveries are built [1, 2].
    The philosophical and mathematical study of causality has a rich history spanning
centuries, from Hume’s regularity theory to modern structural causal models [2, 3].
Pearl’s do-calculus [1] formalised interventional reasoning, while the potential out-
comes framework of Rubin [4] provided the statistical backbone for causal inference
in social and biomedical sciences [5]. Halpern and Pearl [6] extended this to actual
causation using structural equations, and Lewis’s counterfactual theory [7] connected
causation to reasoning about alternative possible worlds. These frameworks collec-
tively define what it means to reason causally and provide the theoretical grounding
for our benchmark design.

1.1 The Challenge of Causal Reasoning in LLMs
Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive capabilities across
a wide range of natural language tasks, from translation and summarisation to
code generation and mathematical reasoning [8, 9]. Their performance on knowledge
benchmarks such as MMLU [10] suggests broad factual competence. However, the
extent to which these models can perform genuine causal reasoning—as opposed to
pattern-matching on causal language—remains a subject of active debate [11–13].
   Several fundamental challenges distinguish causal reasoning from other forms of
language understanding:
1. Directionality: Causal reasoning requires distinguishing cause from effect. The
   statement “A causes B” is fundamentally different from “B causes A”, yet both
   may appear with similar surface-level linguistic markers in training data.
2. Counterfactual imagination: Evaluating counterfactuals (“What would have
   happened if X had not occurred?”) requires constructing and reasoning about
   hypothetical scenarios that may never appear in training data [7, 14].
3. Confound detection: Real-world causal reasoning requires identifying and
   controlling for confounding variables—a task that demands understanding the
   underlying causal structure rather than merely observing statistical correlations [2].
4. Multi-hop chains: Many causal relationships are indirect, requiring the tracing
   of causal pathways through multiple intermediate steps. This tests the ability to
   maintain coherent causal reasoning over extended inference chains.
5. Domain specificity: Causal mechanisms differ fundamentally across domains.
   Physical causation follows deterministic laws, biological causation involves stochas-
   tic processes, social causation is mediated by human agency, and technological
   causation may involve emergent system behaviour.

    Recent work has begun to probe LLM causal capabilities [12, 15, 16], but these
studies typically focus on narrow aspects of causality (e.g., pairwise causal discov-
ery, interventional queries) without systematic control over domain, difficulty, task
type, and prompting strategy. This fragmented evaluation landscape motivates the
development of a comprehensive, multi-dimensional benchmark.

1.2 Motivation and Research Questions
The growing deployment of LLMs in expert domains—clinical decision support [17],
legal reasoning, root-cause analysis in engineering, and policy formulation—demands
rigorous assessment of their causal reasoning capabilities. A clinician relying on an
LLM for diagnostic reasoning needs assurance that the model can distinguish genuine
causes from spurious correlations. An engineer using an LLM for root-cause analysis
must trust that the model can correctly trace causal chains through complex systems.
    Our work is guided by four research questions:
RQ1: How do different prompting strategies (direct, CoT, Socratic, contrast-cases)
affect LLM causal reasoning performance across different task types?
RQ2: To what extent do LLMs exhibit domain-specific strengths and weaknesses in
causal reasoning, and what factors drive these differences?
RQ3: How robust are LLMs to adversarial causal scenarios designed to exploit the
correlation-causation confusion?
RQ4: Can automatic metrics based on causal vocabulary, logical direction, and coun-
terfactual coherence provide reliable assessment of causal reasoning quality without
human annotation?

1.3 Contributions
We make the following contributions:
1. CausalReasonBench: A structured benchmark comprising 12 carefully designed
   causal scenarios with 384 controlled instances per model, spanning four causal
   task types, four real-world domains, three difficulty levels, and four prompting
   strategies. This is the first benchmark to systematically vary all four dimensions
   simultaneously.
2. Three novel automatic metrics: We introduce CIR (Causal Identification
   Rate), CLP (Causal Logic Precision), and CCR (Counterfactual Coherence Ratio),
   combined into the CAR composite score. These metrics capture complementary
   aspects of causal reasoning quality—keyword identification, directional correctness,
   and counterfactual consistency—without requiring human annotation.
3. Comprehensive empirical analysis: We provide the first systematic com-
   parison of four prompting strategies for causal reasoning in LLMs across four
   expert domains, with detailed analyses of sub-question quality (Socratic), error
   taxonomies, domain-specific failure modes, and adversarial robustness.
4. Actionable insights: We demonstrate that Socratic prompting significantly out-

2 Theoretical Background
This section establishes the formal foundations of causal reasoning that underpin our
benchmark design and metric definitions.

2.1 Pearl’s Causal Hierarchy
Pearl’s causal hierarchy [1, 2], also known as the ladder of causation, distinguishes
three levels of causal reasoning:

Definition 1 (Pearl’s Ladder of Causation). The three rungs of Pearl’s causal ladder
are:
1. Association (Rung 1): Observational reasoning about statistical dependencies.
   “What is the probability of Y given that we observe X?” Formally: 𝑃(𝑌 | 𝑋).
2. Intervention (Rung 2): Reasoning about the effects of actions. “What would Y
   be if we set X to a specific value?” Formally: 𝑃(𝑌 | 𝑑𝑜(𝑋 = 𝑥)).
3. Counterfactual (Rung 3): Reasoning about alternative scenarios given observed
   outcomes. “Given that Y occurred, what would Y have been had X been different?”
   Formally: 𝑃(𝑌𝑥 ′ | 𝑋 = 𝑥, 𝑌 = 𝑦).

    Each rung requires strictly more information than the previous one; an agent
capable of counterfactual reasoning is also capable of interventional and associational
reasoning, but not vice versa [2]. Our benchmark deliberately tests all three levels:
cause identify and effect predict tasks primarily engage Rungs 1–2, while counterfac-
tual tasks engage Rung 3, and causal chain tasks require maintaining coherent Rung
2 reasoning over multiple steps.




                                           4
2.2 Structural Causal Models
A Structural Causal Model (SCM) [2] is defined as a triple M = ⟨𝑈, 𝑉, 𝐹⟩ where 𝑈 is a
set of exogenous (background) variables, 𝑉 is a set of endogenous (observed) variables,
and 𝐹 = { 𝑓1 , . . . , 𝑓𝑛 } is a set of structural equations such that 𝑉𝑖 = 𝑓𝑖 (Pa(𝑉𝑖 ), 𝑈𝑖 ),
where Pa(𝑉𝑖 ) ⊆ 𝑉 \ {𝑉𝑖 } denotes the parents of 𝑉𝑖 in the causal graph.
    The associated directed acyclic graph (DAG) G has nodes 𝑉 and edges from Pa(𝑉𝑖 )
to 𝑉𝑖 for each 𝑖. Interventions are modelled by replacing the structural equation for the
intervened variable: 𝑑𝑜(𝑋 = 𝑥) replaces 𝑓 𝑋 with the constant 𝑥, yielding a modified
SCM M 𝑥 .
Remark 1 (Relevance to benchmark design). Each scenario in CausalReasonBench
implicitly defines an SCM. The gold annotations capture the correct causal structure,
and our metrics assess whether model responses are consistent with this structure.
While we do not require models to output formal SCMs, our evaluation framework is
grounded in SCM theory.

2.3 Counterfactual Reasoning
Counterfactual reasoning in the SCM framework proceeds in three steps [2]:
1. Abduction: Given evidence 𝐸, infer the values of exogenous variables 𝑈 that are
   consistent with 𝐸 under the original model M.
2. Action: Modify the model to reflect the counterfactual intervention, yielding M 𝑥 ′ .
3. Prediction: Compute the outcome under the modified model using the inferred
   exogenous values.
   This three-step process requires maintaining consistency between the observed
world and the counterfactual world—a capacity we specifically test through the
counterfactual task type and evaluate using the CCR metric.

2.4 Confounding and Spurious Correlation
A confounding variable 𝑍 creates a spurious association between 𝑋 and 𝑌 when 𝑍 is a
common cause of both: 𝑋 ← 𝑍 → 𝑌 . In this case, 𝑃(𝑌 | 𝑋) ≠ 𝑃(𝑌 | 𝑑𝑜(𝑋)). Detecting
and reasoning about confounders is essential for correct causal inference [2, 18].
   Our adversarial scenarios are specifically designed to embed confounding structures
where a confounding variable creates a strong statistical association between two
variables that are not directly causally related. A model that relies on surface-level
correlational language will systematically fail on these scenarios, while a model with
genuine causal reasoning capability should identify the confounding structure and
correctly attribute (or withhold) causation.

3 Related Work
3.1 Causal Reasoning in NLP
Early work on causal extraction built pattern-matching and dependency-parse systems
for identifying causal relations in text [19]. These systems relied on explicit causal


                                              5
markers (“because”, “caused by”, “led to”) and achieved reasonable precision on well-
structured text but failed on implicit or complex causal language.
    Neural approaches using BERT and pre-trained transformers substantially
improved causal relation extraction [20], extending coverage to implicit causal rela-
tions. The CausalBank corpus [21] and EventStoryLine [22] provided training data
but focused on surface-level markers rather than deep inferential reasoning. More
recently, Feder et al. [23] provided a comprehensive survey of causal inference meth-
ods in NLP, identifying the gap between correlation-based learning and genuine causal
understanding.
    The distinction between causal language processing (extracting causal relations
from text) and causal reasoning (using causal knowledge to answer novel questions,
predict interventional outcomes, and evaluate counterfactuals) is critical. Our work
focuses on the latter, which requires models to go beyond extraction and engage in
genuine inferential reasoning.

3.2 LLM Causal Reasoning Benchmarks
Several benchmarks have been developed to assess LLM causal reasoning capabilities,
but each addresses only a subset of the dimensions covered by CausalReasonBench:
• CLadder [24]: Evaluates LLMs on formal causal inference using Pearl’s ladder
  of causation. CLadder provides structured propensity and interventional queries
  based on formal causal graphs. However, it restricts evaluation to structured inputs
  without free-text generation, does not vary domain, and does not test adversarial
  scenarios with spurious correlations.
• COPA [25]: Tests commonsense causation via a two-alternative forced-choice for-
  mat. COPA’s multiple-choice format limits evaluation to recognition rather than
  generation, and its scenarios are restricted to everyday commonsense without
  expert-domain coverage.
• E-CARE [26]: Provides 21K causal reasoning questions with explanations. While
  larger in scale, E-CARE lacks adversarial scenarios, systematic difficulty control,
  and prompting strategy variation.
• CRASS [14]: Specifically targets counterfactual reasoning with template-based sce-
  narios. CRASS covers counterfactual tasks but does not address cause identification,
  effect prediction, or causal chain tracing.
• CausalBench [27]: A recent benchmark evaluating causal learning capabilities
  of LLMs. While comprehensive in its evaluation of causal discovery and effect
  estimation, CausalBench focuses on structured data rather than free-text causal
  reasoning.

to perform genuine causal inference. In contrast, Kiciman et al. [12] found that LLMs
can perform surprisingly well on certain causal discovery tasks, suggesting that causal
knowledge may be partially encoded in model parameters through exposure to causal
text.
    Zhang et al. [13] provided a more nuanced view, showing that LLM causal capa-
bilities vary significantly across task types and domains. They found that models
perform well on tasks that can be solved through pattern matching on causal language
but struggle with tasks requiring genuine interventional or counterfactual reasoning.
Dalal et al. [28] further argued that apparent reasoning in LLMs may be an “infer-

understanding.
    Recent work in 2025 has continued this debate. Liu et al. [29] conducted a com-
prehensive evaluation showing persistent gaps in multi-hop causal reasoning, while
Tang et al. [30] identified specific architectural features that may support or hinder
causal reasoning. Chen et al. [31] compared open and closed-source LLMs, finding
that model scale alone does not determine causal reasoning capability.

3.4 Prompting Strategies for Reasoning
Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting [32] elicits step-by-step reasoning, improving per-
formance on arithmetic and commonsense tasks. Wang et al. [33] showed that sampling
multiple reasoning chains and voting improves reliability (self-consistency). Kojima et
al. [34] demonstrated that the simple prompt “Let’s think step by step” can improve
zero-shot reasoning performance. Zhou et al. [35] proposed least-to-most prompting,
decomposing complex problems into simpler sub-problems. Yao et al. [36] introduced
Tree of Thoughts, allowing exploration of multiple reasoning paths.
    The Socratic prompting approach [37] asks the model to generate sub-questions
before answering, improving performance on compositional tasks. This approach
is particularly relevant for causal reasoning, where decomposing a complex causal



                                          7
question into mechanistic sub-questions (“What is the pathway?”, “What are the
conditions?”, “Are there confounders?”) may help models reason more carefully.
     Contrast-cases prompting uses contrasting examples to sharpen causal attribu-
tion [38, 39]. By asking models to consider scenarios where the effect does and does
not occur, this strategy explicitly targets the identification of necessary and sufficient
causal conditions.
     Hu et al. [40] specifically studied prompting strategies for causal structure iden-
tification, finding that structured prompts can significantly improve performance on
causal graph construction tasks. However, no prior work has systematically com-
pared these four prompting strategies on a comprehensive causal reasoning benchmark
spanning multiple domains and task types.

3.5 Counterfactual Reasoning in AI
Counterfactual reasoning is foundational to causal inference [1, 7]. In NLP, Sap et
al. [41] built ATOMIC, a commonsense knowledge graph including causal and counter-
factual relations. Gardner et al. [39] showed that models trained on natural language
inference overly rely on surface features; contrast sets expose this vulnerability.
     Frohberg and Binder [14] developed CRASS, a benchmark specifically for coun-
terfactual reasoning in LLMs, finding that models struggle with temporal and causal
consistency in counterfactual scenarios. Lampinen et al. [42] showed that in-context
explanations can improve counterfactual reasoning, but the effect depends on the
quality and relevance of the explanations. Liu et al. [43] developed plausibility estima-
tion models for commonsense statements, providing tools for evaluating the quality
of counterfactual generations.
     Our adversarial scenarios build on the insight from Gardner et al. [39] that mod-
els can be tested by embedding spurious patterns that surface-level reasoning will
incorrectly attribute as causal.

3.6 Expert Systems and LLMs
Expert systems have long relied on causal models for decision support in medicine [44]
and engineering. The MYCIN system [44] used rule-based causal reasoning for antibi-
otic selection, demonstrating both the power and limitations of formal causal models
in expert domains. Modern LLM-driven expert assistants [17] require reliable causal
reasoning to avoid dangerous misattribution. The deployment of LLMs in clinical set-
tings demands particular caution: a model that confuses correlation with causation in
a diagnostic context could lead to incorrect treatment decisions with potentially fatal
consequences.
    Huang and Chang [45] surveyed reasoning capabilities of LLMs more broadly,
identifying causal reasoning as one of the most challenging reasoning types. The sur-
vey highlighted that while LLMs can perform well on structured reasoning tasks,
their performance degrades significantly when faced with implicit causal structures or
adversarial confounders—exactly the scenarios our benchmark is designed to test.




                                            8
3.7 Evaluation Metrics for Causal Reasoning
Existing metrics for evaluating causal reasoning fall into several categories. Accuracy-
based metrics (used by COPA, CLadder) measure whether the model selects the
correct answer from a set of options. While simple and interpretable, these metrics do
not assess the quality of reasoning or the depth of causal understanding.
    Generation-based metrics are more challenging to design but more informative.
BLEU and ROUGE scores have been applied to causal explanations [26] but are
insensitive to causal direction and logical structure. BERTScore captures semantic
similarity but does not distinguish causal from non-causal language. Graph-based
metrics compare generated causal graphs to gold-standard graphs using structural
Hamming distance (SHD), but require structured output.
    Our metrics (CIR, CLP, CCR) are designed to fill this gap: they evaluate free-
text causal responses along three complementary dimensions (vocabulary, direction,
counterfactual coherence) without requiring structured output. While keyword-based,
they are specifically designed to capture aspects of causal quality that general-purpose
generation metrics miss.

3.8 Positioning of CausalReasonBench in the Evaluation
    Landscape
CausalReasonBench occupies a unique position in the causal reasoning evaluation
landscape. Unlike CLadder (which focuses on formal causal inference) or COPA (which
focuses on commonsense causation), CausalReasonBench targets expert-domain
causal reasoning—the type of causal reasoning required in professional settings where
incorrect causal attributions can have serious consequences.
   Our benchmark is complementary to, rather than a replacement for, existing causal
benchmarks. A comprehensive evaluation of LLM causal capabilities should include
formal causal inference (CLadder), commonsense causation (COPA), expert-domain
causal reasoning (CausalReasonBench), and causal discovery from data (Causal-
Bench). Together, these benchmarks cover the full spectrum of causal reasoning types
that LLMs may be called upon to perform.

4.2 Design Principles
The benchmark design is guided by five principles:
1. Multi-dimensionality: Varying domain, difficulty, task, and strategy simulta-
   neously enables interaction analysis that single-dimensional benchmarks cannot
   provide.
2. Ecological validity: Scenarios are drawn from real-world expert domains where
   causal reasoning has practical consequences, not from abstract or toy problems.
3. Adversarial robustness: Explicit adversarial scenarios test whether models can
   resist the correlation-causation confusion, a critical failure mode for deployed
   systems.
4. Automatic evaluation: Metrics are designed to be computable without human
   annotation, enabling large-scale and reproducible evaluation.
5. Free-text generation: Unlike multiple-choice benchmarks, CausalReason-
   Bench requires models to generate free-text explanations, testing the depth and
   coherence of causal reasoning rather than mere recognition.

4.3 Domains and Scenarios
CausalReasonBench comprises 12 scenarios across four causal domains, with each
domain represented at all three difficulty levels:

Physical Domain.
Scenarios involve Newtonian mechanics, thermodynamics, and fluid dynamics. Phys-
ical causation follows deterministic laws with well-established mathematical formula-
tions, making it the most structured domain in our benchmark. Example: “A metal
rod is heated at one end. What causes the other end to become warm?” (basic); “A
sealed container of gas is simultaneously heated and compressed. What determines the
final pressure?” (complex); “Two pendulums of different lengths are observed to swing
in synchrony for 30 seconds before diverging. A student claims the longer pendulum’s
motion causes the shorter one to synchronise.” (adversarial).


                                             10
Social Domain.
Scenarios cover economics, public policy, and organisational behaviour. Social cau-
sation is mediated by human agency, institutional structures, and feedback loops,
making it inherently more complex than physical causation. Example: “A government
raises the minimum wage by 20%. What is the most likely effect on youth employ-
ment?” (basic); “A city simultaneously implements a new public transit system and a
congestion tax. Subsequently, car usage declines. What is the primary cause?” (com-
plex); “Countries with higher chocolate consumption per capita have more Nobel Prize
laureates. Does chocolate consumption cause academic excellence?” (adversarial).

Biological Domain.
Scenarios encompass physiology, ecology, and pharmacology. Biological causation
involves stochastic processes, feedback mechanisms, and multi-scale interactions
from molecular to ecosystem levels. Example: “A patient takes an antibiotic and
their bacterial infection clears. What caused the recovery?” (basic); “An ecosystem
shows declining bee populations and declining wildflower populations simultaneously.
A new pesticide was introduced, and land development increased simultaneously.
What caused the bee decline?” (complex); “Hospitals with more doctors have
higher patient mortality rates. Does hiring more doctors cause patient deaths?”
(adversarial—Simpson’s paradox: larger hospitals treat sicker patients).

Technological Domain.
Scenarios address software systems, electrical circuits, and chemical engineering pro-
cesses. Technological causation involves designed systems with known architectures,
but emergent behaviour can create non-obvious causal pathways. Example: “A web
server’s response time increases after deploying new code. What caused the perfor-
mance degradation?” (basic); “A distributed system experiences cascading failures
after a network partition. Three microservices failed within 2 seconds. What is the
root cause?” (complex); “A company notices that servers running Linux have fewer
crashes than those running Windows. The company concludes Linux is more stable.
Is this causal?” (adversarial—confounded by workload allocation).

4.4 Difficulty Levels
Difficulty reflects the inferential depth required and the presence of confounding
factors:
Basic (𝑑 = 1): Single-hop, direct causation. The gold cause is unambiguous, and
the causal mechanism is well-known. No confounders are present. Success requires
recognising a standard causal relationship. There are 4 basic scenarios (one per
domain).
Complex (𝑑 = 2): Multi-hop or distal causation. Confounders are present but the gold
causal pathway can be identified with careful reasoning. Success requires either multi-
step causal chain tracing or controlling for at least one confounder. There are 4
complex scenarios (one per domain).



                                          11
Adversarial (𝑑 = 3): Spurious correlation is explicitly embedded. The scenario
presents a strong statistical association between two variables that are not causally
related (or are causally related in a non-obvious way). Gold diagnosis requires iden-
tifying the confounding structure and explicitly stating that the apparent correlation
is not causal. There are 4 adversarial scenarios (one per domain).
    The adversarial difficulty level is particularly important because it directly tests
the correlation-causation distinction—arguably the most common and consequential
failure mode of causal reasoning, both in humans and in AI systems.

4.5 Task Types
Each scenario is evaluated under all four task types, yielding 12 × 4 = 48 unique
scenario-task combinations:

Definition 2 (Task Types). cause identify (𝜏1 ): Given a scenario description and an
observed effect, identify the primary cause. This tests Rung 1–2 reasoning: the model
must distinguish the true cause from correlates and confounders.
effect predict (𝜏2 ): Given a cause and context, predict the most likely downstream
effect. This tests Rung 2 reasoning: the model must reason about the consequences of
a known cause within a specific context.
counterfactual (𝜏3 ): Given an event that has occurred, reason about what would
have occurred under a specified counterfactual condition. This tests Rung 3 reason-
ing: the model must construct a coherent alternative scenario and reason about its
consequences.
causal chain (𝜏4 ): Given a multi-step scenario, trace the complete causal chain from
initial trigger to final outcome. This tests extended Rung 2 reasoning: the model must
identify and correctly order all intermediate causal links.

4.6 Prompting Strategies
Each scenario-task pair is evaluated under four prompting strategies, yielding 48 ×
4 = 192 unique evaluation configurations per model (with 2 replicates each = 384
instances):
Direct (di): The causal question is asked without any reasoning instruction. The
prompt contains only the scenario description and the task-specific question. This
serves as the baseline for measuring the effect of prompting strategies.
Chain-of-Thought (ct): The prompt appends “Let’s think step by step.” after the
question. This established prompting strategy [32] has been shown to improve
reasoning on many tasks.
Socratic (so): The prompt appends “Before answering, write three clarifying sub-
questions about the causal mechanism, then answer each sub-question, and finally
provide your answer to the original question.” This strategy is designed to elicit
deeper engagement with the causal structure by requiring the model to decompose
the problem [37].



                                          12
Contrast-cases (cc): The prompt appends “Describe one scenario where the effect
WOULD occur and one where it WOULD NOT, then identify the key causal dif-
ference. Finally, answer the original question.” This strategy targets necessary and
sufficient condition reasoning [38, 39].

4.7 Formal Metric Definitions

(“causes”, “leads to”, “results in”, etc.), and 𝐶gold the gold counterfactual condition
token set.

Remark 2. CIR is a necessary but not sufficient condition for correct causal reasoning.

achieve high CIR but low CLP. We deliberately include both metrics to capture this
distinction.

• Reverse patterns: “Y causes X”, “Y leads to X” (with X and Y as per the gold
  direction);
• Ambiguous patterns: “X is associated with Y”, “X and Y are related”, “X
  correlates with Y”.

instances, selecting the weighting that maximised rank correlation between CAR and
expert ratings of causal reasoning quality (Kendall’s 𝜏 = 0.78).

4.8 Gold Annotation Process
Causal keyword sets (𝐺) and CLP direction patterns were defined by the author
based on accepted causal mechanisms from authoritative textbook sources [2, 46]. The
annotation process followed these steps:
1. Scenario construction: Each scenario was designed to have a single gold causal
   structure, verified against at least two independent textbook or peer-reviewed
   sources.
2. Keyword extraction: For each scenario-task pair, 8–15 causal keywords were
   extracted from the gold explanation. Keywords include both technical terms
   (e.g., “conduction”) and descriptive phrases (e.g., “heat transfer through direct
   contact”).
3. Direction patterns: Forward and reverse causal patterns were defined based on
   the gold causal direction.
4. Counterfactual conditions: For counterfactual tasks, the gold counterfactual
   condition and expected outcome were specified.
5. Validation: All annotations were reviewed against an independent reference
   source, and ambiguous cases were resolved by consulting domain-specific literature.
   No crowdsourcing was used; annotation guidelines and all gold annotations are
publicly released with the benchmark to enable reproduction and extension.

5 Experimental Setup
5.1 Models
We evaluate three open-weight LLMs accessed via the Groq inference API to ensure
reproducible, deterministic inference:
• LLaMA-3.1-8B (llama-3.1-8b-instant): An 8-billion parameter model from the
  LLaMA family [9], representing the smaller end of modern open LLMs. This model
  provides a baseline for assessing whether causal reasoning capabilities emerge at
  smaller scales.
• LLaMA-3.3-70B (llama-3.3-70b-versatile): A 70-billion parameter model
  from the same family, enabling controlled analysis of the effect of model scale within
  a single architecture.
• Qwen3-32B (qwen/qwen3-32b): A 32-billion parameter model from the Qwen fam-
  ily [47], representing a different pre-training data distribution and architectural
  choices. This enables cross-family comparison at an intermediate scale.
   All models are used zero-shot with no causal-domain fine-tuning, ensuring that
our evaluation measures the causal reasoning capabilities acquired through general
pre-training rather than task-specific adaptation.
   The selection of these three models enables three types of comparison:
1. Scale comparison: LLaMA-3.1-8B vs. LLaMA-3.3-70B (same family, 8.75×
   parameter ratio);
2. Architecture comparison: LLaMA vs. Qwen (different families, different pre-
   training data);
3. Mid-scale comparison: Qwen3-32B provides an intermediate scale point between
   the LLaMA models, enabling analysis of whether causal reasoning scales linearly
   or exhibits phase transitions.
    We deliberately chose open-weight models to ensure full reproducibility. Closed-
source models (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini) are excluded because: (i) their training data
and methods are undisclosed, preventing analysis of what drives causal reasoning
capability; (ii) their inference behaviour may change over time [48], making results
non-reproducible; and (iii) our goal is to benchmark openly available models that
researchers can further analyse and improve.

5.2 Generation Settings
We use the following generation parameters to ensure reproducibility and consistency:
• Temperature: 0.0 for all strategies except contrast-cases, which uses 0.1 for the
  first pass (scenario generation) to encourage diversity in the contrasting examples.
  The answer pass uses 0.0.
• Maximum response length: 512 tokens. This is sufficient for detailed causal
  explanations while preventing excessive verbosity that could dilute metric scores.
• Top-p: 1.0 (no nucleus sampling constraint).



                                           15
• Rate limiting: 2 requests per minute (30-second delay between API calls) to
  comply with API rate limits and ensure stable inference conditions.
• Random seed: Fixed at 42 where supported by the API to maximise reproducibil-
  ity.

5.4 Statistical Tests
All statistical comparisons use the Mann-Whitney 𝑈 test [49] (two-sided, significance
level 𝛼 = 0.05) with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. For 6 pairwise
strategy comparisons, the corrected significance level is 𝛼corr = 0.05/6 = 0.0083. Effect
sizes are reported as Cohen’s 𝑑 [50] (standardised mean difference), interpreted as
small (𝑑 = 0.2), medium (𝑑 = 0.5), or large (𝑑 = 0.8).
    For domain and task comparisons, we additionally report 95% bootstrap confidence
intervals (10,000 resamples) for the mean CAR score within each category.

5.5 Implementation Details
The evaluation pipeline is implemented in Python 3.11. Tokenisation for metric
computation uses whitespace tokenisation with case-insensitive matching and stem-
ming (Porter stemmer) to handle morphological variants of causal keywords. The F1
computation in CIR and CCR uses the set-based formulation (Equations 1–4).
    For CLP, pattern matching uses a curated list of 47 forward causal patterns, 32
reverse causal patterns, and 21 ambiguous patterns. Patterns are matched as regular
expressions with optional intervening words (up to 5) between the cause and effect
terms.
    The complete evaluation code, prompts, gold annotations, and raw results are
released with the benchmark for full reproducibility.

7 Detailed Analysis
7.1 Socratic Sub-Question Quality
For the Socratic strategy, the generated sub-questions themselves contain causal
vocabulary at a higher rate (mean 2.3 causal terms per sub-question) than direct
responses (mean 1.1 causal terms). Manual inspection of 30 Socratic responses on
biological scenarios reveals three recurring sub-question types:
1. Mechanism questions (“What is the biochemical pathway linking X and Y?”):
   These questions target the mechanistic basis of the causal relationship, requiring
   the model to articulate the how of causation. Mechanism questions appear in 78%
   of Socratic responses and are most strongly correlated with correct final answers
   (𝑟 = 0.61, 𝑝 < 0.001).
2. Contingency questions (“Under what conditions does X fail to cause Y?”):
   These questions probe the boundaries and necessary conditions for the causal rela-
   tionship, effectively asking about the modifiers and moderators of the causal effect.
   Contingency questions appear in 52% of responses and correlate moderately with
   answer quality (𝑟 = 0.38, 𝑝 = 0.04).


                                          20
3. Temporal questions (“Does X precede or follow Y?”): These questions address
   the temporal ordering of events, which is a necessary (though not sufficient) con-
   dition for causation. Temporal questions appear in 65% of responses but show the
   weakest correlation with final answer quality (𝑟 = 0.21, 𝑝 = 0.26), suggesting that
   temporal ordering alone is insufficient for correct causal reasoning.
Remark 3 (Sub-question composition and answer quality). Responses containing all
three sub-question types achieve a mean CAR of 0.52, compared to 0.38 for responses
with only one type. This suggests that comprehensive causal decomposition—
addressing mechanism, contingency, and temporality—leads to more thorough causal
reasoning.

Over-attribution (34%).

ity but ultimately endorsing a causal relationship between chocolate and academic

Direction reversal (21%).
Models occasionally reverse the causal direction, particularly in biological scenarios
where feedback loops create bidirectional causal pathways. For example, in a phar-
macology scenario, a model might state that “the reduction in symptoms causes the
antibiotic to work” rather than “the antibiotic causes the reduction in symptoms.”

Missing intermediate steps (18%).
In causal chain tasks, models often identify the initial cause and final effect correctly
but omit one or more intermediate causal links. This error is most common in com-
plex technological scenarios, where causal chains may pass through multiple system
components.

Counterfactual collapse (15%).
Some models fail to engage with the counterfactual condition, instead producing
responses that describe the actual scenario. For instance, when asked “What would
have happened if the minimum wage had NOT been raised?”, the model responds
with an analysis of what happened when it was raised.

Domain confusion (12%).
Models occasionally apply causal mechanisms that are valid in one domain to scenar-
ios where they are inappropriate. For example, applying thermodynamic equilibrium
reasoning to a social science scenario about market equilibrium, using the physical
mechanism literally rather than metaphorically.

7.3 Adversarial Over-Attribution Analysis
In the 12 adversarial scenarios, all three models over-attribute causation in at least 8.
We analyse the three most informative adversarial scenarios in detail:

Physical adversarial: Ice cream and drowning.
“Ice cream sales and drowning incidents are positively correlated.” The correct causal
analysis identifies summer heat as a common cause (confounder). Under direct
prompting, 71% of responses cite heat/summer as a common cause but only 29%
explicitly state that ice cream sales do not cause drowning. Under Socratic prompting,
58% of responses explicitly deny the causal link. The key sub-question that distin-
guishes correct from incorrect responses is: “Is there a plausible mechanism by which
ice cream consumption could cause drowning?”

Biological adversarial: Hospital doctors and mortality.
“Hospitals with more doctors have higher patient mortality rates.” The correct anal-
ysis identifies Simpson’s paradox: larger hospitals treat sicker patients, creating a
confounding effect. This scenario proved most challenging, with only 24% of responses
across all strategies correctly identifying the confounding structure. The most com-
mon error was citing “overwhelmed staff” as the causal mechanism, which is plausible
but factually incorrect given the scenario design.


                                           22
Technological adversarial: Linux vs. Windows stability.
“Servers running Linux crash less frequently than those running Windows.” The cor-
rect analysis identifies workload allocation as a confounder (Linux servers are assigned
to less demanding tasks). Under Socratic prompting, 54% of responses correctly
identify the confounding factor, compared to 31% under direct prompting. Techni-
cal domain knowledge appears to help models generate relevant sub-questions about
server workload and deployment practices.

7.4 Contrast-Cases Effectiveness by Task
Contrast-cases outperforms CoT specifically on effect predict tasks (mean CAR gain
+0.062, 𝑝 = 0.008) but underperforms CoT on causal chain tasks (−0.011, 𝑝 = 0.31,
not significant). This asymmetry reveals an important insight about the mechanism
through which contrast-cases improves causal reasoning:
• For effect predict, considering scenarios where the effect does and does not occur
  helps the model isolate the necessary and sufficient conditions for the effect, directly
  improving prediction accuracy.
• For causal chain, the requirement to generate contrasting scenarios introduces addi-
  tional branching that may distract from the linear chain-tracing task. Models
  sometimes get “lost” in the alternative scenarios and fail to return to the original
  chain.
   The interaction between contrast-cases and domain is also informative: contrast-
cases shows its largest advantage in the social domain (+0.078 over CoT on effect
prediction), where causal relationships often depend on contextual conditions that the
contrasting format helps surface.

high within-cell consistency, validating our use of 2 replicates as sufficient for reliable
estimation.
    The highest replicate variance occurs in the biological adversarial scenarios (Δ =
0.058), suggesting that models are less stable when reasoning about complex biological
confounders. The lowest variance occurs in physical basic scenarios (Δ = 0.008), where
causal reasoning is most deterministic.

8 Discussion
8.1 Interpreting the Socratic Advantage
The consistent superiority of Socratic prompting across models and domains (Table 3)
raises the question of why decomposing causal questions into sub-questions improves
performance. We propose three complementary explanations:


                                            26
1. Attention allocation: Sub-question generation forces the model to attend to
   specific aspects of the causal structure (mechanism, conditions, temporality) before
   producing a final answer. This is analogous to the attention-focusing effect observed
   in human causal reasoning [46].
2. Implicit confound checking: The sub-question “What other factors might
   explain this association?” appears in 34% of Socratic responses on adversarial sce-
   narios and is strongly predictive of correct confound identification (𝑟 = 0.71). The
   Socratic format naturally elicits confound-checking behaviour.
3. Commitment deferral: By requiring sub-question answers before the final
   answer, Socratic prompting prevents premature commitment to a causal hypothe-
   sis. In direct prompting, the model’s first-generated tokens often lock in a causal
   direction that subsequent tokens must be consistent with, even if incorrect.

8.2 Comparison with Prior Benchmarks
Our results provide context for interpreting prior findings from related benchmarks.
On CLadder [24], which tests formal causal inference on structured inputs, large LLMs
achieve accuracy above 70% on associational (Rung 1) queries but below 50% on
counterfactual (Rung 3) queries. Our free-text evaluation corroborates this finding:
the counterfactual task type yields the lowest CAR (0.377), confirming that Rung 3
reasoning is consistently the most challenging regardless of evaluation format.
    On COPA [25], which tests commonsense causal reasoning in a two-alternative
forced-choice format, recent LLMs achieve above 90% accuracy. The much lower
performance on CausalReasonBench (mean CAR = 0.41) highlights a critical
distinction: recognition-based evaluation (selecting from alternatives) substantially
overestimates causal reasoning capability compared to generation-based evaluation
(producing free-text explanations). This finding has important implications for bench-
mark design: causal reasoning benchmarks should require generation to avoid inflated
performance estimates.
    On CRASS [14], which specifically targets counterfactual reasoning, models show
particular difficulty with temporal consistency—maintaining a coherent timeline in
counterfactual scenarios. Our CCR metric captures a related phenomenon: counterfac-
tual condition tokens are frequently omitted (CCR = 0.301 for counterfactual tasks),
suggesting that models fail to fully engage with the hypothetical scenario.

8.3 Relationship Between Causal Vocabulary and Causal
    Understanding
An important finding from our error analysis is the dissociation between causal vocab-
ulary usage and genuine causal understanding. Models frequently produce responses
that use sophisticated causal language (“mechanism”, “underlying cause”, “causal
pathway”) without correctly identifying the actual causal structure. This supports the
“causal parrots” hypothesis of Zečević et al. [11]: LLMs can reproduce causal language
fluently without necessarily understanding the causal structures they describe.
    However, our results also provide a more nuanced picture than pure “parroting.”
The Socratic prompting results show that models can improve their causal reasoning


                                          27
when given appropriate structure. If models were purely mimicking causal language,
the prompting strategy should not affect the quality of causal reasoning. The fact that
Socratic prompting consistently improves performance—particularly on adversarial
scenarios where mere language mimicry would be insufficient—suggests that some
form of causal reasoning capability exists within these models, even if it is fragile and
sensitive to elicitation method.

8.4 The Role of Causal Reasoning in LLM Safety
Our adversarial results have implications for LLM safety that extend beyond the
specific domain of causal reasoning benchmarking. The systematic tendency to con-
fuse correlation with causation is a form of reasoning error that can propagate into
consequential decisions when LLMs are used as components of larger systems.
    Consider a healthcare LLM that observes a correlation between a medication and a
side effect in its training data. If the model cannot distinguish whether this correlation
reflects a causal relationship or a confounding effect (e.g., the medication is more
commonly prescribed to patients who already have the condition associated with the
side effect), it may generate incorrect safety warnings or, worse, fail to warn about
genuine causal risks.
    The finding that Socratic prompting reduces adversarial CLP=0 rates from
38% to 18% suggests a practical safety intervention: deploying LLMs with built-in
confound-checking prompts can substantially reduce the rate of causal misattribution.
This “causal safety prompt” approach could be incorporated into LLM deployment
pipelines for safety-critical applications.

8.5 The Correlation-Causation Challenge
Our adversarial results (Table 7) demonstrate that all tested models systematically
confuse correlation with causation, even in well-known scenarios like the ice cream-
drowning example that appears frequently in statistics textbooks. This finding has
significant implications for LLM deployment:
• In clinical settings, a model that confuses correlation with causation could recom-
  mend treatments based on spurious associations in observational data, potentially
  causing patient harm [17].
• In policy settings, causal over-attribution could lead to ineffective or counterpro-
  ductive policy interventions based on confounded associations.
• In engineering settings, incorrect root-cause analysis could lead to applying
  fixes that address symptoms rather than causes, leaving the underlying problem
  unresolved.
   The fact that adversarial CAR (0.332) is significantly below complex CAR (0.411)
despite both requiring multi-step reasoning suggests that the challenge is specifically
in recognising and resisting spurious correlational patterns rather than in general
reasoning complexity.




                                           28
8.6 Domain-Specific Knowledge Gaps
The significant domain effects (Table 5) reveal that LLM causal reasoning is not a
domain-general capability but depends heavily on domain-specific knowledge. The
biological domain’s low performance and high variance suggest that:
1. Biological causal mechanisms (enzymatic pathways, ecological interactions, phar-
   macokinetics) are less well represented in general pre-training data compared to
   physical mechanisms.
2. Biological causation involves greater inherent uncertainty (stochastic processes,
   individual variation) that may interact with model uncertainty in ways that
   compound errors.
3. The vocabulary of biological causation is more specialised and less likely to overlap
   with everyday causal language that appears frequently in training data.
   These findings suggest that domain-specific fine-tuning or retrieval-augmented gen-
eration may be necessary for reliable causal reasoning in specialised domains, rather
than relying solely on general-purpose LLMs.

8.7 Implications for LLM Deployment
Based on our findings, we make the following recommendations for deploying LLMs
in causal reasoning tasks:
1. Use Socratic prompting for counterfactual queries: The consistent advan-
   tage of Socratic prompting, particularly on counterfactual tasks, suggests that
   requiring models to decompose causal questions before answering is a simple and
   effective intervention.
2. Implement adversarial causal screening: Before deploying an LLM in a
   domain where spurious correlations are common (epidemiology, economics, social
   science), evaluate it on domain-specific adversarial scenarios to quantify its
   susceptibility to causal over-attribution.
3. Use task-adaptive prompting: Since no single prompting strategy dominates
   all task types, deployment systems should select the prompting strategy based on
   the type of causal question being asked (Socratic for counterfactual, contrast-cases
   for effect prediction).
4. Calibrate expectations by domain: Physical and technological domains sup-
   port more reliable causal reasoning than biological and social domains. Extra
   validation should be applied when LLMs are used for causal reasoning in the latter
   domains.

8.8 Limitations
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our results:
1. Automatic metrics: Our metrics (CIR, CLP, CCR) are based on keyword match-
   ing and pattern detection, which cannot capture all aspects of causal reasoning
   quality. A response that mentions the correct causal keywords in an incorrect logical
   structure would receive high CIR but should ideally receive a lower score. The pilot


                                          29
   study correlation with expert ratings (Kendall’s 𝜏 = 0.78) provides some validation,
   but more comprehensive human evaluation would strengthen our findings.
2. Gold annotation: Causal keyword sets and CLP patterns are author-defined
   based on textbook sources. While we followed a systematic annotation process,
   broader crowdsourced annotation or expert panel review would strengthen the gold
   standard’s validity.
3. Model selection: We evaluate three open LLMs via the Groq API. Closed-source
   models (GPT-4, Claude) are not included due to API cost constraints and the goal
   of evaluating reproducible, open-weight models. Results may differ for larger or
   more capable models.
4. Modality: CausalReasonBench tests causal reasoning in text. Causal reasoning
   from data, images, graphs, or structured tables is out of scope. Real-world causal
   reasoning often requires integrating evidence from multiple modalities.
5. Zero-shot evaluation: All evaluations are zero-shot. Few-shot evaluation with
   causal demonstrations might significantly alter the relative performance of strate-
   gies and models.
6. Scenario coverage: Twelve scenarios provide systematic coverage but cannot rep-
   resent the full diversity of causal reasoning challenges in each domain. Additional
   scenarios, particularly in under-represented sub-domains, would improve coverage.

8.9 Threats to Validity
Internal validity.
The primary threat to internal validity is the potential for data contamination: if
specific adversarial scenarios (e.g., the ice cream-drowning example) appear in model
training data, performance on these scenarios may not reflect genuine causal reason-
ing. We mitigate this by including novel adversarial scenarios alongside well-known
examples and by testing with multiple models trained on different data.

External validity.
Our results are limited to three model families and may not generalise to all LLMs. The
Groq API’s inference characteristics (e.g., potential differences in precision compared
to local inference) could also affect results, though temperature-0 generation should
minimise stochastic effects.

Construct validity.
The CAR composite score weights three metrics with fixed coefficients determined by
a pilot study. Different weighting schemes could lead to different conclusions about
which prompting strategy is best. We report individual metric scores alongside CAR
to enable alternative aggregation.

9 Conclusion
We presented CausalReasonBench, a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating
LLM causal reasoning across four domains, four task types, three difficulty levels,


                                          30
and four prompting strategies. Three automatic metrics (CIR, CLP, CCR) and the
CAR composite provide a multi-dimensional assessment of causal reasoning quality
reproducible without human annotation.

9.1 Summary of Key Findings
Our key findings are:
1. Socratic prompting superiority: Socratic prompting achieves the highest CAR
   across all models and domains, with particularly strong gains on counterfac-
   tual tasks (+0.09 over CoT). The structured sub-question format helps models
   decompose causal problems and explicitly check for confounders.
2. Adversarial vulnerability: All models systematically over-attribute causation in
   adversarial scenarios, with correlation-as-causation error rates exceeding 40% under
   direct prompting. This represents a fundamental limitation for LLM deployment
   in expert causal reasoning.
3. Domain-specific knowledge gaps: Biological and social domains challenge
   LLMs significantly more than physical and technological domains. The biological
   domain shows the highest inter-model variance, suggesting that domain-specific
   pre-training data composition is a key determinant of causal reasoning capability.
4. Task-strategy interactions: No single prompting strategy dominates all task
   types. Socratic prompting is best for counterfactual reasoning, while contrast-cases
   is best for effect prediction. This suggests that optimal deployment should use
   task-adaptive prompting.
5. Scale and family effects: Model scale improves causal reasoning but does not
   proportionally improve adversarial robustness. Cross-family comparisons (LLaMA
   vs. Qwen) reveal that pre-training data composition can compensate for smaller
   scale in domain-specific causal reasoning.

9.2 Future Work
We plan to extend CausalReasonBench in several directions:
1. Domain expansion: Adding 10 additional domains including medical causa-
   tion (clinical trials, drug interactions), legal causation (proximate cause, but-for
   test), environmental causation (climate change, pollution), and economic causation
   (monetary policy, market mechanisms).
2. Few-shot evaluation: Evaluating the effect of few-shot causal demonstrations
   within each prompting strategy, potentially using curated examples that illustrate
   correct causal reasoning patterns.
3. Metric refinement: Developing CIR extensions using causal graph alignment
   rather than keyword overlap, enabling evaluation of causal structure rather than
   just causal vocabulary. We also plan to validate metrics against larger-scale expert
   annotations.
4. Multimodal extension: Extending the benchmark to include causal reasoning
   from data tables, causal graphs, time series, and images (e.g., interpreting causal
   mechanisms from diagrams).


                                          31
5. Fine-tuning analysis: Investigating whether causal reasoning capabilities can
   be improved through targeted fine-tuning on causal datasets, and whether such
   improvements transfer across domains.
6. Closed-source model evaluation: Extending evaluation to include state-of-the-
   art closed-source models (GPT-4, Claude) to provide a more complete picture of
   LLM causal reasoning capabilities across the model landscape.

9.3 Practical Implications
Expert systems deploying LLMs in causal decision support (e.g., clinical diagnosis
support, root-cause analysis, policy evaluation) should:
• Prefer Socratic prompting for counterfactual queries and effect prediction tasks;
• Implement mandatory adversarial causal screening before deployment in domains
  where spurious correlations are common (epidemiology, economics, social science);
• Provide domain-specific causal knowledge (through retrieval augmentation or fine-
  tuning) for biological and social domains where general-purpose causal reasoning is
  weakest;
• Use task-adaptive prompting that selects the strategy based on the type of causal
  question being asked.

Appendix F              Annotation Guidelines
This appendix describes the annotation guidelines used to construct the gold standard
for CausalReasonBench.



                                                    43
F.1    Keyword Selection Criteria
For each scenario-task pair, gold keywords were selected according to the following
criteria:
1. Causal core: Include the primary causal concept (e.g., “conduction”, “con-
   founder”) and its domain-specific synonyms.
2. Mechanism terms: Include terms describing the causal mechanism (e.g., “lattice
   vibration”, “free electron”).
3. Condition terms: Include terms describing necessary or sufficient conditions (e.g.,
   “direct contact”, “temperature gradient”).
4. Exclusion terms: For adversarial scenarios, include terms that indicate correct
   rejection of spurious causation (e.g., “not causal”, “spurious”, “confounder”).
5. Morphological variants: Include common inflected forms (e.g., “conducts”,
   “conducting”, “conduction”).
   Each keyword set contains 8–15 tokens. Keywords are weighted equally; future
work will explore importance-weighted keywords.

F.2    CLP Pattern Construction
Directional patterns were constructed by:
1. Identifying the gold cause (𝐶) and effect (𝐸) for each scenario-task pair.
2. Generating forward patterns: “𝐶 causes 𝐸”, “𝐶 leads to 𝐸”, “𝐶 results in 𝐸”, “𝐸
   is caused by 𝐶”, “𝐸 is due to 𝐶”, “because of 𝐶, 𝐸”.
3. Generating reverse patterns: the same templates with 𝐶 and 𝐸 swapped.
4. Generating ambiguous patterns: “𝐶 is associated with 𝐸”, “𝐶 and 𝐸 are related”,
   “𝐶 correlates with 𝐸”.
5. Testing patterns on 5 pilot responses per scenario to verify coverage and accuracy.
    The pattern set contains 47 forward templates, 32 reverse templates, and 21
ambiguous templates, with cause/effect slots filled per scenario. Matching allows up to
5 intervening words between template components to accommodate natural language
variation.

F.3    Counterfactual Condition Annotation
For counterfactual tasks, the gold counterfactual condition was annotated by:
1. Specifying the counterfactual intervention (e.g., “minimum wage NOT raised”).
2. Specifying the expected counterfactual outcome (e.g., “youth employment remains
   unchanged or slightly increases”).
3. Extracting 5–10 tokens from both the intervention and outcome descriptions.

F.4    Quality Control
All annotations were validated through the following process:




                                          44
1. Source verification: Each gold causal structure was verified against at least two
   independent authoritative sources (textbooks, peer-reviewed papers, or established
   scientific references).
2. Internal consistency: Each annotation set was checked for internal consistency
   (no contradictions between CIR keywords, CLP patterns, and CCR conditions for
   the same scenario).
3. Coverage test: Each annotation set was tested against 5 pilot responses to ver-
   ify that correct responses achieve high scores and incorrect responses achieve low
   scores.
4. Boundary cases: Ambiguous or borderline cases were documented and resolved
   by consulting domain-specific literature.
