85: The model makes the following structural equation assumptions on the data generating process.
86: 
87: .. math::
88: 
89:     Y =~& \theta(X) \cdot T + g(X, W) + \epsilon ~~~&~~~ \E[\epsilon | X, W] = 0 \\ 
90:     T =~& f(X, W) + \eta & \E[\eta \mid X, W] = 0 \\
91:     ~& \E[\eta \cdot \epsilon | X, W] = 0
92: 
93: What is particularly attractive about DML is that it makes no further structural assumptions on :math:`g` and :math:`f` and estimates them 
94: non-parametrically using arbitrary non-parametric Machine Learning methods. Our goal is to estimate
95: the constant marginal CATE :math:`\theta(X)`.
96: 
97: The idea to estimate :math:`\theta(X)` is as follows: we can re-write the structural equations as
98: 
99: .. math::
100: 
101:     Y - \E[Y | X, W]
102:     = \theta(X) \cdot (T - \E[T | X, W]) + \epsilon
103: 
104: Thus if one can estimate the conditional expectation functions (both of which are non-parametric regression tasks):
105: 
106: .. math::
107: 
108:     q(X, W) =~& \E[Y | X, W]\\
109:     f(X, W) =~& \E[T | X, W]
110: 
111: Then we can compute the residuals:
112: 
113: .. math::
114: 
115:     \tilde{Y} =~& Y - q(X, W)\\
116:     \tilde{T} =~& T - f(X, W) = \eta
117: 
118: which are subsequently related by the equation:
119: 
120: .. math::
121: 
122:     \tilde{Y} = \theta(X) \cdot \tilde{T} + \epsilon
123: 
124: Subsequently, since :math:`\E[\epsilon \cdot \eta | X]=0`, estimating :math:`\theta(X)` is a final regression problem, regressing :math:`\tilde{Y}` on :math:`X, \tilde{T}` (albeit over models that are linear in :math:`\tilde{T}`), i.e.
125: 
126: .. math::
127:     :nowrap:
128: 
129:     \begin{equation}
130:     \hat{\theta} = \arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta} \E_n\left[ (\tilde{Y} - \theta(X)\cdot \tilde{T})^2 \right]
131:     \end{equation}
132: 
133: This approach has been analyzed in multiple papers in the literature, for different model classes :math:`\Theta`.
134: [Chernozhukov2016]_ consider the case where :math:`\theta(X)` is a constant (average treatment effect) or a low dimensional
135: linear function,
136: [Nie2017]_ consider the case where :math:`\theta(X)` falls in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS),
137: [Chernozhukov2017]_, [Chernozhukov2018]_ consider the case of a high dimensional sparse linear space, where :math:`\theta(X)=\langle \theta, \phi(X)\rangle`
138: for some known high-dimensional feature mapping and where :math:`\theta_0` has very few non-zero entries (sparse), 
139: [Athey2019]_ (among other results) consider the case where :math:`\theta(X)` is a non-parametric lipschitz function and 
140: use random forest models to fit the function, [Foster2019]_ allow for arbitrary models :math:`\theta(X)` and give 
141: results based on sample complexity measures of the model space (e.g. Rademacher complexity, metric entropy).
142: 
143: 
144: The main advantage of DML is that if one makes parametric assumptions on :math:`\theta(X)`, then one achieves fast estimation rates and, 
145: for many cases of final stage estimators, also asymptotic normality on the second stage estimate :math:`\hat{\theta}`, even if the first stage estimates on :math:`q(X, W)` 
146: and :math:`f(X, W)` are only :math:`n^{1/4}` consistent, in terms of RMSE. For this theorem to hold, the nuisance
147: estimates need to be fitted in a cross-fitting manner (see :class:`._OrthoLearner`).
148: The latter robustness property follows from the fact that the moment equations that correspond to the final 
149: least squares estimation (i.e. the gradient of the squared loss), satisfy a Neyman orthogonality condition with respect to the
150: nuisance parameters :math:`q, f`. For a more detailed exposition of how Neyman orthogonality 
151: leads to robustness we refer the reader to [Chernozhukov2016]_, [Mackey2017]_, [Nie2017]_, [Chernozhukov2017]_,
