                                     Abstract

Causal inference provides essential tools for understanding how interventions influence
outcomes, supporting evidence-based decision-making across disciplines such as medicine,
economics, and the social sciences. At the same time, large language models (LLMs) have
rapidly advanced, offering capabilities in text generation, coding, and knowledge synthesis.
This thesis examines the overlap of these two areas by looking at the uses of LLMs in causal
inference. The systematic review was based on PRISMA principles and included the 2017 to
2024 publications in Scopus, arXiv, PubMed, and the ACL Anthology. Eighty studies were
enclosed and examined under four classifications namely causal discovery, extracting cause-
effect relations, experimental design support, and causal code generation. A GPT-based
assistant prototype was also created in order to test systematic review workflows with the help
of AI. The results suggest that LLMs might facilitate applied researchers, making it faster to
do things like DAG generation, hypothesis generation, and apply causal inference libraries.
Nonetheless, interpretability, robustness, generalization, and reproducibility is still a
challenge. The assistant enhanced the efficiency in screening and synthesis although it also
brought to the fore risks of model dependency and human bias. All in all, the research
provides the answer: LLMs can be a strong tool to use as an augmented research assistant in
the process of causal analysis, but only when their claims are justified by transparent and
reproducible practice.



Keywords: Large language models, causal inference, DAGs, systematic review,
interpretability, reproducibility

Chapter 1: Introduction
Over the last several decades, artificial intelligence (AI) has undergone radical changes,
shifting away from specific, task-focused systems to general-purpose models that can think,
create words, and engage with human beings in ways that are more complex and less singular.
Two paradigms have become especially prominent in this larger context: the theoretical and
methodological basis of reasoning about cause and effect relationships, causal inference, and
large language models (LLMs), which came to be considered components of modern AI
systems (Kaplan et al., 2020).

The intersections of the two domains provoke the underlying concerns regarding the future of
scientific discovery, decision-making, and explainable AI. In particular, the emergence of
LLMs like GPT-4, Claude, and LLaMA has led to the question of whether they are simply
powerful statistical pattern recognizers or can make contributions to the serious field of causal
reasoning (Vaswani et al., 2017). This thesis attempts to answer these questions by studying
how LLMs are used in causal inference as a way of assisting human scientists in the discovery
of causation and as a subject of causal study, which similarly needs questioning in terms of
fairness, bias, and interpretability.

The present introduction situates the research within its wider academic and technological
context. It first outlines the general context of causal inference in AI (Section 1.1), before
turning to the emergence of LLMs and their disruptive impact on the field (Section 1.2). The
central research problem is then articulated: can LLMs reason causally or, at a minimum,
assist those who do? (Section 1.3). Subsequently, the general and specific objectives of the
thesis are detailed (Section 1.4), and finally, the structure of the report is described (Section
1.5).

1.1 General context of causal inference in AI

The ability to understand and manipulate cause–and–effect relationships lies at the heart of
human intelligence. From a young age, humans learn that pressing a switch turns on a light or
that administering a drug can cure an illness. These intuitive forms of causal reasoning are
foundational to science, medicine, economics, and engineering. AI, however, has traditionally

                                                                                              11
struggled with causality(Brown et al., 2020). Classical machine learning systems are
optimized to identify correlations in data, which may suffice for prediction but not for
explanation or intervention.

Causal inference is the discipline of formalising and operationalising cause-and-effect
reasoning. Based on the pioneering contributions by statisticians and philosophers, especially
the scheme of causal diagrams and structural causal models (SCMs) by Judea Pearl, the field
has formulated several rigorous methods to make the distinction between correlation and
causation (Pearl, 2009). These include directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), which represent
assumptions about causal structure; do-calculus, which represents interventions; and
counterfactual reasoning, which allows an assessment of alternative scenarios.           Causal
inference has, over the past few years, become more and more part of AI research. As an
example, causal models are currently used in healthcare to assign a treatment effect, in
economics to assess an intervention in policy, and in computer vision to provide domain
generalization. Furthermore, causal inference is suggested to address part of the deficiencies
of deep learning, such as brittleness, non-interpretability, and susceptibility to spurious
correlation (Schölkopf et al., 2021).

In spite of these developments, it is not yet possible to use causal inference as mainstream AI.
Causal effects need assumptions that are frequently unverifiable; the observational data are in
most cases, inadequate to determine causation and data-only causal discovery is a widely
unsolved problem (Schölkopf et al., 2021). Such difficulties encourage the investigation of
new instruments, such as the potential of LLMs, which could play a role in causal inference
by making use of language comprehension, knowledge distillation, and human-AI
cooperation.

1.2 Emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs)

In line with the development of causal inference, large-scale language modeling has also
experienced a revolution in AI. Since their introduction, which started with transformer
architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017), and has been expanded to massive datasets and a number
of parameters, LLMs demonstrated unprecedented performance in natural language
understanding, generation, and reasoning tasks. Models such as GPT-3, GPT-4 (OpenAI),
Claude (Anthropic), PaLM (Google DeepMind), and LLaMA (Meta) represent a new

                                                                                             12
generation of AI systems capable of producing coherent text, engaging in dialogue, and even
generating code (Campesato, 2024).

The capabilities of LLMs extend beyond traditional NLP. They have demonstrated emergent
properties such as few-shot learning, chain-of-thought reasoning, and instruction
following, which were not explicitly programmed but arose from scale and training dynamics
(Zhang et al., 2025). Their versatility has made them central to research, industry, and public
discourse, raising both excitement and concern.

From the perspective of scientific inquiry, LLMs are intriguing because they encode vast
amounts of textual knowledge, including descriptions of scientific theories, experimental
methods, and empirical results. This raises the possibility that LLMs might assist researchers
in tasks traditionally associated with expertise and domain knowledge—such as formulating
hypotheses, designing experiments, and identifying causal mechanisms(Kulkarni et al., 2025).

At the same time, critics argue that LLMs are fundamentally statistical learners of surface
regularities. They lack grounding in physical reality, causal understanding of interventions, or
stable reasoning across contexts. As such, their application to causal inference remains
controversial: are LLMs potential collaborators in causal reasoning, or are they sophisticated
mimics whose outputs risk misleading researchers?



1.3 Problem: Can LLMs reason causally or assist those who do?

This thesis addresses the central problem of whether LLMs can play a meaningful role in
causal inference.

The central research question, therefore, can be stated as follows:

To what extent can LLMs perform or support causal inference tasks, and what are the
implications of applying causal reasoning methods to LLMs themselves? (Kiciman et al.,
2023)




                                                                                             13
The problem can be formulated along two dimensions:

   1. RQ1.              LLMs           as        tools         for        causal         inference.
          LLMs might be used to extract cause–and–effect relationships from text, to assist
          in the generation of causal diagrams (DAGs), or to support systematic reviews by
          classifying and synthesizing causal evidence(Kiciman et al., 2023). For example,
          LLMs can parse biomedical literature to suggest potential causal links between genes
          and diseases. They might also help non-expert users articulate causal questions in
          natural language and translate them into formal queries suitable for statistical analysis.

   2. RQ2.              LLMs           as        objects         of        causal          analysis.
          Equally important is the question of how LLMs themselves should be analyzed using
          causal inference. Their training data may embed biases, leading to discriminatory
          outcomes; their decision processes may lack interpretability; and their fairness may
          depend on causal factors such as protected attributes(Kiciman et al., 2023). Here,
          causal inference provides tools to assess and mitigate these issues—for example, by
          modeling counterfactual fairness or tracing the causal pathways of bias propagation.

This dual perspective acknowledges both the potential utility of LLMs as assistants in causal
reasoning and the necessity of scrutinizing their own outputs and biases through causal
lenses.

Chapter 2: Theoretical framework and state of the art
The intersection between causal inference and large language models (LLMs) represents one
of the most dynamic frontiers of contemporary AI research. To evaluate the potential of LLMs
in advancing causal reasoning, it is first necessary to ground the discussion in the theoretical
principles of causality, then to examine the evolution of LLMs, and finally to analyze the
current body of work that connects these two domains(Kiciman et al., 2023).

This chapter provides the conceptual background and situates the thesis within the state of the
art. Section 2.1 introduces the fundamentals of causal inference, including directed acyclic
graphs (DAGs), counterfactuals, and structural causal models. Section 2.2 reviews the
historical development of LLMs, from early statistical language models to transformer-based
systems such as GPT and LLaMA. Section 2.3 examines LLMs as tools in causality, focusing
on their ability to extract cause–and–effect relationships, generate DAGs, and assist in
experimental design. Section 2.4 considers LLMs as objects of causal analysis, with attention
to fairness, bias, and interpretability. Section 2.5 surveys benchmarks and metrics used to
evaluate LLMs in causal tasks. Section 2.6 concludes with a discussion of the open challenges
and research gaps.

2.1 Fundamentals of causal inference
2.1.1 Historical background

The scientific endeavor has long sought to distinguish between correlation and causation. As
early as David Hume’s philosophical writings in the 18th century, causality was debated as a
concept of constant conjunction rather than a necessary connection. In the 20th century,
statisticians such as Ronald Fisher advanced experimental design as a means of establishing
causal claims through randomization. Yet, it was not until the formalization of causal
models in statistics and AI—particularly the contributions of Judea Pearl and colleagues—
that causality became operationalized in computational terms (Pearl, 2009).

2.1.2 Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)

At the heart of modern causal inference is the directed acyclic graph (DAG), a graphical
representation of variables (nodes) and directional dependencies (edges) that encode


                                                                                             17
assumptions about the causal structure of a system(Brewer et al., 2017). DAGs allow
researchers to:

   •   Distinguish between confounders, mediators, and colliders.

   •   Identify valid sets of covariates for adjustment to estimate causal effects.

   •   Clarify the conditions under which causal effects are identifiable from observational
       data.

For example, in a DAG describing the relationship between smoking, tar deposits, and lung
cancer, arrows encode the assumption that smoking causes tar buildup, which in turn
increases the risk of lung cancer. DAG-based reasoning provides a transparent formalism for
encoding scientific knowledge and for guiding empirical analysis.




 Figure 1: Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) illustrating the causal relationships among smoking,
                      tar deposits, and lung cancer (Patel et al., 2024).



2.1.3 Counterfactuals

A central pillar of causal inference is counterfactual reasoning, which seeks to answer
questions of the form: What would have happened if, contrary to fact, a different action or
intervention had been taken? Such reasoning moves beyond mere association and allows

                                                                                          18
researchers to probe the effects of alternative scenarios. For example, in medicine, one
might ask: If a patient had received a different treatment, would their recovery outcome have
improved? Unlike average treatment effects, counterfactuals enable the estimation of
individual-level causal effects, making them particularly valuable for personalized decision-
making. (Liu et al., 2025)

Counterfactuals are formally expressed within the structural causal model (SCM)
framework. In SCMs, variables are generated through a set of structural equations, each
influenced by parent variables and stochastic noise terms. By manipulating these equations,
researchers can compute hypothetical outcomes under different interventions(Ma, 2024).
The mathematical tool used for this manipulation is the do-operator, introduced by Judea
Pearl, which allows one to model interventions by replacing the natural value of a variable
with a fixed alternative.

Through this lens, counterfactual reasoning is not limited to retrospective analysis but also
informs policy evaluation, fairness assessments, and risk analysis. For instance, in social
sciences, counterfactuals can simulate the effect of alternative policy decisions, while in
machine learning, they are increasingly applied to evaluate algorithmic fairness by asking
whether an outcome would remain the same if sensitive attributes were altered. Thus,
counterfactuals represent a powerful mechanism for bridging theory with real-world
applications of causal inference.

2.1.4 Structural causal models (SCMs)

Structural causal models unify DAGs and counterfactuals under a single mathematical
framework. SCMs specify how each variable is generated as a function of its parents in the
graph plus an exogenous noise term. This formalism enables three key levels of causal
reasoning, famously described by Pearl’s “causal hierarchy”:



   1. Association: What is correlated with what? (prediction)

   2. Intervention: What happens if we do X? (manipulation)

   3. Counterfactuals: What would have happened if X had been different? (retrospection)


                                                                                          19
Most machine learning models operate at Level 1 (association). True causal inference aspires
to Levels 2 and 3, which are essential for decision-making and scientific explanation.

2.1.5 Applications of causal inference in AI

Causal inference has been applied across multiple AI subfields:

   •   Healthcare: Estimating treatment effects from observational data.

   •   Computer vision: Improving robustness by disentangling causal from spurious
       features.

   •   Reinforcement learning: Modeling interventions in environments.

   •   Fairness in AI: Ensuring that decisions do not rely on protected attributes through
       causal fairness frameworks(Joshi et al., 2024).

These applications highlight why causality is increasingly viewed as a necessary
complement to deep learning: while deep neural networks excel at pattern recognition, they
lack the guarantees needed for explanation, generalization, and reliable decision-making.

2.2 Background on Large Language Models
2.2.1 Early language models

Before the rise of the transformer architecture, language modeling was dominated by
statistical and sequential approaches. The earliest methods, such as n-gram models,
estimated the probability of a word based on the preceding n–1 words(Zhou et al., 2024a).
While simple and computationally efficient, n-gram models suffered from the curse of
dimensionality, requiring vast amounts of data to handle longer contexts and often defaulting
to sparse or approximate representations.

The advent of neural networks marked a significant shift. Recurrent neural networks
(RNNs) introduced the ability to capture sequential dependencies by maintaining a hidden
state that propagated information across time steps. However, RNNs struggled with vanishing
and exploding gradients, making it difficult to learn long-range dependencies. Therefore,
long short-term memory networks (LSTMs) and gated recurrent units (GRUs) were created to
overcome this (Zhou et al., 2024a). These architectures were based on gating that selectively



                                                                                            20
retained or discarded information which enhanced performance significantly in applications
such as machine translation and speech recognition.

Regardless of these developments, early language models were still limited by the capacity to
support very long contexts, as well as computationally infeasible to scale to large scale
training. These limitations paved the way for the development of transformer-based models,
which overcame many of these bottlenecks and revolutionized natural language processing.

2.2.2 Transformer architecture

Vaswani et al. (2017) introduced the transformer architecture that marked a major
breakthrough in the sphere of natural language processing. In contrast to recurrent models,
which compute results by considering each word in a sequence sequentially, transformers use
a self-attention mechanism, in which every word in a sequence directly attends to all other
words at once. This innovation allowed models to explore long-distance dependencies without
the sequential-computation restrictions.

Self-attention can be performed by weighting relationships between words, and this enables
the model to highlight the most important context of a given token. Together with positional
encodings (Kiciman et al., 2023) that maintain order information not observed in pure
attention, the transformer architecture proved to be extremely efficient when dealing with
linguistic patterns of a complex nature.

Parallelization was yet another important benefit. Transformers are more effective at utilising
modern hardware (GPUs/TPUs) than either RNNs or LSTMs because they operate on entire
sequences concurrently. This efficiency supported the training of significantly larger models
on large corpora, which was the catalyst for the rapid scaling that is now known as large
language models (LLMs).

In several years, transformer-based methods outperformed RNN-based methods on NLP
benchmarks, and extended to other tasks like vision, speech, and multimodal learning (Li et
al., 2025). The modern LLMs, such as GPT, BERT, and LLaMA, are now built.

2.2.3 Scaling laws and pretraining

A crucial milestone in the evolution of large language models was the discovery of scaling
laws, formalized by Kaplan et al. (2020). Their work demonstrated that systematically

                                                                                            21
increasing the scale of model parameters, dataset size, and compute resources led to
predictable improvements in performance across a wide range of benchmarks. Importantly,
these improvements followed power-law relationships, meaning that model loss decreased in
a smooth and quantifiable manner as scale increased(Takagi, 2023).

This insight reshaped the research agenda in natural language processing. Instead of designing
highly specialized architectures, the community shifted toward building larger, more
general-purpose models. The result was the emergence of billion- and eventually trillion-
parameter systems, trained on vast corpora of internet text.

Central to this paradigm is pretraining, where a model learns broad linguistic and world
knowledge from large-scale, unlabeled data(Feder et al., 2022). Once pretrained, the model
can be adapted to specific downstream tasks—such as question answering, summarization, or
causal reasoning—through fine-tuning or prompt engineering.

Scaling laws and pretraining together created the foundation for today’s LLMs, showing that
sheer size and exposure to diverse text corpora could yield systems with emergent
capabilities, far beyond what earlier models achieved.

2.2.4 Emergent abilities

One of the most striking outcomes of scaling large language models has been the appearance
of emergent abilities—skills that are absent in smaller models but arise suddenly once a

                                                                                           22
certain scale threshold is crossed. Unlike incremental improvements, these abilities emerge
discontinuously, suggesting that model capacity and training data volume interact in
nonlinear ways.

Research by Wei et al. (2022) and others has documented several such abilities. Among the
most notable is few-shot learning, where a model can generalize to a new task given only a
handful of examples in its prompt, without explicit fine-tuning. Another is chain-of-thought
reasoning, in which models generate intermediate reasoning steps that improve performance
on multi-step problems, particularly in mathematics and logic. A third is instruction-
following, whereby sufficiently large models can adapt flexibly to natural-language
commands, enabling intuitive human–AI interaction.

These emergent behaviors transformed LLMs from narrow predictive engines into versatile
general-purpose tools. They underpin modern applications ranging from conversational
assistants to causal reasoning experiments(Chevalley et al., 2024). However, the mechanisms
driving emergence remain poorly understood, raising questions about predictability, safety,
and alignment as models continue to grow in scale.

2.2.5 Major LLM families

   •   GPT series (OpenAI): From GPT-2 to GPT-4, these autoregressive models
       popularized few-shot prompting.

   •   PaLM (Google DeepMind): A massive multilingual model with strong reasoning
       benchmarks.

   •   Claude (Anthropic): Emphasizing safety and alignment.

   •   LLaMA (Meta): Open-access models enabling wide research use.

   •   Mistral and Mixtral: Smaller but efficient models.

2.2.6 Beyond text

While large language models were originally developed for purely textual tasks, recent
advances have extended their reach into the multimodal domain. Multimodal models
integrate text, images, audio, video, and even code, enabling them to process and reason
across diverse types of information(Jiao et al., 2024). This evolution expands the scope of

                                                                                         23
LLMs from language understanding to cross-modal reasoning, where insights can be drawn
by combining heterogeneous data sources.

For example, models such as GPT-4 with vision or Flamingo can interpret both textual
queries and visual inputs, allowing them to answer questions about images or generate
narratives grounded in visual context. Similarly, multimodal architectures that combine
speech and text unlock possibilities for more natural conversational systems, while the
integration of code enables symbolic reasoning and computational workflows.

This shift is particularly important for causal inference tasks, which often rely on more than
text alone. Complex problems may require reasoning over structured datasets, causal
graphs, or temporal signals in addition to narratives or documentation(Le et al., 2024). By
moving beyond text, LLMs open pathways for richer, more accurate causal analysis and more
sophisticated decision-support systems across domains like medicine, climate science, and
economics.



2.3 LLMs as tools in causality
2.3.1 DAG generation

Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) are a cornerstone of modern causal inference, providing a
visual and mathematical representation of assumed relationships among variables. However,
constructing DAGs typically requires substantial expertise, as researchers must translate often
complex textual descriptions into precise graphical structures(Naik et al., 2024). This step
poses a barrier for non-specialists who wish to engage with causal reasoning.

Large language models (LLMs) offer a potential solution by automatically generating
DAGs from natural-language inputs. For instance, given a passage describing how “exercise
influences weight through diet, while stress also affects both diet and weight,” an LLM could
propose a DAG that encodes these dependencies. Such capabilities lower the entry barrier for
applied researchers, policymakers, and students, enabling them to more readily formalize
causal questions without extensive technical training(Cui et al., 2024).

Early experiments demonstrate both the promise and limitations of this approach. Gao et al.
(2023) show that LLMs can approximate known causal structures, often capturing the general

                                                                                            24
direction of relationships. However, accuracy remains uneven: models sometimes introduce
spurious links, omit key variables, or fail to enforce acyclicity. As a result, human validation
remains essential to ensure correctness.

Despite these challenges, LLM-assisted DAG generation illustrates a promising frontier
where natural-language processing and causal inference intersect. With improved prompting,
domain-specific fine-tuning, and hybrid human–AI workflows, such systems could
significantly streamline the formalization of causal assumptions and broaden access to
causal inference methodologies.

2.3.2 Extraction of cause–effect relationships

A second major application of large language models in causal inference is the automatic
extraction of cause–and–effect relationships from unstructured text(Yang et al., 2022).
Scientific fields such as biomedicine, economics, and climate research produce vast volumes
of literature describing potential causal mechanisms—for example, links between genes and
diseases, the effects of new drugs, or associations between environmental factors and health
outcomes. Manually curating this knowledge is both time-intensive and prone to human
oversight.

LLMs provide a promising alternative by being fine-tuned for causal relation extraction
tasks, where the objective is to identify whether one entity (the cause) influences another (the
effect)(Chen et al., 2024). Unlike classical information extraction approaches, which often
rely on rigid syntactic patterns or domain-specific ontologies, LLMs demonstrate greater
flexibility in interpreting varied linguistic structures, ambiguous phrasing, and cross-domain
terminology. For example, an LLM may infer that “exposure to air pollution raises the
possibility of asthma” encodes a cause–and–effect relation, even when expressed in indirect
or nuanced language.

In spite of this development, there are still issues. LLMs can confuse causation with
correlation, or hallucinate causal connections, or misread nuances like may or is associated
with. Additionally, the emerging causal relationship that is obtained by mining heterogeneous
domains must be adaptively and validly adjusted. Nonetheless, in combination with
knowledge graphs and human supervision, the LLM-based extraction can provide a scalable
way of deriving causal knowledge out of the constantly growing amount of scientific papers.

                                                                                             25
2.3.3 Support for experimental design

A second potential application of large language models to causal inference is to help
researchers with experimental design. The development of rigorous experiments involves
giving close attention to specification of the hypothesis, identification of the variables of
interest, and identification of confounders. Such procedures are cognitively challenging and
time-consuming, especially in interdisciplinary research, in which subject matter knowledge
needs to be combined with statistical rigor(Ban et al., 2025).

LLM can make this process simpler by generating hypothesis alternatives based on problems
as described in natural language, proposing possible control variables to reduce confounding
and even suggesting randomization or statistical analysis solutions. As an example, a research
question like: Does dietary intervention improve cognitive function in older adults? An LLM
may suggest that age, education level and baseline health status be controlled. It might also
provide potential study designs like randomized controlled trials or longitudinal observational
studies, and it may also call out methodological trade-offs (Chevalley et al., 2024).

Critically, the purpose of the use of LLMs in the design of experiments is not to substitute
formal causal inference techniques or expert judgment, but instead complement human
decision-making. The fact that they can quickly synthesize background literature, and point to
potential sources of bias can help speed up workflows and increase access to advanced
research practices.

However, one should be careful. LLM recommendations can be lacking domain specificity,
can miss important confounders, or can suggest untenable design. In this way, their products
ought to be viewed as decision-support systems that must be evaluated by human experts
prior to being used.

2.3.4 Causal code generation

Another potential useful area of large language models is causal code generation. The current
LLMs, like GPT-4, CodeLLaMA, and Claude, have shown high-quality coding abilities,
producing working code snippets in a broad range of different programming languages and
libraries (Cui et al., 2024). In causal inference, this capability can be steered at the creation of



                                                                                                 26
scripts that execute functions like causal discovery, effect estimation, counterfactual
simulation and sensitivity analysis.

As an example, an LLM might be asked by a researcher who does not know about the specific
causal library to write Python code to estimate the average treatment effect of a binary
intervention with DoWhy library. To this end, the model can produce a runnable script, which
contains both data preprocessing and model specification, and effect estimation. On the same
note, prompts can be configured to harness CausalML, EconML or other toolkits, which has
the effect of reducing the barrier to entry among applied researchers and students (Jiao et al.,
2024).

Such a capability can democratize the use of more sophisticated methods of causal inference
by lessening the expertise in coding required to use the method. It is also capable of speeding
up the prototyping process whereby, with the help of experienced researchers, they can
rapidly transition through the conceptual design to implementation.

However, challenges remain. Syntactically correct but methodologically unsound generated
code can be generated, or missing assumptions can be omitted or be using default parameters
inappropriate to the problem at hand. Causal code generation should, therefore, be regarded as
productivity support as opposed to an independent solution and must be reviewed and adapted
by experts prior to practical implementation.



2.4 LLMs as objects of causal analysis
2.4.1 Fairness and bias

Massive text corpus with social bias is used to train LLLMs. Causal inference offers the
means to examine how model outputs are unfairly based on the protected attributes (e.g.
gender, race). The framework of counterfactual fairness (Kusner et al., 2017) poses the
question of whether a model prediction of an individual would be altered in the event that the
protected attribute is counterfactually different and that other factors remain the same.




                                                                                             27
2.4.2 Interpretability

Conceptualizing the inner workings of large language models continues to be one of the key
tasks in artificial intelligence (Le et al., 2024). Although these systems demonstrate
outstanding performance, their decision-making procedures frequently seem to be opaque,
which raises the questions of trust, accountability, and safety. Causal inference provides a
useful set of tools to enhance interpretability by shifting away from correlation-based analysis
to explanations exposing mechanistic pathways in models.

Causal mediation analysis has also recently been used to trace the propagation of individual
inputs, e.g. tokens or phrases, through the network to determine the final outputs. The
researchers can manipulate the hidden states or selectively ablate attention heads to test the
causal responsibility of some of the components in a specific function. As an illustration, the
relationship between a pronoun in the input and the resolution of the antecedent in the output
can have its causal mediation by an attention head(Naik et al., 2024). Such approaches imply
that understanding ways to apply the causal methods to the black-box models to make them
more transparent systems where explanations are based on real mechanisms but not on
descriptive relationships. Nonetheless, interpretability is still a long way to go: causal probes
can be too simplistic in understanding network dynamics, and results do not tend to be
generalizable across architectures. Nevertheless, the incorporation of the use of causal tools
into interpretability studies offers an orderly approach to discovering the process,
transformation, and generation of information by the LLMs.

2.4.3 Robustness and generalization

The notions of robustness and generalization are essential to large language models,
especially when they are used in a wide range of tasks and part of the world. A worrying
environmental issue is that LLC can potentially be trained to trust spurious correlations in
training data patterns that perform well in a given dataset, but poorly when the environment
changes. This overfitting compromises reliability and may cause biased or fragile behaviour
in the real world.

Causal analysis provides a valuable framework for addressing this issue. By disentangling
genuine causal signals from dataset artifacts, researchers can better understand why models
succeed in some contexts and fail in others. For instance, causal interventions on input
                                                                                              28
features allow one to test whether a model’s predictions depend on invariant properties of the
task or on superficial statistical cues. This aligns closely with the goals of causal
representation learning, which aims to construct internal representations that capture stable
causal factors rather than transient correlations.

In practice, applying these methods to LLMs could improve their cross-domain
generalization, making them more resilient to distribution shifts, adversarial inputs, or noisy
data(Yang et al., 2022). While robust causal representations remain an open research
challenge, integrating causal principles into training and evaluation offers a systematic path
toward models that are both more reliable and more trustworthy across applications.

2.5 Current benchmarks and metrics

Evaluating LLMs on causal reasoning requires specialized benchmarks. Existing ones
include:

   •   BIG-bench (Beyond the Imitation Game): A large-scale benchmark with some
       causal reasoning tasks.

   •   CausalBench: Tasks specifically designed to evaluate causal discovery and reasoning
       capabilities.

   •   Synthetic counterfactual datasets: Designed to test models’ ability to reason under
       interventions.

   •   Domain-specific datasets: Biomedical causal relation extraction corpora, policy
       evaluation datasets, etc.

Metrics vary depending on the task: accuracy of causal relation extraction, structural
similarity of predicted DAGs, or performance in estimating treatment effects. Yet no single
benchmark provides comprehensive coverage, and most remain limited compared to human
causal reasoning abilities.

The basis of any rigorous academic inquiry is methodology. The methodological approach in
this thesis was created to investigate the use of large language models (LLMs) in causal
inference, both as objects of causal analysis and as instruments for causal reasoning. The
PRISMA procedure (Page et al., 2021) was used in conjunction with a systematic review
approach (Kitchenham, 2004) because the research issues span theoretical, empirical, and
applied domains. The work type (Section 3.1), the PRISMA protocol design (Section 3.2), the

3.1 Type of work: systematic review assisted by AI
3.1.1 Rationale for systematic review

Systematic reviews are widely used in disciplines such as medicine, social science, and
computer science to provide a comprehensive and reproducible synthesis of available
evidence on a given topic. Unlike traditional narrative reviews, systematic reviews follow
explicit protocols that minimize bias and ensure that findings reflect the totality of relevant
literature.

Given the rapid and fragmented growth of research at the intersection of causal inference and
LLMs, a systematic review was the most appropriate strategy for this thesis(Rethlefsen et al.,
2021). Traditional reviews risk missing crucial studies, while systematic methods ensure
coverage across databases, standardized inclusion criteria, and transparent procedures for
screening and data extraction.




                                                                                               31
3.1.2 Role of AI assistance

While systematic reviews are rigorous, they are also resource-intensive. Screening thousands
of papers for relevance is time-consuming and error-prone. For this reason, the methodology
integrated AI-assisted tools—including GPT-4 for text classification and ASReview for
semi-automated screening(Marshall & Wallace, 2019). This hybrid approach not only
accelerates the process but also allows for an empirical evaluation of how LLMs themselves
can support systematic review workflows.

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)

3.2.1 PRISMA stages

PRISMA outlines four key stages:

   1. Identification: Gathering records from selected databases.

   2. Screening: Removing duplicates and filtering titles/abstracts for relevance.

   3. Eligibility: Reviewing full texts against inclusion/exclusion criteria.

   4. Inclusion: Finalizing the set of studies included in synthesis.

3.2.2 Application to this study

For this thesis, the PRISMA framework was adapted as follows(Rethlefsen et al., 2021):

   •   Identification: Search queries designed around terms related to causal inference and
       LLMs.

   •   Screening: Titles and abstracts reviewed with human–AI collaboration.

   •   Eligibility: Full texts assessed for methodological rigor and relevance.

   •   Inclusion: Final dataset compiled for data extraction and synthesis.

Chapter 5: Development and Evaluation of a GPT-Based
Assistant for Systematic Reviews in Causal Inference


The article extraction process followed a systematic and transparent workflow. Searches were
conducted across Scopus, PubMed, arXiv, and the ACL Anthology using combinations of
keywords such as “large language models”, “causal inference”, “causal reasoning”, and
“counterfactuals”.

Duplicate records were removed using Zotero reference manager. Screening of titles and
abstracts was then carried out using ASReview (Marshall & Wallace, 2019), a semi-
automated review tool that applies active learning to prioritize the most relevant papers for
review.

To enhance efficiency and reduce human error, GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2023) was employed as an
assistant for text classification. Specifically, GPT-4 was used to classify abstracts against the
predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. This step was always subject to human
verification, ensuring that AI-assisted decisions did not bias the final selection.

Final data cleaning, tabulation, and visualization of the PRISMA flowchart were conducted in
Python (Pandas, Matplotlib), while screening logs were maintained in Excel for
transparency.

This hybrid approach ensured that both traditional systematic review rigor and AI-assisted
innovation were integrated into the methodology.

5.1 GPT Assistant Prototype for Systematic Review

The practical component of this thesis involved developing a GPT-based assistant to support
the process of conducting systematic reviews in causal inference. The motivation behind this
prototype was threefold:

   1. To test whether LLMs can reduce the workload of researchers by automating
          repetitive tasks.

                                                                                              51
   2. To evaluate the accuracy and reliability of LLM-assisted screening and
       summarization(Anderson, 2000).

   3. To explore the integration of human–AI collaboration in academic workflows.

The assistant was designed as a semi-automated pipeline: researchers provided queries and
controlled inclusion/exclusion criteria, while the GPT model supported tasks such as record
classification, summarization, and causal terminology extraction.

5.1.1 Core functionalities

The prototype included the following features:

   •   Automated screening: The model classified records into “relevant,” “possibly
       relevant,” and “irrelevant” categories.

   •   Summarization of abstracts: GPT generated structured summaries highlighting
       causal terms, datasets, and methodologies(Ouyang et al., 2022).

   •   Causal    terminology     extraction:     The   system   flagged   keywords   such   as
       “counterfactual,” “DAG,” “intervention,” and “structural model.”

   •   Evidence mapping: Extracted terms and summaries were indexed into Zotero for
       integration with existing reference management systems.

5.1.2 Technical implementation

The assistant was implemented using the OpenAI GPT-4 API integrated with Python scripts.
For reproducibility, the codebase was structured in modular form:

   •   A query module ingested metadata exported from Scopus, PubMed, or arXiv.


                                                                                            52
   •   A classification module applied zero-shot and few-shot prompting for relevance
       screening.

   •   A summarization module transformed abstracts into structured notes.

   •   A logging module recorded all outputs for transparency(Bai et al., 2022).

The assistant was not intended to replace human reviewers, but to function as a co-pilot in the
systematic review process.

5.2 Prompting, Workflow, Implemented Functions

The central innovation of the prototype was the prompting strategy. While previous studies
used general-purpose summarization, this assistant employed carefully engineered prompts
tailored to causal inference.

5.2.1 Prompt engineering

Three categories of prompts were tested:

   •   Screening prompts: Designed to assess whether a paper included causal inference
       elements(Li et al., 2024).

   •   Summarization prompts: Structured outputs into “Background,” “Methodology,”
       and “Causal Elements.”

   •   Extraction prompts: Targeted specific keywords or phrases related to causal
       modeling.

5.2.2 Workflow stages

The implemented workflow followed six sequential steps:

   1. Import data from databases into the prototype.

   2. Automated classification by GPT.

   3. Human review of uncertain cases.

   4. Summarization of abstracts.

   5. Extraction of causal terms.

   6. Integration with Zotero and Overleaf.

5.4 Validation
Validation   involved   testing   the   prototype   for   robustness,   generalizability,   and
reproducibility.

5.4.1 Robustness

Robustness testing was conducted by altering prompt phrasing. Accuracy remained stable
across variations, though more complex prompts tended to improve precision at the expense
of recall.


                                                                                             57
5.4.2 Generalizability

Generalizability refers to the ability of a system or methodology to perform well beyond the
conditions under which it was originally developed. To evaluate this dimension, the GPT-
based assistant was tested on datasets that lay outside the original query scope, including
literature on causal inference in climate science and educational policy(Kiciman et al., 2023).
These domains differ significantly from the biomedical and computer science corpora that
dominated the training and evaluation phases of the prototype.

The results indicated a modest decline in performance, with accuracy dropping from 85% in
the original domain to around 78% in new contexts. The reduction was most evident in cases
where domain-specific terminology overlapped imperfectly with general causal language(Li
et al., 2024). For example, terms like “radiative forcing” in climate studies or “learning
outcomes” in education required contextual interpretation that the assistant did not always
capture correctly.

This finding suggests that while the assistant can adapt to unfamiliar domains, its
performance depends heavily on prompt design and domain knowledge embedded in its
training data(Ouyang et al., 2022). Domain-specific prompt tuning and supplementary
retrieval-augmented techniques could mitigate these limitations, enabling the assistant to
achieve more consistent accuracy across disciplines.

5.4.3 Reproducibility

Reproducibility is a cornerstone of scientific research, ensuring that results can be verified
and extended by others. In this thesis, reproducibility was addressed by sharing open-source
datasets, scripts, and prompt templates through a public GitHub repository(Liu et al.,
2025). This approach allowed external researchers to access the same workflow and replicate
the AI-assisted systematic review process with minimal setup.

Independent trials were conducted by three separate groups working with different domains,
including biomedical sciences, social sciences, and climate research. While there were minor
variations in outputs, particularly in borderline cases where causal terminology was
ambiguous, the overall results were consistent(Nordling, 2025). Accuracy levels across trials
averaged 81%, with most discrepancies arising from differences in domain-specific language
rather than failures of the system itself.

A key factor in maintaining reproducibility was the transparent documentation of prompts
and decision logs. By recording every query, classification decision, and summarization
output, the workflow enabled reviewers to trace the reasoning behind results. This level of
detail made replication easier and increased confidence in findings(Jan, 2025).

These outcomes suggest that reproducibility in LLM-assisted workflows is achievable when
supported by open science practices, including code release, dataset availability, and
comprehensive documentation.

Chapter 6: Conclusions and Future Directions



   6.1 Summary of Findings

This thesis explored the intersection of large language models (LLMs) and causal
inference, addressing both theoretical and practical dimensions. The systematic review
encompassed 80 carefully selected studies spanning biomedical science, social science,
computer science, and applied AI. The practical development involved designing and
validating a GPT-based assistant prototype for systematic reviews in causal
inference(Vaswani et al., 2017).

The findings reveal several overarching themes. First, there has been a rapid surge of
interest in applying LLMs to causal reasoning tasks, especially after 2020. Second, although
proprietary models like GPT dominate the landscape, there is a noticeable uptake of open-
access models such as LLaMA, reflecting the research community’s concern for
reproducibility(Wu et al., 2024). Third, the majority of existing studies fall into two
categories: causal reasoning (answering “what if” and “why” questions) and fairness/bias
evaluation of LLMs. Meanwhile, fewer contributions tackle causal discovery or automated
code generation.

From a methodological perspective, the systematic review demonstrated that while LLMs
hold significant promise as tools to support causal inference, they also struggle with
formal rigor(Zhou et al., 2024a). Their outputs often approximate human reasoning rather
than strictly following the mathematical formalisms of structural causal models or directed
acyclic graphs.



                                                                                         61
The prototype evaluation provided a complementary perspective. By testing the GPT
assistant, it became evident that AI can significantly reduce time in tasks such as abstract
screening and summarization. Human reviewers consistently reported efficiency gains,
although concerns persisted regarding hallucination, reproducibility, and accuracy in
borderline cases. This duality—high efficiency but uneven rigor—frames the central
conclusion of this thesis: LLMs are valuable support tools in causal inference, but not
substitutes for human expertise or formal causal methodology.

   6.2 Value of LLMs as Support Tools for Causal Inference

The review and prototype demonstrate that LLMs can serve as supportive, not standalone,
tools in causal inference research. Their strengths include:

   1. Efficiency gains: Screening, summarization, and information extraction can be
        automated to reduce researcher workload.

   2. Accessibility: LLMs can democratize access to causal inference concepts by
        providing natural-language explanations(Yang et al., 2022).

   3. Knowledge integration: Their training on vast corpora enables recognition of causal
        terminology across disciplines.

   4. Rapid prototyping: As shown in the assistant prototype, LLMs enable fast design of
        systematic review pipelines.

                                                                                           62
Yet these values must be contextualized. Without human oversight, LLMs risk misclassifying
articles, fabricating causal links, or producing results that appear rigorous but lack
methodological validity. The interpretability gap remains central: LLMs do not inherently
“understand” causality but approximate it through statistical correlations(Chen et al., 2024).

The value of LLMs, therefore, lies in augmenting human expertise—acting as accelerators
of research processes rather than autonomous analysts.

   6.3 Limitations of the Study

Every academic investigation must acknowledge its boundaries, and this thesis is no
exception. Despite the careful design of the review and prototype development, several
limitations should be recognized when interpreting the findings.

   6.3.1       Scope of the systematic review

The systematic review included 80 studies selected through rigorous PRISMA protocols.
However, the pace of publication in artificial intelligence and causal inference is
exceptionally rapid, with new preprints and peer-reviewed work emerging weekly. As a result,
the dataset used here may already be outdated or incomplete. This limitation reduces the
generalizability of the findings and underscores the need for “living systematic reviews” that
are updated continuously rather than once-off

   6.3.2       Model dependency

The practical component relied exclusively on GPT-4. While this choice was justified by
performance and availability, it introduces a model-dependency bias. Results might differ
substantially with other frontier models such as Claude, LLaMA, or PaLM(Zhu et al., 2023).
Comparative testing across multiple model families would have strengthened the conclusions,
but resource constraints limited this possibility.

   6.3.3       Evaluation design

The evaluation compared manual and AI-assisted workflows on a sample of 100 records.
This   provided    a   useful   proof    of   concept   but   does   not   guarantee   statistical
robustness(Rethlefsen et al., 2021). Larger trials with more diverse datasets would provide
stronger evidence for efficiency gains, accuracy, and generalizability across domains.

                                                                                                 63
   6.3.4       Researcher bias

Although PRISMA guidelines were followed to maximize transparency, human judgment
inevitably influenced screening decisions and qualitative coding. Reviewer bias may have
shaped inclusion/exclusion outcomes or interpretation of borderline cases. Dual screening and
greater automation could help reduce this risk in future studies(Patel et al., 2024).

Together, these limitations highlight the need for broader validation, model diversity, and
ongoing replication to build stronger evidence for the role of LLMs in causal inference
research.

   6.4 Possible Next Steps

The thesis not only synthesizes existing research but also lays the groundwork for further
exploration. Several next steps emerge.

   6.4.1       Technical directions

   •   Benchmark development: A unified benchmark for causal reasoning in LLMs would
       allow more consistent evaluation.

   •   Hybrid models: Combining symbolic causal inference methods (e.g., structural causal
       models) with LLM capabilities could enhance rigor.

   •   Prompt optimization: Domain-specific prompt engineering and retrieval-augmented
       generation may reduce hallucinations(Takagi, 2023).

   •   Evaluation metrics: Beyond accuracy, metrics such as robustness, transparency, and
       interpretability should be prioritized.



                                                                                             64
   6.4.2         Applied directions

   •   Healthcare applications: Using LLMs for causal extraction in medical literature has
       the potential to accelerate discovery but requires strict validation.

   •   Policy evaluation: Simulating counterfactuals for policy interventions remains
       underexplored but could be impactful.

   •   Education and training: LLMs could serve as teaching assistants in causal inference
       courses, bridging accessibility gaps.

   6.4.3         Ethical and governance considerations

   •   Bias auditing: Ensuring that LLM-assisted causal inference does not amplify societal
       biases.

   •   Transparency: Mandating disclosure of model prompts, datasets, and evaluation
       strategies in published work(Kiciman et al., 2023).

   •   Responsible deployment: Clarifying the role of human oversight in workflows where
       decisions have real-world implications.

   6.5 Broader Implications

The results of this thesis resonate beyond causal inference. They illustrate how AI-human
collaboration can redefine knowledge discovery. Just as systematic reviews once relied
exclusively on manual labor, the integration of LLMs signals a future where researchers
supervise AI collaborators(Chen et al., 2024).

This paradigm shift aligns with the wider transformation in science:


                                                                                        65
   •   From data-intensive research to AI-augmented reasoning.

   •   From individual expertise to collective intelligence mediated by technology.

   •   From static reviews to living, continuously updated knowledge systems.

The broader implication is that the epistemic role of AI in science is changing—from being a
tool for computation to being a participant in reasoning. This raises fundamental questions
about authorship, accountability, and trust in research outputs.

   6.6 Concluding Remarks

This thesis set out to explore a deceptively simple question: Can large language models
reason causally or assist those who do? The answer, based on systematic review and
practical development, is nuanced:

   •   LLMs cannot yet reason causally in a formal sense. They lack the mathematical
       grounding of structural models and counterfactual frameworks.

   •   However, LLMs can meaningfully assist human researchers by accelerating
       screening, summarization, and information extraction(Chen et al., 2024).

   •   Their value lies not in replacing causal inference but in supporting and amplifying
       human expertise.

Future progress will depend on integrating LLMs into hybrid workflows where human
judgment, causal formalism, and AI efficiency coexist. As benchmarks evolve, open-source
models mature, and ethical frameworks solidify, the role of LLMs in causal inference may
transition from assistant to collaborator.

In conclusion, this thesis contributes to an emerging field by providing both a state-of-the-art
synthesis and a practical demonstration of how LLMs can support causal inference(Patel et
al., 2024). It closes with both optimism and caution: optimism about the transformative
potential of AI-assisted science, and caution about the need for rigor, transparency, and ethics.

Appendix B


1. Tools used

The methodology combined traditional academic tools with AI-assisted systems.

2. Zotero

Used as the reference management system to collect and organize citations, attach PDFs,
and export in APA format.

3. Overleaf

Used for collaborative drafting and formatting of parts of the thesis, ensuring consistency with
academic templates.

4. ASReview

An AI-powered system that implements active learning for systematic reviews. It helps
prioritize the most relevant studies for human screening, reducing workload while
maintaining quality(Tsafnat et al., 2014).

5. GPT-4

Used as an assistant for:

   •   Screening titles/abstracts for potential relevance.

   •   Summarizing methodological details of papers.

   •   Suggesting causal categories (discovery, reasoning, code generation).
